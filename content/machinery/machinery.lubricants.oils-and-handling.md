# Lubricants: oils, grease and handling

An engine with no oil does not run for long. Lubricating oil is the cheapest part of the machinery and the one whose
neglect costs the most. The SVMO syllabus asks you to know what lubricants do, how they are ==chosen, checked, stored
and disposed of==, and what a bad sample tells you. This lesson covers all of that in plain terms.

## What a lubricant does

Two metal surfaces that slide on each other wear out quickly. A film of oil keeps them apart, which is the main job,
but a good lubricant does five things at once:

- ==Reduces friction and wear== by keeping a film between moving parts.
- ==Cools== by carrying heat away from bearings and pistons to the sump and cooler.
- ==Cleans== by carrying soot and tiny particles to the filter, with detergent additives holding dirt in suspension.
- ==Seals== the gap between piston rings and cylinder walls.
- ==Protects against corrosion== on internal surfaces.

Grease is oil held in a soap-like thickener so it stays where you put it. It is used on bearings, steering
pivots, winch gear and anywhere oil would run away.

## Viscosity and grades

==Viscosity is resistance to flow==. Honey has a high viscosity, water has a low one. Oil gets thinner as it warms and
thicker as it cools, and that is the problem to solve. Oil that is too thin when hot leaves a weak film and lets metal
touch metal. Oil that is too thick when cold will not reach the bearings quickly at start-up, which is when most wear
happens.

A ==multigrade== oil is made to behave like a thin oil when cold and a thicker oil when hot. A grade such as
==15W-40== has two numbers. The first, with the W for winter, tells you how it flows when cold (a lower number flows
more easily in the cold). The second tells you how thick it stays at running temperature. Engines in coastal BC rarely
see extreme cold, but a boat left in a yard in January still starts with cold oil.

Always use the ==grade and specification the maker states==. The specification (an API or similar rating) tells you
the additive package, which matters as much as the thickness.

## Oils are not interchangeable

Engine oil, hydraulic oil, gear oil and some gearbox fluids have ==different additives and different properties==.
Engine oil has detergents to hold soot. Hydraulic oil has anti-wear and anti-foam additives. Some marine gearboxes
use automatic transmission fluid, others use gear oil, and the maker decides which. Putting the wrong one into a
system can damage clutches, seals and pumps. Do not mix brands or types casually, and if you must top up in an
emergency, use the nearest suitable oil, record it, and change it properly at the first chance.

## Checking the level

1. Stop the engine and let the oil drain back to the sump, which takes a few minutes. The vessel should be upright.
2. Pull the dipstick, wipe it, push it fully home, pull it again and read it.
3. The level should sit ==between the minimum and maximum marks==. Top up in small amounts and re-check.

Too little oil starves the bearings. ==Too much oil is also harmful==: the spinning crankshaft whips the oil into foam,
the foam carries air that does not lubricate, and pressure and cooling drop. Overfilling also pushes oil past seals and
into the breather. Check gearbox and hydraulic reservoirs the same way, using their own dipsticks or sight glasses.

## Reading the oil

Look at the oil on the dipstick and rub a drop between finger and thumb (with a glove or a rag).

| What you see | What it usually means |
|---|---|
| ==Milky or creamy, light brown== | Water or coolant in the oil. Suspect a head gasket, cooler or leaking seal. |
| Thin, smells of fuel, level rising | Fuel dilution, for example a leaking injector or lift pump diaphragm. |
| Very black soon after a change | Normal in a diesel. Soot is being held in suspension. Judge by hours and condition, not colour alone. |
| Glittery or gritty | Metal or dirt. Wear in a bearing or gear, or a filter failure. |
| Burnt smell | Overheating. |

Oil analysis is a lab service that tests a small sample for metals, fuel, water and soot. It lets you catch wear early
on larger engines.

## Oil pressure and filters

Every pressure-lubricated engine has an oil pump, a relief valve, a filter and a pressure gauge or alarm. A ==low oil
pressure alarm means stop the engine==. Do not carry on while you look for the cause, because bearings can be ruined in
a minute or two. Common causes are low level, thin or diluted oil, a clogged pickup, or a failing pump.

Change the oil and filter at the maker's interval, or sooner if the engine runs in harsh conditions. A new filter
should be filled and the oil level checked again after the first run. Never reuse a filter gasket, and never run an
engine with the filter loose.

## Storing, handling and disposal

- Keep oil in closed, labelled containers in a locker that can catch a spill. Keep it clean, because dirt in new oil
  is dirt in the engine.
- Wipe up drips at once. Oil on a deck or in a bilge is a slip hazard and a fire hazard.
- Store oily rags in a ==closed metal container==, not on the engine or in a pocket. They can heat and ignite.
- Wear gloves. Used oil is a skin irritant and can carry harmful combustion by-products.
- ==Used oil goes into a sealed container for an approved disposal or recycling facility==. Never put it over the side,
  into the bilge, or into a stove. Oil in bilge water that is pumped overboard is a pollution offence, and a sheen on
  the water must be reported. Many BC marinas have a used-oil depot.
- Keep a spill kit at hand, and drip trays under filters and drain points when you change oil.

## A BC engine-room example

You are the engineer on a 15 m passenger vessel running out of Campbell River. On a pre-start round you find the
starboard engine dipstick shows a pale, milky smear and the level is up by two centimetres since yesterday. That is
cooling water, probably from an oil cooler or head gasket, getting into the sump. You do not start that engine, you
tell the master, and you leave the oil in place as evidence for the repairer. Running it would wash the film off the
bearings and could wreck it.

## Common mistakes

- Judging oil only by its colour.
- Filling past the maximum mark, "to be safe".
- Topping up a hydraulic system with engine oil because it was handy.
- Checking the level straight after shutdown, before the oil has drained back.
- Putting used oil in the bilge or over the side.
- Ignoring a low oil pressure alarm and "just getting in".
