# Steering gear: types, checks and emergency steering

If the engine fails the vessel can anchor. If the steering fails at speed she can hit something in seconds. For that
reason the steering gear is checked before every departure, and engineers must know how it works and how to rig the
back-up. The SVMO syllabus asks you to ==know the types, the checks, the faults and the emergency arrangements==.

## What steering gear does

When the helmsman turns the wheel, the steering gear turns the ==rudder== (or on other vessels turns an outboard,
sterndrive, azimuth drive or steerable nozzle) so that the water flow pushes the stern sideways and the vessel turns.
The helmsman needs to know the angle, so a ==rudder angle indicator== shows the rudder position at the helm.

Key parts on a conventional rudder installation: the ==rudder blade==, the ==rudder stock== (a vertical shaft going up
into the hull through a trunk with a gland), a ==tiller or quadrant== fixed to the top of the stock, and the means of
moving the tiller or quadrant. ==Rudder stops== limit travel, normally to a little over 30 degrees each side.

## Types of steering

**Manual (mechanical).** Wire, cable, chain or rod and quadrant, or a direct tiller. Simple, with few parts to fail but
heavy to use on anything beyond a small boat. Wire and chain stretch, so check tension and pulleys.

**Hydraulic.** The most common arrangement on small commercial vessels. Turning the wheel works a small ==helm pump==. It
pushes oil through pipes or hoses to a ==steering cylinder (ram)== which moves the tiller or the engine drive. Oil
returns to a reservoir. Because oil does not compress, the response is immediate and firm, and the helm can be mounted
a long way from the rudder.

**Power-assisted or electro-hydraulic.** On larger vessels, a power pump (electric or engine-driven) supplies the oil
and the helm only operates a control valve or electrical signal. These often have two power units, one main and one
standby. They can have a follow-up system that stops the rudder at the angle requested.

**Others.** Outboard, sterndrive and jet steering move the drive or the nozzle by cable or hydraulics. Twin-screw
vessels can steer partly with the engines.

## Hydraulic steering faults

A hydraulic steering system is a closed oil circuit, so the faults are those of any hydraulic system (see the hydraulics
lesson) with the added risk that the effect is on the helm.

| Symptom | Likely cause |
|---|---|
| ==Spongy, jerky or lagging helm== | Air in the oil. Bleed the system and find the leak or low level that let air in. |
| Wheel turns but rudder does not follow, or drifts | Leak, low fluid, worn pump or valve, damaged seal |
| Hard to turn | Low fluid, stiff bearings, seized rudder stock or linkage, obstruction |
| Oil on the floor | Leaking hose, fitting or ram seal. Find and fix it, and clean it up |
| Noise or heat | Low oil, air, blocked line, failing pump. Some warmth is normal, but fast heating is not |
| Rudder angle indicator not matching | Slipped or damaged sender or linkage, loose mounting |

Fill the reservoir only with the oil named on the unit. Do not top up from a mixed bottle, and do not mix types.

## Pre-departure checks

Before every departure, and after any work on the steering, the engineer and the helmsman test together:

1. ==Turn the helm from midships to full lock one way, then back through midships to full lock the other way.== Watch
   the rudder (or have someone watch it) to see it move smoothly and the full distance.
2. ==Compare the rudder angle indicator== with the real rudder position.
3. Listen for noise, watch for jerks and look for leaks at the pump, ram and fittings.
4. Check the reservoir level and the condition of the oil.
5. Check the linkages, pins and cotter pins, and the rudder stock gland, for wear or leaks.
6. Where there are two power units, test each, and check that the changeover works.
7. Check that the ==emergency steering== is in place, with the tools and a means of talking to the bridge.

Report any defect to the master before sailing, and record the check in the log.

## Emergency steering

If the main steering fails, the vessel must still be steerable. The back-up depends on the vessel. It may be an
==emergency tiller== that fits onto the rudder stock or quadrant, a ==hand pump== that works the steering ram, a second
power unit, or steering by the engines on a twin-screw vessel. The rules for what a particular vessel must carry depend
on its size and service, so check the requirements that apply to your own vessel.

For the engineer:

- Know ==where the equipment is, how to rig it and how long it takes==. Practise in port, in daylight, with the
  tools in the locker and a warm engine room. A tiller you have never fitted is hard to fit in a seaway.
- Keep the area clear and the emergency gear free of paint, rust and stowed gear.
- Have a communications link between the helm and the steering position (sound-powered phone, hand-held radio or
  voice pipe), with clear orders for the helm.
- A person at the rudder needs clear orders, and the master or mate on the bridge needs clear answers. Repeat
  back the orders.

## When steering fails

1. ==Tell the bridge at once.== A failed steering gear is an emergency.
2. The master decides how to respond. It usually means slowing, warning other vessels, and perhaps stopping or
   anchoring.
3. Go to the steering position, check for the obvious causes (oil level, a tripped breaker, a closed valve) and rig the
   emergency steering if needed.
4. Do not fiddle with the controls while the vessel is turning, unless you have to. Keep the bridge informed.
5. When working, record what failed, and have it repaired before sailing.

## Maintenance

Grease the pins and bearings at the points the maker lists. Keep the rudder stock gland adjusted so it holds water out
without binding. Inspect chains and cables. Look at the rudder and the carrier bearings when the vessel is lifted out.
Never leave a hydraulic hose with a chafe mark. A rudder that is stiff, or one that has play, is telling you something.

## A BC engine-room example

You are the engineer on a 22 m crew boat out of Port Hardy, leaving at dawn for a night crossing. In the harbour you
turn the wheel hard over each way. The rudder follows, but the indicator reads two degrees low on one side. You find the
linkage to the indicator sender has loosened, and you tighten and recheck it. You also notice the steering reservoir is a
little low, find a damp fitting on the port side of the ram, and tighten it before topping up with the correct oil. The
emergency tiller is in its locker, the bridge phone works, and you rig the tiller once to prove it fits.

## Common mistakes

- Skipping the steering test because "it worked yesterday".
- Checking only one direction.
- Topping up with whatever oil is nearby.
- Never having rigged the emergency steering.
- Ignoring air in the system and carrying on.
- Telling the bridge only after you have tried to fix it.
