# Underwater fittings: sea chests, seacocks, anodes and propulsors

Anything that passes through the hull below the waterline is a possible hole. The SVMO syllabus asks you to ==know the
common underwater fittings, what each is for, how it is kept working, and what to do if one fails==. They are also the
items you inspect when the vessel is hauled out of the water.

## Hull openings

### Sea chest and seacock

Cooling water, fire pump water and ballast all come from outside the hull. On many vessels the water comes through a
==sea chest==, a box or recess built into the hull with a grating on the outside so large objects cannot enter. A pipe
leads from the chest to the pumps. Some sea chests have steam, compressed air or a fresh-water connection to blow
debris clear.

On smaller boats a single pipe, a ==through-hull fitting==, passes through the hull with a ==seacock== bolted on the inside.
The seacock is a valve (often a ball or a tapered plug type) that you can close to shut the opening. Rules of thumb:

- Every hull opening below the waterline should have a seacock, and the valve must be easy to reach and ==work by hand==.
- ==Exercise them regularly==. A seacock that has not moved for a year can seize open just when you want it closed.
- Fit hose clamps in pairs (stainless steel) on every hose connected to a seacock, and check the hose for cracks, bulges
  and hardness. A hose is a weak point, and an old one can collapse inside, blocking the cooling water.
- Mark each valve with what it serves, and keep the handle where it can be found.

### Sea strainer

Between the seacock and the raw-water pump sits a ==sea strainer==, which has a basket or element that catches weed,
plastic, jellyfish and sand. In BC waters kelp and eelgrass in summer and jellyfish blooms are common causes of blocked
strainers. A blocked strainer starves the pump and the engine runs hot, so clean it as part of every daily round.

To clean a strainer that is on a below-waterline line:

1. ==Close the seacock==.
2. Open the strainer lid slowly. Expect some water to spill.
3. Clean the basket and the sealing face, and check the gasket.
4. Close the lid, ==open the seacock and check for leaks==, and check the engine is getting cooling water.

### Discharges

Water leaves through overboard discharges. These openings are low in the hull on some vessels, and a non-return valve
or a high loop stops sea water from coming back in. The same care applies: a seacock, double clamps and regular
checks. Engines with exhausts that discharge near the waterline may also have anti-siphon fittings.

## Sacrificial anodes

When two different metals sit in sea water, which is a conductor, one of them corrodes faster. This is ==galvanic
corrosion==. A bronze propeller on a steel shaft in a steel or aluminium hull is a battery. To protect the important
metal, the vessel carries a block of a more reactive metal, a ==sacrificial anode==, which corrodes first and leaves the
rest alone. Anodes are usually zinc, aluminium or magnesium, depending on the vessel's material and whether she
operates in salt or fresh water.

- Anodes are bolted to the hull, rudder, shaft and sometimes inside coolers or heat exchangers.
- Check them at every haul-out. ==Replace one that is mostly wasted.== A new anode is bright and heavy, and a worn one
  is pitted and small.
- ==Never paint an anode== and never fit one where the surface is dirty or coated, because the electrical contact
  must be good.
- Stray current from shore power or faulty wiring can eat metal quickly. Unusual corrosion points to an electrical fault.
- Use the metal that suits the vessel and water type, as the wrong one gives poor protection. If unsure, ask the builder.

## Propulsors

**Fixed-pitch propeller.** ==The blades are cast at a set angle== that cannot be changed in service. Direction is
changed at the gearbox. It is simple, strong and by far the most common.

**Controllable-pitch propeller (CPP).** The blades swivel on the hub, worked by hydraulics through the shaft. The pitch is
set from the bridge, giving ahead, neutral and astern while the engine runs at constant speed. It is more complex and
costly, but flexible for vessels such as tugs and ferries.

**Nozzle.** A ==Kort nozzle== is a ring around the propeller. It directs and accelerates the water, giving more thrust at
low speeds. That is why tugs and trawlers use it. Steerable nozzles also steer the vessel.

**Bow and stern thrusters.** A tunnel through the hull with a propeller inside, pushing the bow or stern sideways. It
has its own motor (hydraulic or electric), and its tunnel is another opening to inspect.

**Keel cooler.** A set of pipes or a box welded or bolted to the outside of the hull. Hot engine coolant flows through them
and the sea water outside cools them. ==No raw-water intake is needed==, so there is nothing to block with weed or
jellyfish and less salt corrosion inside the engine. They are common on tugs and workboats. They can be damaged by
grounding, so look at them when the vessel is hauled out.

**Rudders and skegs.** The rudder turns on a stock, which passes through a gland in the hull. A skeg is a fixed fin that
supports and protects the propeller and rudder.

**Transducers.** The echo sounder and speed log have transducers through the hull. Do not paint them without advice.

## At haul-out

Walk round the vessel with a torch and a pad:

- Inspect through-hull fittings and seacocks for corrosion, and operate each valve.
- Check anodes, propeller, shaft, rudder and stern gland.
- Check for marine growth, damaged paint or bent blades.
- Look at the keel cooler, thruster tunnels and the transducers.
- Check for blisters, cracks or dents in the hull.

## If a fitting fails

1. ==Shut the seacock== if it is within reach and works.
2. If not, drive a tapered ==softwood bung== into the opening (these are kept near the seacock, with a mallet) or fit a
   damage-control plug.
3. Start bilge pumps and tell the ==master== at once.
4. Keep watching the bilge level after the repair.

## A BC engine-room example

You are the engineer on a 17 m seiner at a Steveston dock. The port engine temperature climbs steadily and the alarm
sounds. The pump is turning, so you check the sea strainer. You close the seacock, open the strainer and find the basket
choked with eelgrass. You clean it, close it, open the seacock, check for leaks and the temperature falls. Later you
notice the zinc anode on the shaft is half gone, and put a new one on the work list for the next haul-out.

## Common mistakes

- Opening a strainer before closing the seacock.
- Never operating the seacocks, so they seize.
- Painting an anode.
- Replacing a hose without double clamping.
- Forgetting to open the seacock after cleaning, then running dry.
- Confusing a sea chest (the intake box) with a sea strainer (the filter).
