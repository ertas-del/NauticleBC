# Fuels: storage, sounding, transfer and contamination

Fuel problems stop more small-vessel engines than almost anything else. The SVMO syllabus asks you to know the fuels
used on ships and how they are ==stored, transferred, heated, filtered and purified==. This lesson covers the practical
side: keep it safe, keep it clean, and know how much you have.

## Diesel and gasoline

Diesel is the normal fuel on commercial vessels because of its ==high flash point==. The flash point is the lowest
temperature at which a fuel gives off enough vapour to flash when a flame is present. Gasoline flashes well below
freezing, so its vapour is dangerous in a closed engine space. Diesel does not form explosive vapour at normal
temperatures, which makes it much safer to carry.

**If you must store gasoline** (for example for an outboard or a portable pump):

- Keep it in approved, labelled containers or tanks.
- Store it where vapour cannot collect in a low or closed space, and away from heat, sparks and the engine room.
- For an inboard gasoline engine, ==run the bilge blower for at least four minutes before starting== to clear vapour
  from the bilge, and check the bilge by smell before starting.
- No smoking, no naked flames, and no electrical sparks near it.

## Sounding a tank

A ==sounding pipe== runs from the deck (or machinery space) to the bottom of the tank. You lower a sounding rod or
tape down it and read the fuel height, then convert it to a volume with the tank's ==calibration table==.

- Pipes that end in a machinery space have a ==self-closing cock== or screw cap, and a ==striker plate== at the bottom
  so the rod does not hole the tank. Keep the cap or cock closed after use, because an open pipe is a fuel and fire
  hazard.
- ==List and trim== change the depth at the sounding point, so the reading is only exact when the vessel is upright
  and level, or when you correct it using the tables.
- A smear of ==water-finding paste== on the end of the rod shows how much water sits under the fuel.

## Working out how much fuel you have

Fuel volume is in litres, and mass is volume times specific gravity. One cubic metre of diesel (1 000 litres) weighs
about 850 kg. **Endurance** is the number of hours you can run at the current burn rate:

> endurance (hours) = fuel on board (litres) divided by consumption (litres per hour)

Example: 800 litres at 20 litres per hour is ==40 hours==. Always keep a reserve and plan on less than the total.

## Transferring fuel between tanks

Before you start:

1. Agree the plan with the master, and tell the bridge what you are doing.
2. Check how much room there is in the receiving tank. Do not rely on the gauge alone.
3. Set the valves (the right tank, the right pump) and check there are no unintended cross connections.
4. Make sure the tank vents are clear, and put drip trays and spill gear in place.

While transferring: watch the level, stop well before the tank is full, and stay at the valves. When done, close the
valves and record the transfer. The same care applies when ==bunkering== from a fuel dock.

## Day tank, filters and purifier

- A ==day tank== is a small tank close to the engine that supplies fuel by gravity. It lets water and dirt settle out
  and keeps the engine fed while you work on the main supply.
- A ==primary filter== (often a water separator) and a ==secondary filter== catch dirt and water before the injection
  pump. Fine injector nozzles are easily damaged.
- Many filters have a pressure gauge or sight glass. High pressure drop across a filter means it is clogged. Duplex
  filters let you switch to a clean one without stopping the engine.
- A ==purifier== (centrifuge) spins out water and solids from fuel on larger vessels. Small boats rely on filters and
  settling.

## Water and fuel bugs

Water enters fuel through tank vents (condensation), bunkering and leaking deck fills. Because diesel is lighter than
water (specific gravity about 0.85), the water ==settles to the bottom==. Drain a little from the bottom of the tank
or filter bowl regularly and check it for water.

==Fuel bugs== are bacteria and fungi that live at the fuel and water boundary. They form a dark sludge that clogs
filters and can corrode tanks. Warning signs are repeated filter blockages, slime on the water-finding paste, and a
sour smell. Keep tanks as full as possible to cut condensation, drain water often, and follow the maker's advice on
treatments. A contaminated tank may need cleaning.

## Handling fuel safely

Avoid skin contact and wear gloves. Fuel-soaked rags are a fire hazard: store them in a closed metal container, not in
the open. Clean any spill straight away and keep it out of the water. Report a spill into the water to the Coast
Guard.

## A BC engine-room example

You take over a 12 m aluminium crew boat that has been sitting at a marina in Nanaimo for two weeks. Before sailing you
drain a cupful from the bottom of the water separator and it comes out cloudy with a layer of water. You drain until
it runs clean, check the filter, and record it. If you had ignored it, water could have reached the injectors.

## Common mistakes

- Believing water floats on diesel. Water sinks, so drain from the lowest point.
- Leaving a sounding cap open after use.
- Trusting a gauge on a tilted vessel.
- Topping up a tank right to the brim.
- Forgetting a reserve when working out endurance.
