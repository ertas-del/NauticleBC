# Safe working practices: WHMIS, PPE, lockout, hot work and confined spaces

An engine room is noisy, hot, tight and full of moving parts and hazardous products. Most injuries come from a few
causes: machinery that starts unexpectedly, bad air in an enclosed space, burns, electric shock and fire during hot
work. The syllabus asks you to ==work safely and know the precautions before entering enclosed spaces==. This lesson is
the checklist.

## Hazardous products and WHMIS

WHMIS is Canada's ==Workplace Hazardous Materials Information System==. Its idea is simple: every hazardous product must
arrive with information on how to use it safely.

- A ==label== shows the product name, the hazard symbols and short precautions. Never decant a product into an unmarked
  container.
- A ==safety data sheet (SDS)== gives the full detail: hazards, protective equipment, first aid, fire, spill and storage
  advice. Keep the SDS binder where crew can reach it.
- Training: you should be taught what the hazard symbols mean before you handle the product.

Typical engine-room hazardous products are fuel, oils, coolant (antifreeze), battery acid, solvents, paints and
refrigerants.

## Your rights and duties

Under occupational health and safety law in Canada, a worker has three basic rights: the ==right to know== about hazards,
the ==right to participate== in health and safety (for example through a committee or by reporting), and the ==right to
refuse unsafe work==. You also have the duty to work safely, use protective equipment and report hazards. Check the
exact wording that applies on your vessel with the master or the company.

## Personal protective equipment (PPE)

PPE is the last line of defence. It does not replace removing the hazard, but you wear it as well.

| Hazard | PPE |
|---|---|
| Noise from engines | ==Hearing protection== (earmuffs or plugs) |
| Flying chips, grinding, splashes | ==Safety glasses or face shield== |
| Fuels, oils, coolant, acid | ==Chemical-resistant gloves==, overalls, eye protection |
| Dropped objects, tripping | ==Safety boots==, hard hat where overhead work |
| Dust, fumes, paint spray | Suitable respirator |
| Rotating shafts and belts | Close-fitting clothing. No loose sleeves, jewellery or dangling lanyards. Tie back long hair. |

## Lockout and tag-out

Many injuries happen when a machine that was "off" starts. Before working on any machine that could move, start or
release stored energy:

1. ==Tell== the master or bridge and anyone affected.
2. ==Isolate== the source: stop the machine, then open the breaker, shut valves, remove the starter key.
3. ==Lock== the isolator off and ==tag== it with your name and the reason. Keep the key.
4. Release stored energy: bleed hydraulic or air pressure, let hot parts cool, block or secure anything that could fall.
5. ==Test== that it cannot start (try the start button) before putting your hands in.
6. When done, replace guards, remove your lock and tag, and tell everyone affected before restoring power.

Only the person who placed a lock removes it. Never rely on a stop button alone.

## Rotating machinery, tools and handling

- Keep guards on shafts, belts and couplings. Do not reach over turning shafts.
- Use the right tool for the job and keep it in good order. A mushroomed chisel or a damaged cable is a hazard.
- Lift with your legs, not your back. Use a hoist or get help for heavy parts.
- Keep decks and ladders clear of oil and rags, and wipe up spills straight away.
- Hot surfaces: exhaust pipes, turbochargers, oil and coolant are scalding. Let the engine cool before opening a
  radiator cap, and open it slowly.

## Electrical safety

Treat all electrical equipment as ==live until isolated and proved dead==. Use insulated tools. Do not work on a live
board alone. Never touch electrical gear with wet hands or standing in a wet bilge. A damaged cable or plug must be
repaired or replaced, not taped. After an electric shock, always get checked, because the effect on the heart can be
delayed.

## Hot work

Welding, cutting, grinding and brazing throw sparks and heat. Before starting:

- Clear or cover ==combustibles== and check the other side of the bulkhead and below you, because heat travels through
  steel.
- Make sure fuel lines and tanks nearby are isolated, empty or gas-free.
- Have an ==extinguisher== ready and a ==fire watch== (a second person) who stays for a time afterwards.
- Ventilate, and wear the correct eye protection and gloves.
- Get permission from the master where your company requires a hot work permit.

Never weld or cut a tank that has held fuel until it is certified gas-free by a competent person.

## Confined and enclosed spaces

A tank, void, cofferdam or bilge can look harmless and yet kill in minutes. The air may be short of oxygen (rust uses it
up), or contain toxic gas or flammable vapour. Before anyone enters:

1. ==Get permission== and follow the vessel's procedure or permit.
2. ==Isolate== the space from pipes and machinery that could pump something in.
3. ==Open and ventilate== the space well, mechanically if needed.
4. ==Test the atmosphere== with a calibrated gas meter for oxygen (normal air is about 21 per cent), flammable gas and
   toxic gas. Test at different heights.
5. Have a ==standby person== outside, in contact, with rescue and breathing apparatus ready, and a way to call help.
6. Keep testing while people are in there.

If someone collapses inside, raise the alarm and do not rush in. Many deaths in enclosed spaces are would-be rescuers.
Rescue only with breathing apparatus and a plan.

## A BC engine-room example

A deckhand on a work boat at Campbell River asks you to clear a blocked bilge pump suction in the aft void. You do not
send him into the void. You ask for the hatch to be opened and the space ventilated for a time, test it with the gas
meter, set a standby on deck, and use a hand pump through the opening first. The meter reads low in oxygen at the
bottom, so nobody goes in until it clears.

## Common mistakes

- Trusting a stop button instead of locking out.
- Entering a confined space "just for a second" without testing it.
- Starting hot work with oil-soaked rags or lagging nearby.
- Wearing loose clothing or a ring near rotating machinery.
- Opening a radiator cap on a hot engine.
- Rescuing a collapsed person from a bad atmosphere without breathing apparatus.

## Check yourself

1. What do a WHMIS label and an SDS each tell you?
2. What are the three basic rights of a worker?
3. Put these in order for lockout: test, isolate, lock and tag, tell.
4. What are the main checks before hot work and before entering a tank?
5. What should you do first if a colleague collapses in a tank?
