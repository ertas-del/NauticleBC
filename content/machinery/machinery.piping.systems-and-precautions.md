# Piping systems, valves and precautions

Pipes, valves and pumps make up the circulatory system of a vessel. The syllabus asks you to identify ==bilge and
ballast systems, fuel and lubricating oil systems, valves, drains, traps and other fittings==, and to know the
precautions for ==pipe expansion, water hammer, cross connections, venting and overflow, and routine pumping==.

## Bilge system

The bilge system removes water that collects in the lowest parts of the vessel.

- A ==suction pipe== in each compartment ends in a ==strum box== (a strainer) to keep rubbish out of the pump.
- Each pipe has a ==non-return valve== so water cannot flow back, and so one flooded compartment cannot flood another.
- A ==bilge pump== (electric, engine-driven or hand) pumps overboard through a discharge valve and overboard
  non-return.
- ==Float switches== and ==high bilge alarms== warn of rising water.
- Oily bilge water must not be pumped overboard. It is a pollution offence. Keep the bilge clean and follow the
  pollution rules.

## Ballast system

Ballast tanks are filled or emptied to adjust a vessel's trim, list and draught. They have pumps, valves and
==vents==. Small vessels may have none, but the syllabus expects you to know the idea.

## Fuel and lubricating oil systems

Fuel runs from storage through a transfer pump, filters and a day tank to the engine. Lubricating oil circulates from
the sump through a pump, filter and cooler. Both systems have ==valves==, ==filters==, ==drains== to remove water or
sludge, and ==gauges==.

## Valves

| Valve | Use |
|---|---|
| ==Gate== | fully open or fully closed; poor for regulating flow |
| ==Globe== | regulates flow |
| ==Ball== | quick quarter-turn open or closed |
| ==Butterfly== | quick, compact; large pipes |
| ==Non-return (check)== | allows flow one way only |
| ==Relief== | opens at a set pressure to protect the system |
| ==Seacock (sea valve)== | closes the hull opening for sea water |

## Gauges

A ==pressure gauge== shows pressure above atmospheric and belongs on the ==discharge== side. A ==vacuum gauge== (or a
compound gauge that reads both) shows pressure below atmospheric and belongs on the ==suction== side of a pump or
filter. A rising vacuum reading on a suction gauge means a blockage in the suction.

## Hot liquids and expansion

Steel pipe ==expands== when heated, so hot fluid lines have expansion bends, loops or expansion joints and are
supported so they can move. When you bring a hot line into service, ==warm it up gradually==, open the drains to clear
any condensed water, and do not close valves suddenly. Lagging protects people from burns and reduces heat loss.

## Water hammer

Water hammer is a pressure shock caused by suddenly stopping or changing the flow of liquid in a pipe, for example
by closing a valve fast or stopping a pump. It sounds like a hammer blow and can burst pipes and damage valves and
pumps. To reduce it, ==open and close valves slowly==, start pumps against a partly open discharge, remove air and
condensate from the line, and use air chambers or accumulators where fitted.

## Cross connections

Some pipework can serve more than one purpose, for example bilge, ballast and fire systems linked by valves. These
==cross connections== are useful but dangerous: the wrong valve line-up can put fuel into a water tank, or sea water
into a fuel tank, or let water flood a dry compartment. Always check the line-up before pumping, use blanks or
non-return valves where fitted, and close the valves after use.

## Venting and overflow

==Tank vents must be clear== when filling or pumping out. A blocked vent can burst a tank when filling and collapse it
when emptying. Overflows lead to a safe place, and spills must be contained.

## Accumulator tank

An ==accumulator== in a fresh water system holds a cushion of air, often in a rubber bladder, which keeps the
pressure steady and stops the pump starting and stopping all the time. If the pump cycles rapidly, the tank is probably
==waterlogged== and the air charge needs restoring.

## Tracing a bilge pump that is not lowering the level

1. Check the pump is running and the fuse or breaker is in.
2. Check the strum box and strainer for blockage.
3. Check the suction and discharge valves are open, and that the overboard non-return is not stuck.
4. Check for air leaks on the suction side and that the pump is primed.
5. Check the impeller, diaphragm or gears for wear or damage.
6. Check that the pump is large enough, and that water is not coming in faster than the pump can remove it. If so,
   tell the master immediately.

## Getting to know a new vessel

Study the piping diagrams, trace each system pipe by pipe, label the valves, and go over the line-ups with the
previous engineer or the owner before you take over.

## A BC engine-room example

You take over as SVMO on a 16 m passenger boat on a Gulf Islands route. On your first morning the high bilge alarm
sounds. You find the aft bilge pump is running but not moving water. The strum box is choked with a plastic bag. You
clear it and the level falls. Then you check why so much water collected: a leaking stern gland. You log it and tell
the master.

## Common mistakes

- Using a gate valve to regulate flow.
- Closing a valve fast on a hot or pressurised line.
- Pumping without checking the valve line-up.
- Filling a tank with a blocked vent.
