# Fire-fighting equipment, fixed systems, quick-closing valves and watertight integrity

You prevent fires by good housekeeping. You survive the ones you did not prevent by knowing your equipment before you
need it. The syllabus asks you to ==know the fire and safety equipment in a machinery space and how it is operated==, and
what keeps the hull watertight.

## Portable extinguishers

Match the extinguisher to the fire class from the last lesson.

| Type | Suits | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| ==Water== | ==A== only | Never on electrical or oil fires. Water conducts electricity and spreads burning oil. |
| ==Foam== | A and B | Floats on the liquid surface and smothers it. Not for live electrical fires. |
| ==Dry chemical== (powder) | B and C, many also A | Quick knock-down. Leaves a corrosive, messy powder. Check the label for classes. |
| ==Carbon dioxide (CO2)== | B and C | Leaves no residue. Displaces oxygen, so use with care in small spaces. Very cold at the horn. |

Operate any portable with the idea of ==PASS==: pull the pin, aim at the ==base of the flames==, squeeze the handle, and
sweep side to side. Stand upwind or with an exit behind you. A portable extinguisher empties quickly, often in well under a minute, so
use it on a small fire only and call for help first.

**Maintenance:** look at every extinguisher on your rounds. Check the pressure gauge is in the green, the pin and
tamper seal are in place, the hose and horn are clear, it is in its bracket and the label can be read. A competent
person services and tests them at the intervals required, and you record it.

## Fire pump, hydrants and hoses

A vessel with a fire main has a ==fire pump==, often driven off the main engine or by its own motor, with hydrants (outlets)
on deck and in the machinery space. A fire hose runs from the hydrant to a ==nozzle== that gives a jet or a spray (fog).
Jet reaches further. Fog protects you from heat and works on class A fires. Keep hoses properly stowed and nozzles
attached. Run the pump regularly to prove it works.

A ==fire blanket== smothers a small galley or oil fire. A fire bucket with sand is the old but effective way to deal with
a small spill fire. Breathing apparatus (a ==self-contained breathing apparatus==) and escape sets let trained people
enter or leave smoke. Never enter smoke without the right gear and training.

## Fixed fire-extinguishing systems

Many commercial vessels have a fixed system in the machinery space. The commonest is a bank of ==CO2 cylinders== piped
to nozzles in the space. Others use foam, water mist, or a different gas. All work the same way: they flood the space so
the fire has no oxygen (or no heat).

The gas is dangerous to people. A person breathing CO2 in a flooded space passes out within minutes. So the release
sequence is strict:

1. Raise the alarm and tell the bridge.
2. ==Account for everyone== who was in the space or could have been. Count heads.
3. ==Stop engines, fuel pumps and ventilation fans==, and shut the quick-closing valves.
4. ==Close all openings==: doors, hatches, dampers, skylights. A fire needs a sealed space.
5. Discharge on the master's order. A ==pre-discharge alarm== sounds in the space and often a delay lets last people leave.
6. Keep the space shut. ==Do not re-enter== until it has cooled and been ventilated and is declared safe, with breathing
   apparatus if there is any doubt.

If the alarm sounds before a discharge, leave at once and never switch it off.

## Quick-closing valves and remote stops

A fire in the engine room must not keep being fed. So a vessel has ==quick-closing valves== on fuel tank outlets that
you can close from outside the machinery space, often with a pull wire or lever on deck. There are also ==remote stops==
for the fuel transfer pumps, ventilation fans and sometimes the engines themselves. The point is that you can stop fuel
and air without having to go into a space full of fire and smoke.

Test these on a schedule. Make sure the pull wires move freely, are clearly labelled, and nothing is stowed in front
of them. After testing, reset them and make sure the fuel is turned back on. Record it.

## Detection and alarms

Heat and smoke detectors in the machinery space give early warning at a panel on the bridge. Test them regularly and
never disable one without permission. A bilge high-level alarm, covered in the watchkeeping lesson, is part of the same
safety family.

## Watertight, weathertight and fire-resisting closures

- A ==watertight door== or hatch resists water under pressure when a compartment floods, so it keeps the vessel
  subdivided. Keep it closed at sea unless someone is passing, and never wedge it open.
- A ==weathertight== closure keeps out spray and rain, not flooding. A ventilation louvre is weathertight at best.
- A ==fire door== slows the spread of fire and smoke. It also must stay closed to work. Dampers close vent trunks and are
  closed before a fixed system is released.
- Bulkheads and decks that divide the vessel limit flooding and fire to one compartment.

For damage control, know where wedges, soft plugs and a patch kit are kept, and which bilge pumps cover which
compartment.

## A BC engine-room example

You are the engineer on a 20 m passenger vessel running between Nanaimo and Vancouver. In a drill, the master calls
"fire in the engine room". You close the quick-closing valves with the deck wire, stop the fan, report the headcount
(all out), shut the engine-room door and vents, and wait for the master's order to release the CO2. Nobody goes
back in until the master and you agree it is safe.

## Common mistakes

- Spraying water on an electrical or oil fire.
- Fighting a fire without raising the alarm, or without an exit behind you.
- Releasing a fixed system before everyone is accounted for or before openings and fans are shut.
- Re-entering too soon after a gas release.
- Leaving a watertight door wedged open, or stowing gear in front of a remote stop.
