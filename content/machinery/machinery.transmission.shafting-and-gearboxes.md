# Power transmission: shafting, gearboxes and propellers

The diesel makes power at the flywheel. Getting that power to the water, in the right direction, at the right speed,
without breaking anything on the way, is the job of the drive line. The SVMO syllabus asks you to ==name the parts, say
what each does, and spot the faults==. This lesson follows the power from the engine to the propeller.

## The power path

On a typical small single-screw vessel the path is:

==engine, flexible coupling, gearbox, intermediate shaft, thrust block, propeller shaft through the stern tube and stern
gland, propeller==

On many small vessels the thrust bearing is built into the gearbox, so there is no separate thrust block. Twin-screw
vessels have two complete lines. Some boats have a sterndrive or a water jet, which are different machines doing the
same job.

## Coupling

The ==flexible coupling== joins the engine (or gearbox) to the shaft. A small amount of flexibility absorbs vibration,
torque pulses and slight misalignment. Rubber elements perish with age, oil and heat, so look for cracks, loose bolts
and rubber dust. A shaft line that is out of line tears at bearings and seals, so the alignment is checked after any
work or if the engine mounts have been disturbed.

## Gearbox

The gearbox (marine gear) does three things:

- It lets you go ==ahead, astern or neutral== without reversing the engine, using clutches.
- It ==reduces the speed== of the shaft, because a diesel runs fast and an efficient propeller turns slowly.
- On many designs it contains the thrust bearing.

The ==reduction ratio== is the engine speed divided by the shaft speed. A ratio of 3:1 means the engine turns three times
for each turn of the shaft.

Worked example. The engine runs at 2 400 rpm and the gearbox ratio is 3:1. The shaft turns at 2 400 divided by 3, which
is ==800 rpm==.

Gearbox care:

1. Before starting, ==check the oil level and condition== (milky means water, burnt smell means overheating). Use the
   oil the maker specifies.
2. Watch oil pressure and temperature when running. Many gearboxes have a cooler, usually a small heat exchanger fed
   by raw water or the engine coolant.
3. Change gear with the engine at ==idle==, and pause in neutral when going from ahead to astern, so the clutch plates
   engage without shock. Never force the lever.
4. If the vessel is being towed or sailing with the engine stopped, the shaft may need to be locked or the box put in
   neutral, as the maker directs, because a freewheeling propeller can turn the gearbox with no oil pressure and damage it.

## Shaft and bearings

The shaft is a long steel bar supported by plain bearings (bushes) that are oil or water lubricated, or by roller
bearings with grease. A bearing that is hot to the hand, noisy or leaking lubricant is on its way out. Check line shaft
bearings by feel (carefully, around moving machinery), and watch for fresh grease or oil leaks. Guards must be fitted
over rotating shafts and couplings, and nobody should work near a turning shaft with loose clothing, long hair or a rag in hand.

## Thrust block

When the propeller pushes water astern, the water pushes the propeller forward. That ==thrust passes down the shaft to
the thrust block==, which holds it in a bearing and passes it to the hull structure through its seating. Without a
thrust block, the engine and gearbox would be shoved forward off their mounts. The block runs in an oil bath, so check
oil level and temperature.

## Stern tube and stern gland

The shaft leaves the hull through a ==stern tube==, which has a bearing at the outer end, usually a rubber (cutless)
bearing lubricated by sea water, or a metal bearing lubricated by oil. The ==stern gland== seals the inboard end so
sea water does not flood in.

A traditional **packed gland** has rings of flax or modern packing squeezed against the shaft by a gland follower.

- It ==should drip slightly==, a few drops a minute when running, because the water cools and lubricates the packing.
- Tighten the nuts evenly and a little at a time. If you screw it dry, the packing burns, the shaft is scored and the
  gland runs hot.
- If it leaks too much, take up the nuts a little, and if there is no adjustment left, repack it.

A **mechanical or "dripless" seal** uses a carbon face running against a stainless or ceramic ring. It needs cooling
water supplied by the maker's arrangement and checks for leaks, but it does not drip when working correctly. A leak
from either type that is more than expected goes to the master, as it is a hole in the hull.

## Propeller

A propeller has blades set at an angle, like a screw thread.

- ==Pitch== is the distance the propeller would move forward in one turn if it were screwing through a solid.
- ==Slip== is the shortfall between that theoretical advance and the real advance of the vessel, because water is not
  solid. Some slip is normal and needed, as it is what produces thrust.
- ==Cavitation== is the formation of vapour bubbles on the blades when the pressure is too low. It causes noise,
  vibration, pitting and loss of thrust. Typical causes are overloading, a damaged blade, or running too fast for the pitch.
- A ==right-handed== propeller turns clockwise (seen from astern) when going ahead.

Worked example. A propeller has a pitch of 0.8 m and turns at 500 rpm. In one minute the theoretical advance is
0.8 x 500 = 400 m. In one hour that is 24 000 m, or 24 km, which is ==12.96 knots== (24 000 divided by 1 852). The
vessel actually makes 9.72 knots. Slip is (12.96 minus 9.72) divided by 12.96, which is ==25 per cent==.

A bent or chipped blade, a rope round the shaft or weed on the blades leaves you with vibration and loss of speed.
Check the propeller whenever the vessel is lifted out.

## Faults in the drive line

| Symptom | Likely causes |
|---|---|
| Vibration at all speeds | Bent shaft, bent or fouled propeller, misaligned coupling, worn cutless bearing |
| Hot gearbox | Low oil, blocked cooler, slipping clutch, over-long operation at low rpm |
| Hot or dry stern gland | Over-tightened packing, no lubrication |
| Heavy dripping at the gland | Worn packing or shaft, loose nuts |
| Engine revs rise, vessel does not respond | Slipping clutch, damaged or fouled propeller, shaft key sheared |

## A BC engine-room example

You are running a 14 m charter boat from Tofino. After a rough passage through rocky water the engine room feels fine
but the whole boat shakes at 1 800 rpm. You stop, check the gearbox oil, look at the stern gland (it is dripping
normally), and ask the deckhand to check over the stern. A length of heavy rope is wrapped round the propeller. You
report it, and after returning to dock a diver clears it.

## Common mistakes

- Screwing the stern gland up until it is dry.
- Forgetting to check the gearbox oil.
- Changing from ahead to astern at speed.
- Confusing pitch (distance per turn) with diameter (blade tip to tip).
- Treating all slip as a fault. Some slip is normal.
