# Preventing pollution: bilge oil, garbage, bunkering and spill response

A cup of oil spreads into a film that can cover a large area of water and harm birds, fish and shorelines. In BC, with its
fish, shellfish and tourism, spills are taken seriously and can lead to large fines. The syllabus lists ==pollution
prevention== as its own topic and expects you to know the rules in general terms and to ==act correctly when something
spills==. Exact limits and forms change, so this lesson stays general. Always check the current regulations and your
company's procedures.

## Where pollution comes from

- ==Bilge water.== It collects drips of fuel, oil and coolant. Pumped overboard without treatment, it carries the oil
  out with it.
- ==Leaks== at glands, hose joints, filters, drains and cooler tubes.
- ==Overflows== from overfilled tanks, or fuel pushed out of a tank vent during transfer or bunkering.
- ==Sludge and waste oil== from oil changes, filters, purifier waste and rags.
- ==Garbage== including plastics, food waste, packaging, fishing gear and cleaning rags.
- ==Sewage== and grey water, where a vessel has a holding tank or treatment system.
- A grounding or collision that holes a tank.

## The general rule

As a rule, ==do not pump oil or oily water into the sea==. Regulations allow limited discharges only if the vessel has the
approved equipment and is operating within set limits. Those details depend on vessel size, location and equipment. What
you need to remember is the habit: ==keep it on board== in a holding tank or drums, and put it ashore at a reception
facility.

- Keep the bilge clean so there is little to pump. Fix leaks at the source.
- Never open a bilge pump overboard discharge without checking what is in the bilge.
- Never put detergent in the bilge to "break up" oil. It only makes the sheen harder to see.
- Keep waste oil in labelled drums or tanks and land it ashore. Keep oily rags in a closed metal bin.

For **garbage**, ==never throw plastics overboard anywhere==. Other garbage is limited by distance from land and regulation.
For **sewage**, discharge is restricted close to shore, in harbours and in sensitive areas. Hold it until you are
allowed to discharge or can pump out ashore.

## Records and plans

- An ==oil record book== records bilge pumping, sludge disposal and fuel transfers where regulations require one for
  the vessel. Entries are made promptly, dated and signed. Whether your vessel needs one depends on its size and type.
- A ==shipboard oil pollution emergency plan== (sometimes called SOPEP) tells the crew who to call, what to do in a spill and
  where the spill gear is kept. Know where yours is and what it says.
- Keep a log of bunkering and transfers. A good record helps you show you did the right thing.

## Bunkering without a spill

Most spills happen at the fuel dock, not at sea. Treat bunkering as a planned job.

**Before**
1. Agree the plan with the master and the fuel supplier: which tank, how much, what rate, and how to stop.
2. ==Check communication== between the vessel and the fuel source. A signal to stop must reach the pump at once.
3. Calculate how much room each tank has, and set the valves for the correct tank only.
4. ==Plug the scuppers== and set out drip trays under connections. Put spill gear (sorbent pads, boom, bucket) where
   you can reach it.
5. Make sure there is no smoking or hot work, and that the vessel is secure alongside and bonded or earthed where needed.

**During**
- Start at a ==slow rate== and check for leaks before raising it.
- Watch the ==tank level and the tank vent==, and stay at the valves. Check by sounding, not by guesswork.
- Slow the flow before the tank fills. ==Stop well before it is full.== Leave room for expansion.
- Keep checking the hose and the connection.

**After**
- Close the valves, drain the hose into a container, disconnect, and cap the fill.
- Sound the tanks, record the amount received and any problems, and check the bilge for leaks.

## If oil does spill

1. ==Stop the source== (stop the pump, close the valve) and raise the alarm.
2. ==Contain it== with sorbent pads, boom or whatever you have. Keep it on the deck or in the drip tray if possible.
3. ==Tell the master==, who reports it. A spill into the water, or one that might have reached it, must be reported
   ==immediately==, in Canada through the Canadian Coast Guard (for example via Marine Communications and Traffic
   Services on VHF channel 16, or the regional pollution reporting line). Confirm the current contact details.
4. Follow your emergency plan. Do not use dispersants or detergent unless an authority tells you to.
5. Keep sorbent materials as oily waste for disposal ashore.
6. Record what happened and what you did.

## A BC engine-room example

Your supplier at Port Hardy starts pumping at a high rate. You are at the valves and see the level in the tank
sounding rise faster than you expected, so you signal "stop" with the agreed hand signal and radio call. A few
millilitres seep from the vent into your drip tray. Because you planned for it, the tray catches it and the pads clean it up. No
oil reaches the water, but you still record the event and tell the master.

## Common mistakes

- Pumping the bilge overboard "just this once".
- Leaving the scuppers unplugged while fuelling.
- Trusting the gauge, not a sounding, during bunkering.
- Topping the tank up to the brim.
- Adding detergent to a spill.
- Not reporting a small spill because it looks minor.
