# Engine cooling systems: fresh water and raw water

About a third of the energy in the fuel leaves an engine as heat in the cooling system. If that heat is not carried
away, the engine overheats and seizes. The SVMO syllabus asks you to identify and operate ==air and liquid cooling
systems==. On most small vessels that means a ==closed fresh water circuit== backed by a ==raw (sea) water circuit==.

## The two circuits

**Fresh water (closed) circuit.** A circulating pump pushes coolant through passages in the block and head, then to
the heat exchanger and back. The coolant is treated fresh water with additives. Because it is a closed loop, nothing
salty ever enters the engine.

**Raw water circuit.** Sea water enters through a ==sea chest or seacock== and a ==strainer==, is pushed by a raw
water pump (often a rubber ==impeller== pump) through the heat exchanger, oil coolers and sometimes the exhaust,
and goes back overboard. Heat passes from the fresh water to the sea water in the ==heat exchanger==.

{{figure:svmo-cooling-circuits}}

Some vessels use a ==keel cooler== instead: the fresh water runs through pipes fixed to the outside of the hull, so the
sea cools it directly and there is no raw water intake. Others use an ==air-cooled radiator== with a fan, as on a
truck.

## Components and their jobs

| Component | Purpose |
|---|---|
| ==Circulating pump== | Moves coolant round the engine |
| ==Thermostat== | Keeps the engine at working temperature: stays shut when cold, opens when warm |
| ==Expansion (header) tank== | Sits high so air escapes, coolant can expand, and the system stays full |
| ==Pressure cap== | Raises the system pressure and boiling point; releases excess pressure |
| ==Heat exchanger / keel cooler / radiator== | Transfers heat out of the coolant |
| ==Sea water strainer== | Catches weed, plastic and debris before the raw water pump |
| ==Raw water impeller pump== | Draws sea water through the circuit |
| ==Engine heater== (some) | Keeps the engine warm in cold weather |

## The pressure cap

The cap lets the system run under pressure, which raises the boiling point of the coolant. A faulty cap can cause
coolant to boil off, overflow, or hoses to collapse as the engine cools. ==Never remove a hot cap==: the coolant can
flash to steam and scald you. Let the engine cool and cover the cap with a cloth when opening it.

## Additives

Fresh water is treated with a ==corrosion inhibitor and antifreeze== (glycol). The additives protect against rust and
scale inside the engine, raise the boiling point, and stop the coolant freezing. Mix to the maker's ratio and do not
mix different types.

## Why not sea water inside the engine?

Sea water in the engine block would cause ==corrosion==, build up ==salt and scale== in the passages, and lead to hot
spots. Heat exchangers separate the two so that the engine only ever sees treated fresh water.

## Pressures in coolers

In coolers where one fluid could leak into another, the ==pressures are arranged so a leak goes in the least harmful
direction==. In an oil cooler, oil is usually kept at a higher pressure than the water, so any leak sends oil into
the water (you see oil in the expansion tank) instead of water into the oil. In a fresh water and sea water heat
exchanger the fresh water is usually kept at the higher pressure, so a leak loses a little coolant rather than letting
salt into the engine.

## Leaks and how to find them

Common leak points are ==hoses and clamps==, the ==water pump seal==, the ==heat exchanger== and its end caps, gaskets,
drain plugs and core plugs. Look for drips, white crusty stains, a falling expansion tank level, and a damp bilge. A
pressure test can find hidden leaks. Fix them by replacing the damaged part. Milky oil or white smoke can mean coolant
is entering the engine through a failed head gasket or cooler.

## Overheating

Typical causes:

- Low coolant level
- ==Blocked sea water strainer== or intake
- ==Worn or failed raw water impeller==, or a loose or broken drive belt
- Stuck ==thermostat==
- Blocked or fouled heat exchanger or radiator
- Air in the system
- Engine overloaded, for example by a fouled propeller

If the temperature alarm sounds: reduce load, tell the master, check the raw water flow at the exhaust, then check
coolant level, strainer, belt and impeller. Stop the engine if it keeps rising.

## A BC engine-room example

On a day-charter boat out of Victoria, the high temperature alarm sounds in a kelp bed. The water jet at the exhaust
is weak. You stop the engine, close the seacock, open the strainer and find it packed with kelp. You clean it, reopen
the seacock and restart, and the temperature comes back down. You note the cause in the log and plan to check the
strainer more often on that route.

## Common mistakes

- Opening a hot pressure cap.
- Adding plain water repeatedly instead of the correct coolant mix.
- Forgetting to reopen the seacock after cleaning the strainer.
- Running with a damaged impeller and no spare on board.
- Ignoring a slow rise in temperature.
