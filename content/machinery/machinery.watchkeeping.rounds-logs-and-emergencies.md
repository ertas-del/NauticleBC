# Watchkeeping: rounds, logs, handover and engine-room emergencies

Most of the time a good engineer is bored: they walk around, find nothing wrong and write down the numbers. That is
the job working well. The syllabus lists ==watchkeeping procedures and routine duties== as its own topic, and the oral
exam asks "what would you do if...". This lesson gives you a routine for the quiet times and a set of answers for the
bad ones.

## Before the voyage

Before departure, use the start-up checks from the engine lesson and add voyage checks:

- ==Oil, coolant and fuel levels==, filters, belts, hoses and leaks.
- ==Bilge levels and bilge pump and alarm== tested.
- ==Steering gear== moves freely and the standby system works.
- ==Fire and safety equipment== in place, quick-closing valves and remote stops free, fire doors working.
- Sea suctions open, strainers clean, anodes and stern gland checked.
- Fuel quantity worked out against the passage, with a reserve (see the fuels lesson).
- Batteries charged, lights and alarms working, the bridge told the engine is ready.

## Rounds

A round is a regular walk through the machinery space. Repeat it at intervals set by the company or the master. Use
all your senses.

| Sense | What to notice |
|---|---|
| ==Sight== | Leaks, bilge level, gauge readings, exhaust colour, loose parts, a dirty filter |
| ==Hearing== | New knocks, whines, squeals, changes in engine note or pump noise |
| ==Smell== | Hot oil, burning insulation, fuel, exhaust, gas |
| ==Touch== | Unusual heat or vibration on bearings, stern gland and pump casings (careful of hot surfaces) |

Check pressures and temperatures against the normal range, cooling-water discharge, the stern gland drip, alternator
and battery charge, hydraulic and air systems, and the bilge. Something that is slightly off today is the failure of
tomorrow. Report any change to the master.

## The engine-room log

The ==engine-room log== is the written record of what the machinery did. Enter readings at set intervals and note:

- Running hours, oil and coolant temperatures and pressures, battery voltage, fuel levels.
- Fuel and oil transfers, bunkering, bilge pumping and any pollution event.
- Faults, repairs, parts changed, and orders from the bridge (stops, standby, speed changes).
- Drills and tests (fire, bilge alarm, emergency stops).

Write neatly with the date and time and sign your entries. If you make a mistake, ==draw one line through it, initial it==
and write the correction. Never erase, tear out or cover an entry. The log can be used in investigations and survey.

## Handover

When you hand over a watch, make sure the relief knows:

- Which machinery is running, stopped or in standby, and the normal readings.
- Faults, alarms and anything that behaved oddly.
- Work in progress, isolated systems and lockouts.
- Fuel quantity, orders from the master and any change of plan.

Do not hand over to someone who is not fit for watch, and do not leave until the relief has taken over and understands.
Do not hand over in the middle of an emergency.

## Talking to the bridge

The bridge cannot see your machinery. Tell the master ==early== about any problem, any shutdown, any alarm, or any
need to reduce speed. Acknowledge engine orders, repeat them, and log them. A small voice from the engine room can
save a big problem.

## The seven standard emergencies

For each one, tell the bridge first, then use this order of thinking: stay safe, find the cause, stop the damage,
restore, record.

**1. Low oil pressure.** Tell the bridge. If it does not return at once, stop the engine, because bearings can be ruined in
seconds. Check oil level, look for leaks, filter, and the gauge itself. Do not restart until you know the cause.

**2. High coolant temperature.** Tell the bridge and reduce load or speed. Check coolant level (only when cool),
sea-water flow and strainer, pump impeller, belts, thermostat and any blocked cooler. Do not open a hot pressure cap.
Do not pour cold water into a hot engine. If it keeps rising, stop the engine and let it cool.

**3. Low coolant.** Stop and let the engine cool. Find the leak (hoses, clamps, pump seal, gasket). Refill slowly with the
right mixture when cool, bleed air, and restart only when the leak is found and fixed. Check oil for a milky look.

**4. High bilge alarm.** Check the alarm is real by looking and sounding. Find the source: a split hose, a failed fitting,
a leaking seacock, the stern gland, or the hull. Start the bilge pump, tell the bridge, close the sea valve or isolate
the leak, and plug or patch. If the water is rising faster than the pump can clear, use all pumps and tell the master so
they can call for help.

**5. Engine-room fire.** Raise the alarm and tell the bridge. Stop the engine, fuel and ventilation using the remote stops and
quick-closing valves, close openings, and account for everyone. Use a portable extinguisher on a small fire only if
safe and you have an exit. If the fire is not out, leave, shut the door, and release the fixed system when the master
orders. Do not re-enter until it is declared safe.

**6. Unexpected engine stop.** Tell the bridge, because they may need to anchor or avoid something. Check for alarms and
obvious causes: fuel valves, tank level, filters, air in the fuel line, a fouled propeller, or a shutdown that tripped. Fix
the cause before cranking, and if it was a safety shutdown, find out why it operated. Restart only when safe.

**7. Suspected grounding.** Tell the master, who decides what to do. Sound the bilges and tanks, check the stern gland and
shaft for damage and vibration, and the rudder and steering. Check the sea strainers and cooling intake for sand and
mud. Do not go astern or restart without the master's decision, because that may worsen damage or free a holed hull.

## Know your certificate

An SVMO certificate lets you act as chief or watchkeeping engineer on small vessels within set power limits and
voyage types. A ==restricted (SVMO-R) certificate== is valid ==only on the named vessel==, a passenger vessel under 750 kW.
Check the current limits on the certificate itself and with Transport Canada.

## A BC engine-room example

On a night run from Vancouver to Gibsons, the oil pressure on the single engine dips and then drops to zero. You tell
the bridge, shut the engine down, and the master stops the vessel. You check the dipstick: the sump is almost empty. A
return hose to the cooler has split and oil has gone into the bilge. You replace the hose, refill, clean the bilge,
restart and watch the gauge. You write the event, time, and cause in the log.

## Common mistakes

- Not telling the bridge, or telling them late.
- Restarting before finding the cause.
- Opening a hot pressure cap.
- Erasing a log entry.
- Entering a smoky or flooded space alone.
- Rushing a handover, or leaving without confirming the relief understands.
