# Pumps: types, priming and fault-finding

Pumps move the liquids that keep a vessel running and afloat: cooling water, fuel, lubricating oil, hydraulic oil,
bilge water and fire water. The SVMO syllabus asks you to identify and operate ==reciprocating, centrifugal, screw and
displacement gear pumps==. For each you should know how it works, where it is used, and what to check when it fails.

## Two families

- **Centrifugal (kinetic) pumps** throw the liquid outward using a spinning impeller.
- **Positive displacement pumps** trap a fixed amount of liquid in a space and force it out. Reciprocating, gear,
  screw and flexible-impeller pumps are all of this type.

## Priming

==Priming== means filling the suction pipe and pump casing with liquid so the pump can draw liquid in instead of
churning air. Most ==centrifugal pumps are not self-priming==: they must be below the liquid level or be primed by
hand or by a separate priming device. ==Positive displacement pumps are usually self-priming==, because they can pump
air and so lift the liquid up the suction pipe.

## Pump types

| Pump | How it works | Typical uses | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| ==Centrifugal== | impeller spins liquid outward | engine cooling water, fire, bilge, general service | high flow, modest pressure; needs priming; flow falls as discharge pressure rises |
| ==Reciprocating== (piston or diaphragm) | piston or diaphragm moves back and forth | hand bilge pumps, diaphragm bilge pumps, small high-pressure jobs | pulsating flow; self-priming; needs relief valve |
| ==Gear== | two meshing gears carry liquid round the casing | lubricating oil, fuel transfer, hydraulic oil | positive displacement; needs relief valve; do not run dry |
| ==Screw== | two or three meshing screws push liquid along | fuel and oil transfer, thick liquids | smooth, quiet flow; positive displacement |
| ==Flexible impeller== | rubber vanes sweep liquid round an eccentric housing | engine raw water cooling, small bilge pumps | self-priming; ==never run dry==; replace impeller regularly |

## Relief valves and safety

A positive displacement pump keeps pushing no matter how high the pressure rises. If the discharge is closed
something will burst or stall. So these pumps have a ==relief valve== that returns excess flow to the suction side.
==Never start or run one against a closed discharge==, and never set a relief valve higher than the system rating.

## Fault-finding: pump not delivering

Work from the suction side to the discharge.

1. **Liquid supply:** is there liquid in the tank or bilge? Is the pump primed?
2. **Valves:** are the suction and discharge valves open, and is the right valve line-up set?
3. **Strainer:** is the suction strainer blocked? This is the commonest cause.
4. **Air leaks:** a loose joint, gland or cracked pipe on the suction side lets air in and the pump loses suction.
5. **Drive:** is the motor running and turning the right way? Check fuse, breaker, belt or coupling.
6. **Pump condition:** worn impeller, worn gears, a broken impeller vane, or a worn seal gives low flow.
7. **Relief valve:** a stuck-open relief valve sends the flow back round.
8. **Noise and vibration:** rattling or a gravel noise suggests ==cavitation==: the suction is restricted or the liquid
   is too hot or the lift is too great.

## A BC engine-room example

The raw water pump on a 14 m charter boat is giving only a thin trickle at the exhaust, and the engine temperature
is climbing. You stop the engine, close the seacock and take the pump cover off. One of the rubber vanes has
broken off. You fit the spare impeller, check where the broken vane went (it can block a heat exchanger), restart,
and see a strong flow again. You record the repair and order a new spare.

## Common mistakes

- Running a flexible-impeller pump dry, even briefly.
- Closing the discharge on a gear or piston pump.
- Assuming a pump has failed when only the strainer is blocked.
- Forgetting to open the suction valve.
