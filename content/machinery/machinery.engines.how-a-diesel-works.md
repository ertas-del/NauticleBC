# How a diesel engine works

Almost every SVMO question about faults, fuel, cooling or lubrication rests on this: what the engine does and where
each part sits. Learn the cycle first, then the parts.

## The four-stroke cycle

A stroke is one trip of the piston up or down. A four-stroke engine takes four strokes, so ==two turns of the
crankshaft==, to make one power stroke.

| Stroke | Piston | What happens |
|---|---|---|
| ==Intake== | Down | Inlet valve open. Air (only air in a diesel) is drawn in |
| ==Compression== | Up | Both valves shut. Air is squeezed and gets very hot |
| ==Power== | Down | Fuel is injected, burns, and pushes the piston down |
| ==Exhaust== | Up | Exhaust valve open. Burnt gas is pushed out |

Memory aid: ==suck, squeeze, bang, blow==.

## Compression ignition

In a diesel the air is squeezed to a high pressure (compression ratios of roughly 14 to 22 to 1), which heats it
well above the fuel's ignition point. Fuel sprayed in at the top of the stroke ==ignites by itself==. That is
==compression ignition==.

A gasoline engine mixes fuel and air first (carburettor or injection) and lights it with a ==spark plug==. If you
see spark plugs or a carburettor, it is a gasoline engine.

## Main parts

- **Cylinder block and liner**: hold the cylinders and the water passages.
- **Piston and rings**: the ==compression rings== seal the gas, the ==scraper ring== controls the oil film.
- **Connecting rod and crankshaft**: turn up-and-down movement into rotation.
- **Cylinder head**: holds the valves, the injector and, in many engines, the camshaft drive.
- **Valves and camshaft**: open and close the inlet and exhaust at the right moment. ==Valve lash== (the clearance)
  is set by adjusting the rocker arm, because heat makes metal grow. Too tight and a valve may not seat. Too loose
  and it is noisy and opens late.
- **Flywheel**: smooths out the power strokes.
- **Sump**: the oil reservoir at the bottom.

## The fuel system in brief

The ==injection pump== meters the right amount of fuel and delivers it at very high pressure at exactly the right
moment. The ==injectors== atomise it into a fine spray. Timing and quantity set power and economy. Clean fuel
matters because the nozzles are very fine (see the fuels lesson).

## The governor

A diesel has no air throttle. The ==governor== senses speed and changes the fuel delivered. Load goes up, speed
starts to drop, the governor gives more fuel. Load drops, it gives less. This keeps the speed steady.

## Two-stroke engines

A two-stroke engine makes a power stroke every turn. Fresh air is blown in through ports as the burnt gas leaves.
This is ==scavenging==, and it needs a blower or pump. Two-strokes are more common in big ships and in small
outboards than in small commercial inboards.

## Turbochargers

Exhaust gas spins a turbine, which drives a compressor on the same shaft. The compressor ==forces more air into the
cylinders==, so more fuel can be burned and the engine makes more power from the same size. Many turbocharged engines
also have an ==intercooler== (aftercooler) that cools the compressed air so it is denser.

## A BC engine-room example

On a 14 m crew boat out of Port Hardy, the maker's schedule calls for a valve lash check at the next service.
You find one exhaust valve noisy and loose. You set the lash cold with a feeler gauge to the figure in the manual,
and the tapping stops. You record the work in the log.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking a diesel has a spark plug or carburettor.
- Mixing up the stroke order.
- Setting valve lash to a guess instead of the maker's figure.
- Thinking the governor works on air. It works on fuel.
