# How a diesel engine works

Most of the machinery you look after on a small vessel is driven by, or depends on, an internal combustion engine.
The SVMO written exam expects you to know ==how it works in principle==, what its main parts are called, and how to
tell what it is doing from what you see, hear and read on the gauges. This lesson covers the engine itself. The next
lesson covers starting, running and faults.

## The four-stroke cycle

A stroke is one trip of the piston from one end of the cylinder to the other. In a ==four-stroke== engine the cycle
takes four strokes, which is ==two turns of the crankshaft==.

| Stroke | Piston | Valves | What happens (diesel) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Intake | moves down | inlet open | Air is drawn (or blown) into the cylinder |
| Compression | moves up | both closed | Air is squeezed and becomes very hot |
| Power | pushed down | both closed | Fuel is injected, burns, and the expanding gas drives the piston |
| Exhaust | moves up | exhaust open | Burnt gas is pushed out |

A handy memory phrase is ==suck, squeeze, bang, blow==. The valves are opened by a ==camshaft== (through pushrods and
rockers, or directly on an overhead-cam engine). The camshaft turns at ==half the speed of the crankshaft==, which is
why each valve opens once every two turns.

## The two-stroke cycle

A ==two-stroke== engine fires every turn of the crankshaft. Compression happens on the way up and power on the way down.
Exhaust and fresh air take place near the bottom of the downstroke. Fresh air is blown through the
cylinder to push the burnt gas out and refill it. That process is called ==scavenging==, and it needs a blower or
pump. Two-strokes give more power for their size but are less common on small commercial vessels than four-strokes.

## Diesel and gasoline compared

| | Diesel | Gasoline |
|---|---|---|
| Ignition | ==heat of compression==, no spark | ==spark plug== |
| Fuel and air | air only is compressed; fuel is injected at the end | fuel and air mixed before compression |
| Compression ratio | high (about 14:1 to 22:1) | lower (about 8:1 to 11:1) |
| Fuel metering | injection pump and injectors | carburettor or fuel injection |
| Fuel safety | high flash point, less explosive vapour | low flash point, dangerous vapour |

Because diesel fuel is only injected when it is wanted, a diesel has no throttle on its air supply. The engine
controls its output by changing ==how much fuel is injected==.

## Main parts

- **Cylinder block and liner:** the cylinders the pistons run in, with water passages around them.
- **Cylinder head:** closes the top, holds the valves, injectors and (usually) the rocker gear. A leaking head gasket
  lets water, oil or gas cross between passages.
- **Piston and connecting rod:** the piston takes the force of combustion and the rod passes it to the crankshaft.
- **Piston rings:** ==compression rings== seal combustion gas in and stop it leaking into the crankcase. The
  ==scraper (oil control) ring== wipes oil off the liner so it does not burn. Worn rings cause low compression, smoke
  and oil use.
- **Crankshaft and crankcase:** the crankshaft turns the up-and-down motion into rotation. The crankcase holds the
  lubricating oil.
- **Valves:** an inlet and an exhaust valve per cylinder (often two of each).
- **Fuel injection pump and injectors:** the pump delivers a measured amount of fuel at very high pressure at exactly
  the right moment. The injector sprays it into the cylinder as a fine mist.

## Valve lash

Valve lash is the small gap in the valve train, measured with a feeler gauge on a cold engine. The gap is needed
because metal expands as it heats. If the lash is ==too tight==, a valve may not fully close when hot, which leaks
compression and can burn the valve. If it is ==too loose==, the valves tick, open late and close early, and the engine
loses power. Always use the figure in the engine maker's manual.

## Speed and power control

Pushing the throttle lever does not open an air valve. It tells the ==governor== to allow more fuel. A governor senses
engine speed and moves the fuel rack up or down to keep that speed as the load changes. Without one, a diesel would
race when the load came off. Power is limited by how much air is available to burn the fuel. Fuel beyond that comes
out as black smoke.

## Turbocharger

A turbocharger has a turbine in the exhaust and a compressor in the air intake on one shaft. The exhaust gas spins the
turbine, which spins the compressor, which forces more air into the cylinders. More air allows more fuel to be burned,
so the same engine makes more power and burns cleaner. Many engines also cool the compressed air with an
==aftercooler== before it enters the cylinders. Turbochargers spin very fast and rely on engine oil, so follow the
maker's instructions for warm-up and cool-down.

## Typical operating figures

Normal oil pressure, coolant temperature and exhaust temperature depend on the engine. Coolant is commonly somewhere
around 70 to 90 degrees Celsius. What matters is that you know ==the normal figures for your engine== and notice when
they change. They are in the manual and often marked on the gauges.

## A BC engine-room example

You take over the engine room of a 14 m passenger boat that runs a four-stroke diesel on a short sheltered-waters
route. At the handover the previous operator tells you the engine "has been ticking a bit since the last service".
Ticking that comes and goes with engine speed points to valve lash that has gone loose or a worn rocker. The right
steps are to record it in the engine room log, tell the master, and arrange a lash check with a feeler gauge on the
next cold engine. It is not something to ignore until a valve fails.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking a diesel has spark plugs. It ignites by compression.
- Thinking the throttle controls air. In a diesel it controls the fuel.
- Forgetting that the camshaft runs at half crankshaft speed.
- Setting valve lash on a hot engine.
- Confusing compression rings (seal gas) with the scraper ring (controls oil).
