# Deck machinery: windlasses, winches, capstans and cranes

Deck machinery is where engineering meets seamanship. The deck crew operate it, and the engineer keeps it working and
makes it safe. The SVMO syllabus asks you to ==know the common machines, how they are powered and braked, and what
precautions apply==. This lesson is the engineer's view. The seamanship version, covering use at sea, is in the deck
lessons for the other tickets.

## The machines

| Machine | What it does | How you recognise it |
|---|---|---|
| ==Windlass== | Raises and lowers the anchor chain | Horizontal shaft, a notched wheel called a ==gypsy (wildcat)== for chain, often a warping drum beside it |
| ==Capstan== | Hauls mooring lines or anchor cable | Vertical shaft with a drum that spins about a vertical axis, the line wraps round it |
| ==Winch== | Hauls wire or rope on a drum, for towing, trawling, mooring, lifting | Horizontal drum, sometimes with a spooling gear and a brake |
| ==Crane or davit== | Lifts loads or a boat over the side | Boom, slewing base, hoist wire, with limit switches |

On small BC working boats, many machines are hydraulic and driven from a power pack on the engine. A windlass on a
small yacht may be electric. A hand pump or crank is often fitted as a back-up on the smallest vessels.

## How they are powered and controlled

- ==Hydraulic==: a hydraulic motor turns the drum or gypsy through a gearbox. Oil comes from an engine-driven pump or a
  separate power pack. The control valve gives speed and direction. Strong, smooth and good in wet conditions.
- ==Electric==: a motor and gearbox, with contactors and push-buttons or a hand remote. It needs a heavy cable and a
  breaker, and suits smaller windlasses. Keep it sealed from salt water.
- ==Manual==: a lever or handle and ratchet, used on the smallest vessels and as an emergency back-up.

Every machine has a way to hold and to release the load:

- A ==clutch== connects the drum or gypsy to the drive, so that it can be disengaged to let the anchor run out or the
  line render.
- A ==brake== (a band or disc, usually hand-tightened) holds the load when the drive is stopped and controls the speed
  when letting go.
- A ==chain stopper== or bow stopper grips the anchor chain when the vessel is anchored. It carries the strain, so the windlass
  is not holding the vessel against wind and tide. ==The windlass is for lifting the anchor, not for holding it==.
- The chain leads through a spurling pipe into the chain locker, where the inboard end is secured so the chain cannot run out
  completely.

Look for guards over gears and chain, an emergency stop, and a clearly marked safe working load on winches and cranes.

## Working with the anchor

The engineer or deckhand will start the power, take the load on the windlass, release the brake and let the anchor out
under control. A common sequence when anchoring is to lower the anchor until it touches bottom, then pay out chain as the
vessel backs away, and apply the brake. After the anchor is set, put the chain stopper on and take the load off the windlass. To
weigh anchor the windlass heaves the chain in while the vessel is driven slowly up to it. Heaving a vessel up to her
anchor against a wind or current from a distance overloads the windlass.

## Checks and maintenance

- Check the ==hydraulic reservoir level== and look for leaks around hoses, motors and fittings. If the level is well down,
  ==find the leak before topping up==. Oil on deck is a fire and slip hazard and will pollute the sea.
- Grease the bearings, gears and the pins at the points the maker lists.
- Check the brake lining and clutch. A worn lining, or one soaked in grease or oil, slips.
- Check the guards, bolts and foundations. Loose holding-down bolts are dangerous.
- Inspect wire rope for ==broken strands, kinks, flattening and corrosion==. A wire with kinks or a "birdcage" must be
  replaced. Chain should be checked for worn or stretched links.
- Test limit switches, emergency stops and overload devices on cranes.
- Run machines regularly. A winch that sits unused for months seizes.
- Keep controls and decks clean. Salt, ice and dirt cause seized controls and slips.
- In cold weather let the hydraulic oil warm up before heavy use.

## Safety

Deck machinery causes serious injuries. The usual dangers are fingers or clothing drawn into a drum, a line or wire
parting and whipping back, and a load falling.

1. ==Never stand in the bight of a line== (inside the loop) or in line with a wire or rope under strain. If it
   parts, it recoils.
2. Keep ==hands, hair and clothing== clear of rotating drums, gypsies and chain. Do not wear loose gloves near a turning
   drum. Do not lean over a gypsy.
3. Never exceed the ==safe working load== (SWL). Check the load before lifting, with attention to the angle of the
   sling and any shock loading in a seaway.
4. Keep clear of the load. Never walk under a suspended load.
5. Agree ==signals== between the operator and the person guiding the load, and one person only signals.
6. Check the area is clear before operating, and know where the emergency stop is.
7. Before repair, adjustment or cleaning, ==isolate the power and lock it out==, tag it, and release stored energy.
   Hydraulic pressure, a spring or a suspended load can move the machine without warning.
8. Land the load or support it securely before opening any hydraulic line.
9. Use proper PPE: safety footwear, hard hat where overhead loads are lifted, gloves suited to the work, and a flotation
   device when working over the side.
10. Do not use a machine you suspect is faulty. Report it, tag it, and have it repaired.

## A BC engine-room example

You are the engineer on a 25 m prawn boat out of Sointula. The anchor windlass labours and the hydraulic pressure gauge
reads low. You isolate it, find the reservoir half empty and a wet hose at the motor. You do not top up. You shut down the
power pack, lock it out, replace the hose, fill with the specified oil and cycle the windlass in both directions to bleed
the air. The deck crew watch from a safe distance until you hand it back.

## Common mistakes

- Leaving the anchor held by the windlass instead of the stopper.
- Cleaning or adjusting a machine that is still live.
- Topping up hydraulic oil without finding the leak.
- Standing inside a coil or in line with a loaded wire.
- Confusing a windlass (chain, horizontal) with a capstan (vertical).
- Wearing loose clothing near a drum.
