# Common ship materials: steel, cast iron, copper alloys, aluminium and GRP

Every part of a vessel is a choice between strength, weight, cost and how well the material survives salt water. The SVMO
written exam only asks you to ==recognise common materials, say what they are made of and know where they are used==.
Learn the short list below, then learn the one big way they fail: corrosion.

## Properties worth knowing

A handful of words come up again and again.

- ==Strength==: how much load a material carries before it breaks.
- ==Hardness==: resistance to scratching and wear.
- ==Ductile==: it stretches or bends before it breaks, like mild steel. ==Brittle==: it snaps with little warning, like
  cast iron.
- ==Corrosion resistance==: how well it lasts in damp, salty air and in seawater.
- ==Heat and electrical conduction==: copper and aluminium conduct both well, which is why copper is used for wiring and
  heat exchanger tubes.

## Iron and steel (ferrous metals)

A ==ferrous metal== is one that contains iron. Most are magnetic and most rust unless protected by paint or coating.

**Steel** is iron with a small, controlled amount of ==carbon==. The more carbon, the harder and stronger the steel,
but the less ductile and the harder to weld. Low-carbon "mild" steel is tough, bends before breaking and welds well,
so it is used for hulls, frames, pipes, tanks and bolts. Higher-carbon steels are used where hardness matters, such as
shafts, gears and tools.

**Cast iron** has more carbon than steel and is poured into moulds. It is cheap, casts into complex shapes, takes
compression well and damps vibration, so it is used for engine blocks, cylinder liners, pump casings and flywheels.
Its weakness is that it is ==brittle==: a hard knock or a sudden temperature change can crack it. Never cool a hot cast
iron part with cold water.

**Stainless steel** is steel with ==chromium== added. The chromium forms a thin film that resists rust, so it is used for
propeller shafts, fasteners, exhausts and galley equipment. Stainless can still corrode in stagnant seawater where air
cannot reach it, so do not treat it as maintenance-free.

## Copper and its alloys (non-ferrous)

A ==non-ferrous metal== has no significant iron in it. It does not rust, and is usually not magnetic.

- **Copper** is soft and ductile and conducts heat and electricity very well: wiring, small-bore fuel and oil pipes,
  heat exchanger tubes.
- **Brass** is ==copper and zinc==. It machines easily and is used for fittings, valves and fresh-water pipe work.
- **Bronze** is ==copper and tin== (often with other metals added). It is hard-wearing and handles seawater well, so it is
  used for propellers, seacocks, bearings and pump parts.
- **White metal** (a soft tin-based alloy) lines some bearings so that a failure wears the soft lining before the shaft.

Brass has one particular fault. In seawater the zinc can leach out, called ==dezincification==, leaving a weak, pink,
porous copper structure that looks fine but can fail suddenly. That is why a seacock below the waterline should be
bronze (or a marine-grade equivalent) and not plain brass.

## Aluminium, zinc and lead

**Aluminium alloy** is about one third the weight of steel, does not rust, and welds. It is common for small boat hulls,
superstructures, fuel tanks and some engine parts. Its weakness is the next topic: it is the least noble of the common
structural metals and corrodes quickly if it is in contact with copper, brass or steel in seawater.

**Zinc** is used for coating steel (galvanising) and for sacrificial anodes. **Lead** is soft and heavy: ballast,
lead-acid battery plates and some bearing metals.

## Non-metals

**Glass-reinforced plastic (GRP, fibreglass)** is glass fibre set in resin. It does not rust or electrolytically corrode and
is easy to repair, but it burns, can soften in heat and can delaminate after impact. **Rubber and plastics** are used
for hoses, gaskets, seals and some fittings. They age, harden and crack with heat, oil and sunlight, so check them as
part of your rounds.

## Galvanic corrosion and sacrificial anodes

Seawater conducts electricity. If two different metals touch (or are joined by a wire) while both are wet with
seawater, they behave like a weak battery and one of them wastes away. This is ==galvanic corrosion==. The metal that is
eaten is the ==less noble== one. Roughly, from most easily eaten to least: zinc, aluminium, steel, then brass and bronze,
with stainless steel and propeller bronze toward the noble end.

The bigger the difference between the metals, the faster the attack. A small aluminium part bolted to a large bronze
fitting in seawater will disappear quickly.

We use this on purpose. A ==sacrificial zinc anode== (aluminium anodes are used on some vessels) is bolted near the
propeller, rudder, shaft or inside coolers. Because it is less noble than everything around it, it corrodes first and
protects the rest. Check anodes on every haul-out or inspection, and ==replace them when they are mostly gone==. Do not
paint over an anode, because it stops working.

## A BC engine-room example

You are checking a 12 m aluminium crew boat in Prince Rupert. The raw-water strainer body is bronze and sits on an
aluminium bulkhead through a stainless bolt. A white crust and pitting have appeared on the aluminium around the bolt.
That is classic galvanic attack: three different metals are wet with seawater. The fix is to isolate the metals with
insulating washers and sleeves, replace the damaged part, and tell the owner. You also check the hull anodes, which are
worn to slivers and need replacing.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking "non-ferrous" means "non-corroding". Copper alloys and aluminium corrode too, just differently.
- Mixing up brass (copper and zinc) with bronze (copper and tin).
- Painting over a sacrificial anode or fitting one that is not bonded to the metal it should protect.
- Thinking hard means tough. Cast iron is hard but brittle.
- Using brass below the waterline where dezincification can occur.
