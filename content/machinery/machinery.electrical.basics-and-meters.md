# Electrical basics and measuring instruments

Almost everything aboard now depends on electricity: engine starting, lights, navigation equipment, alarms, pumps and
steering. The SVMO syllabus asks you to identify the ==measurement and protective devices== and to work safely with
==electric circuits==. This lesson covers the units, the two formulas you need, and the three meters.

## The four units

| Unit | Symbol | Measures | Water pipe comparison |
|---|---|---|---|
| ==Volt== | V | Electrical pressure that pushes current | Water pressure |
| ==Ampere (amp)== | A | Flow of current | Flow rate |
| ==Ohm== | Ω | Resistance to the flow | Narrow or blocked pipe |
| ==Watt== | W | Power, the rate of doing work | Work done by the flowing water |

## Two formulas

- **Ohm's law:** ==V = I × R==. Voltage equals current times resistance. So I = V ÷ R and R = V ÷ I.
- **Power:** ==P = V × I==. Watts equal volts times amps.

Examples:

- A 12 V circuit across a 4 Ω load carries 12 ÷ 4 = ==3 A==.
- A 24 V pump drawing 10 A uses 24 × 10 = ==240 W==.
- Double the voltage across the same resistance and the current ==doubles==.

A kilowatt (kW) is 1 000 watts. The size of a diesel engine's alternator and the total load you can put on it are
usually given in kW (or kVA on AC systems).

## Voltmeter

A voltmeter measures ==voltage==. Connect it ==in parallel==, across the component or supply you want to measure. Start
on a high range and step down. On DC, observe polarity (red to positive). Never use a voltmeter on a range lower than
the circuit voltage.

## Ammeter

An ammeter measures ==current==. It is connected ==in series==, so the current flows through it. Never connect an ammeter
across a supply, because its resistance is very low and it will act like a short circuit and may burn out or start a
fire. On heavier circuits a ==clamp meter== lets you measure current without breaking the circuit.

## Ohmmeter

An ohmmeter measures ==resistance== (and checks continuity). It supplies its own small current, so the circuit must be
==isolated and de-energised== first. Connecting it to a live circuit can ruin the meter and give a dangerous reading.
It is useful for checking a coil, a switch, a fuse (a blown fuse reads open circuit), or an insulation fault.

## Working safely

1. ==Isolate== the supply (breaker or switch off, fuse removed where appropriate).
2. ==Lock out and tag== the isolator so nobody can switch it back on.
3. ==Prove it dead== with your meter, after checking the meter on a known live source.
4. Use insulated tools, keep hands and the floor dry, and remove rings and watches.
5. Never work alone on live equipment, and never work live unless it cannot be avoided and you are qualified.
6. Treat batteries as always live, because they can still supply a huge current.

## A BC engine-room example

A bilge pump on your 12 m passenger boat will not run. You check that the float switch is working, then find the
12 V supply reads 0 V at the pump terminals with the pump switched on. You turn the breaker off and tag it, replace a
corroded connector, and check with a meter that the supply is back before you restore power. Then you test the pump.

## Common mistakes

- Putting an ammeter across the supply.
- Using an ohmmeter on a live circuit.
- Replacing a fuse without isolating first.
- Working on a circuit because "the switch is off" without checking it is dead.

## Check yourself

1. What is the current when 24 V is across a 6 Ω resistor?
2. A 12 V heater draws 5 A. How many watts is that?
3. How is a voltmeter connected, and an ammeter?
4. Why must a circuit be dead before using an ohmmeter?
5. What are the first three steps before working on electrical equipment?
