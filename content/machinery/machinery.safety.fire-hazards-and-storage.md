# Fire hazards in the engine room and safe storage of flammables

Fire is the engineer's worst emergency. A vessel at sea cannot call the fire brigade, the space is small and full of
fuel, and smoke fills it quickly. The syllabus asks you to ==recognise fire hazards and prevent fires==. This lesson is
about that: knowing what burns, what starts it, and how to store flammable things so they never do.

## What a fire needs

A fire needs three things at once, the ==fire triangle==: ==fuel==, ==heat== and ==oxygen==. Take away any one and the fire
goes out. Cooling with water removes heat. Smothering with foam, gas or closing the space removes oxygen. Stopping a
fuel pump or closing a fuel valve removes fuel. Prevention is the same idea: keep fuel and heat apart.

## Fire classes

Extinguishers are chosen by the kind of fire, which is why you must know the classes.

| Class | What burns | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| ==A== | Ordinary combustibles | wood, paper, cloth, rubbish, plastics |
| ==B== | Flammable liquids and gases | diesel, gasoline, lube oil, paint, propane |
| ==C== | Live electrical equipment | switchboards, motors, wiring |
| ==D== | Combustible metals | magnesium, some metal shavings (rare on small vessels) |

Class C is a hazard rather than a fuel. Once the power is isolated, the fire becomes Class A or B. Use the next lesson
to match each class to the right extinguisher.

## Flash point and vapour

A liquid does not burn; its ==vapour== does. The ==flash point== is the lowest temperature at which a liquid gives off
enough vapour to ignite briefly when a flame is present. The lower the flash point, the easier the liquid ignites.
Gasoline flashes far below freezing, so it gives off ignitable vapour at any temperature a vessel will see. Diesel flashes
at a much higher temperature, which is why it is the normal commercial fuel.

Gasoline and propane vapour is ==heavier than air==. It sinks into the bilge and collects there, waiting for a spark from
a starter, a switch or a pump. Before starting an inboard gasoline engine, ==run the bilge blower== for the time the maker
specifies and check by smell. Diesel vapour is a much smaller risk, but hot diesel spray can still ignite.

## Common ignition sources in a machinery space

- ==Hot exhaust pipes and manifolds==, especially when oil or fuel leaks onto them. Lagging and shields keep fuel and oil
  off them. Oil-soaked lagging is itself a hazard.
- ==Electrical faults==: loose connections, overloaded cables, worn insulation, arcing at switches and battery terminals.
- ==Hot work==: welding, grinding and cutting throw sparks a long way.
- ==Static electricity== when pouring or transferring fuel, and sparks from tools.
- ==Smoking== and naked flames, which are banned in and around machinery spaces and fuelling areas.
- ==Overheated bearings==, brakes and belts rubbing.

## Common fuels in a machinery space

- Fuel and oil leaks, drips and spray from a broken pipe or hose under pressure. A fine spray burns far more easily
  than a puddle.
- ==Oily bilges==. A film of oil in the bilge is fuel waiting for a spark.
- ==Oily rags and waste.== Cloth soaked in some oils slowly oxidises, producing heat. In a warm, bunched pile it can
  ignite on its own, called ==spontaneous combustion==.
- Paints, thinners, solvents and cleaning fluids.
- ==Hydrogen== from batteries on charge. It is explosive in small amounts, so battery spaces must be ventilated.
- Packing, lagging, insulation, wood and plastics in or near the machinery space.

## Storing flammables safely

- Keep fuel, oils, paints and solvents in ==approved, labelled containers== in a ventilated locker, away from heat and
  from the engine room itself where possible.
- Never keep rags in the open. Use a ==closed metal bin with a lid== and empty it ashore.
- ==Gas cylinders== (propane, welding gases) are kept secured upright, valves closed, caps on, in a ventilated place
  away from heat and from the accommodation. Leaked gas must not be able to collect low in the vessel. Never use oil or
  grease on oxygen cylinder fittings, because oxygen and oil can ignite violently.
- Fuel tank vents carry a fine ==wire gauze flame screen==, a flame arrester, to stop a flame from outside travelling into
  the tank. Keep it clean and undamaged, and do not remove it.
- Keep the engine room tidy. Stow tools and loose gear, clear access to exits and extinguishers, and never block a
  fire door or quick-closing valve.
- Fit and test detectors and alarms. A fire found early is a small fire.

## A BC engine-room example

On a small tug at Port Alberni you find a dark shiny patch on the lagging around the exhaust pipe. Closer inspection
shows a pinhole leak in a fuel return hose dripping onto it. You stop the engine, let it cool, replace the hose, clean the
lagging or replace it, and mop out the bilge. If the leak had sprayed onto the hot manifold, you would have had a
fire. You also find a rag soaked in oil under the workbench and put it in the closed metal bin.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking diesel cannot burn. It burns well once it is hot or sprayed as a fine mist.
- Believing an oily rag in a warm pile is harmless. It can ignite on its own.
- Letting gasoline vapour collect in the bilge, or starting the engine without ventilating first.
- Removing a vent flame screen because it clogs, rather than cleaning it.
- Storing paint and solvents next to the engine or a heater.
