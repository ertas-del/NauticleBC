# Electrical protection, generators and distribution

Electricity is dangerous in two ways: it can injure people, and it can start a fire. Protective devices exist to stop
both. This lesson covers the devices you will meet, how generators supply the vessel, and the circuits that must never
fail.

## Fuses

A ==fuse== has a thin wire that melts when the current is too high, cutting the circuit before the cables overheat.

- Replace it only with a fuse of the ==same rating and type==. A bigger fuse defeats the protection.
- Isolate the circuit first.
- Never bridge a fuse with wire or foil.
- A fuse that blows again straight away means there is a fault. Find it before trying again.

## Circuit breakers

A ==circuit breaker== trips (opens) automatically on overload or short circuit and can be reset by hand. Thermal
breakers need to cool for a short time before they will reset. ==Do not keep resetting a breaker that trips==. That is
a warning that something is wrong. Find and fix the cause first.

## Earth faults and earth lamps

An ==earth fault== (or ground fault) occurs when a live conductor touches the hull or a metal frame. On larger vessels
the distribution system is ==insulated== from the hull so that one earth fault does not stop the supply, but you must
find and fix it before a second one causes a short circuit.

==Earth lamps== are two lamps connected between the two lines and earth. With no fault they glow equally. If one line
earths, ==the lamp on that line goes dim or dark== and the other glows brightly. You then isolate circuits one at a
time until the lamps even out again, which tells you which circuit has the fault.

## Generators, alternators and motors

| Machine | Converts | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| ==Generator== | mechanical → electrical | Often used for any machine that makes electricity; strictly, a DC machine |
| ==Alternator== | mechanical → electrical (AC) | Driven by an engine; most small vessel charging systems |
| ==Motor== | electrical → mechanical | Runs pumps, winches, steering, fans |

Generator load is read from an ==ammeter== (current), often with a ==kilowatt meter==. Never exceed the rated load.

## Paralleling generators

On vessels with more than one generator you can connect them to the same busbar, which is called ==paralleling==. This
lets them ==share the load== and lets you change from one set to another without a blackout. Before connecting the
incoming generator it must match the bus in ==voltage, frequency and phase==, usually shown by a synchroscope or
synchronising lamps. On a small vessel with one generator you may never do this, but the syllabus expects you to know
the idea.

## Bus bars and emergencies

- **Blackout:** total loss of main power. The standby generator starts or you start it, and the ==emergency
  generator== or batteries supply the emergency services.
- **Essential bus:** supplies equipment essential to propulsion and safety.
- **Emergency bus:** supplied from the emergency generator or a battery, for emergency lighting, communications,
  steering and navigation lights.
- **Non-essential trip:** if the generator is overloaded, a ==non-essential trip== opens first to shed load and
  protect the essential services.

## Navigation lights and alarms

==Navigation-light circuits== and ==alarm circuits== are separate, protected circuits with their own supply and
usually a ==failure alarm== to warn when a lamp or the circuit fails. They must also be backed up by emergency power.
Do not use them for other loads.

## A BC engine-room example

On a night charter in Johnstone Strait, the navigation light failure alarm sounds on the bridge. You check the
panel and find the starboard side light fuse has blown. You find a chafed wire shorting to the hull. You tape and
re-run the wire, fit a correct-rated fuse, and test the light. You log the repair and tell the master. You did not
fit a larger fuse to "make it work".

## Common mistakes

- Replacing a fuse with a higher rating.
- Resetting a breaker again and again without finding the fault.
- Ignoring a dim earth lamp because "the lights still work".
- Using the navigation-light circuit to run other loads.
