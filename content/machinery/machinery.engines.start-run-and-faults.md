# Starting, running and fault-finding a diesel engine

The SVMO exam asks what you would **do**, and it favours the safe, cautious answer. This lesson is a routine to carry
into the engine room: check before you start, watch while you run, and react correctly when something changes.

## Before starting

Walk round the engine and check:

- ==Crankcase oil level==, on the dipstick, with the engine level and stopped for a few minutes.
- ==Coolant level== in the expansion tank (cold engine only).
- ==Fuel level==, and drain a little from the bottom of the fuel filter or water separator to check for water.
- ==Sea suction (seacock) open== and the sea water strainer clear.
- Belts, hoses and clamps, and any signs of ==leaks== on the engine or in the bilge.
- Gearbox oil level, and the gear lever in ==neutral==.
- Nothing loose on or near the engine: tools, rags, or lines.
- Batteries and electrical connections secure.

On many vessels you also tell the bridge that the engine is about to start and check that any engine-room alarms are
not already sounding.

## At start-up

Start the engine without cranking for too long (the starter can overheat, so use short attempts with rests, as the
maker advises). As soon as it fires, check ==oil pressure== first. Oil pressure should rise within a few seconds. If it
does not, ==stop the engine at once==, because an engine can be badly damaged in seconds without oil. Then check
that there are no alarms, no unusual noise, no leaks, and that cooling water is coming out at the exhaust or
overboard discharge.

## While running

Check the engine at regular intervals and record readings in the ==engine room log==. Watch:

- Oil pressure and coolant temperature.
- Exhaust colour and temperature.
- Noise and vibration.
- Leaks of oil, fuel and water, and the bilge level.
- Charging (battery voltage), and the stern gland (a slight drip is normal).
- Fuel level and gauge pressures.

Record ==running hours==. They tell you when the oil, filters and other services are due, and they help you spot
problems early.

## Exhaust smoke

| Colour | Usual cause |
|---|---|
| Light grey or almost clear | normal |
| ==Black== | too much fuel for the air: overload (for example a fouled propeller), dirty air filter, faulty injectors |
| ==Blue== | lubricating oil burning: worn rings or valve guides, too much oil in the sump, turbo seal |
| ==White== (after warm-up) | water in the cylinders (such as a failed head gasket), or unburnt fuel from low compression or late injection |

A little white smoke on a cold start that clears as the engine warms is normal.

## Responding to alarms

| Alarm | First actions |
|---|---|
| ==Low oil pressure== | Reduce load, and normally stop the engine. Tell the master. Check oil level, look for leaks, and do not restart until you know the cause. |
| ==High coolant temperature== | Reduce load, tell the master. Check coolant level, the sea water strainer, the belt or raw water impeller, and the thermostat. Stop if the temperature keeps rising. Do not remove a hot pressure cap. |
| ==Low coolant level== | Shut down if safe, let it cool, find the leak, then top up with the correct coolant. |
| ==High bilge alarm== | Find the source of the water and tell the master. |

## An engine that will not start

If the engine turns over but does not start, think about fuel, air and compression:

- ==No fuel==: empty tank, closed fuel valve, blocked filter, air in the fuel system, stop control left in the off position.
- ==Water or dirt in the fuel==.
- Cold weather, with glow plugs or heaters not working.
- Low compression (worn rings, leaking valves).

If the engine does not turn over at all, check batteries, terminals, starter, and any interlock or ==gear in neutral==.

## An engine that runs but has low power

Common causes are a blocked fuel or air filter, a fouled propeller or hull, restricted exhaust, injector faults,
overheating, wrong or contaminated fuel, and a turbocharger problem. Work through them in order, starting with the
easy checks.

## Crankcase explosions

A crankcase explosion occurs when an ==oil mist== forms inside the crankcase and is ignited by a ==hot spot== such
as an overheated bearing or a rubbing part. The warning signs include a smell of hot oil, smoke from the breather,
unusual noise, and a hot crankcase. If you suspect it:

1. Reduce speed and stop the engine as soon as it is safe.
2. ==Do not open the crankcase== or inspection doors straight away. Fresh air can feed a second, worse explosion.
3. Keep clear of the crankcase relief doors, tell the master, and wait for the engine to cool before opening.

## A BC engine-room example

On the way back to the dock, the high coolant temperature alarm sounds on your passenger boat. You reduce speed,
tell the master, and find that the raw water flow at the exhaust is weak. The sea water strainer is packed with
eelgrass. You stop the engine, close the seacock, clean the strainer, reopen the seacock, restart, and see a
strong stream of water again. You record the event in the log. If the flow had stayed weak you would have checked the
impeller next.

## Common mistakes

- Skipping the oil pressure check at start-up.
- Opening a hot expansion tank or radiator cap.
- Opening the crankcase straight after a suspected explosion.
- Restarting after a low oil pressure alarm without finding the cause.
- Thinking black smoke is normal for a loaded engine. A little may occur; a lot means a fault.
