# Anchors, cable and anchoring

**Study aid only.** Anchoring is a bridge and forecastle team job under the master's orders. Learn the words here, then
learn the drill on your ship. Where this lesson says "general seamanship", check with your instructor.

## What you need to know

TP 10936 lists the parts of the anchoring system (anchor, cable, joining shackles, windlass, brake, compressor or
stopper, hawse pipe, chain locker), the order of events for anchoring and weighing, and how to secure the anchor for sea and for
emergency use. The exam-sized facts:

- A ==shackle of cable== is ==15 fathoms== = ==27.5 m== (90 ft).
- Chain lengths are joined by ==joining shackles==, usually ==Kenter (lugless)==.
- ==Scope== is rode length compared with depth; about ==4:1 to 5:1== in moderate conditions, ==7:1 or more== in bad weather.
- ==Up and down==, ==aweigh== and ==brought up== are the words you must use correctly. (==Brought up== is general seamanship: no source was found for its wording, so it is not in the exam-focus card; check with your instructor.)
- A ==dragging== anchor shows in changing bearings and a jerking cable; flag ==Y== means "I am dragging my anchor".

## The anchor and the cable

**Parts of an anchor.** The ==shank== is the long central shaft. The ==flukes== (or arms) dig into the bottom. A
==stock== is a crosspiece on some anchors that makes the flukes turn and bite. On a stockless anchor the flukes
pivot on the head and the ==tripping palms== make them dig as the pull comes on. The ==crown D shackle== at the top
of the shank is where the cable is joined.

| Anchor | Description | Where it works | Weakness |
|---|---|---|---|
| ==Stockless== | Heavy pivoting flukes, no stock | Nearly universal on large ships; easy to handle and stows in the hawse pipe | Needs weight for holding |
| Admiralty (fisherman) | Shank with stock and two arms | Rocky or weedy ground; old design (general) | Awkward to stow |
| Plough (CQR) | Shaped like a plough | General use, stows in a bow roller | Can break out and return to the surface |
| ==Danforth== | Two large hinged flukes | Sand and mud (general); ==compact and buries well== | ==Struggles in gravel and weed== |
| ==Mushroom== | Inverted mushroom | ==Silt and mud==; sinks in and holds well | Poor on hard ground |
| ==Grapnel== | Four or more tines, no stock | ==Coral and rock==; sets quickly | ==Little hold in soft bottoms== |

**The cable.** Ships' anchor cable is made of chain (some small vessels use rope, or rope joined to chain). A length
of chain between joining shackles is a ==shackle== (the Americans call it a ==shot==). One shackle is
==15 fathoms== = ==27.5 m== = ==90 ft==. A ship may carry about 10 to 12 shackles on each anchor.

- Lengths are joined by ==joining shackles==. The usual type is the ==Kenter (lugless) shackle==, which has no lugs
  to snag, so it passes over the cable lifter without trouble. A lugged D shackle is another type.
- The ==bitter end== is the inboard end of the cable; it is secured in the chain locker in a way that can be
  slipped in an emergency (a pelican hook or senhouse slip is typical; check with your instructor).

**Marking.** The chain is ==marked at each shackle== so that the person on the windlass or the forecastle can see
how many shackles are out and report it to the bridge. Typical marks use painted links and wire turns. The scheme
==varies from ship to ship== (some ships use a count of white links that rises by one at each shackle, yachts often use
colours). The ==detachable (joining) link is marked== to show how many shots it is from the anchor, and the ==last
two shots are painted differently== as a warning that the end is near. Learn your own ship's scheme from the
anchor plan; a single worldwide scheme was not found in the sources read.

## Scope and rode

**Scope** is the ratio of the length of cable you put out to the depth of water. Depth is measured from the
==hawse pipe== (where the cable leaves the ship), so it is the water depth plus the height of the hawse pipe above the water.
Remember the tide: use the depth you will have at high water.

| Conditions | Rule of thumb (sources differ) |
|---|---|
| Moderate weather, room to swing | about ==4:1 to 5:1== |
| Bad or worsening weather, lee shore | ==7:1 or more==, up to 10:1 |

A longer scope lays more cable along the bottom, so the pull on the anchor is horizontal and it holds better. The master
decides the scope; these figures are rules of thumb from general sources.

### Worked example (invented numbers)

A ship will anchor in 12 m of water at high water. The hawse pipe is 4 m above the water. Use 5:1, then 7:1 in a gale.

1. Depth from the hawse pipe = 12 + 4 = 16 m.
2. At 5:1: rode = 16 x 5 = 80 m. Shackles = 80 / 27.5 = 2.91, so round up to 3 shackles (3 x 27.5 = 82.5 m).
3. At 7:1: rode = 16 x 7 = 112 m. Shackles = 112 / 27.5 = 4.07, so round up to 5 shackles (5 x 27.5 = 137.5 m). Four shackles
   would be 110 m, which is just short.

Always round the number of shackles up, never down, and check that there is room to swing on that scope.

## Anchoring: the order of events

The words are traditional; each ship has its own procedure (general seamanship; check with your instructor).

1. **Prepare.** The bridge says which anchor and how much cable. On the forecastle, take off the lashings and the
   devil's claw, remove the hawse pipe cover, check that the brake is on and the windlass is in gear or out of gear as
   the procedure says. Report "anchor ready".
2. **Walk it out.** The anchor is lowered by the windlass until it is just clear of the water or the bottom (depending on
   depth and procedure).
3. **Let go.** On the order to let go, the brake is released and the anchor and cable run out. The forecastle
   reports the number of shackles out as each marker passes ("one shackle in the water", "two shackles on deck").
4. **Check and hold.** The brake is used to check the cable and then to hold it as the ship's way is taken off. The
   forecastle reports how the cable is ==leading== (ahead, astern, or to port or starboard) and whether it is ==growing==
   (coming taut) or ==slack==.
5. **Brought up.** When the anchor has taken hold and the ship's way is stopped, the ship is ==brought up==. The forecastle
   reports "anchor holding".
6. **Veer to the planned scope**, apply the brake, ease the compressor or bow stopper and report. The bridge fixes the
   position and notes it (see the anchor watch lesson).

### Terms to know

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ==Up and down== | The cable is vertical and the ship is ==directly above the anchor== |
| ==Anchor aweigh== | The anchor ==has left the bottom== and hangs on the cable |
| ==Brought up== | The anchor has ==taken hold== and the ship's way is stopped (general) |
| ==Leading== | The direction the cable runs from the hawse pipe (e.g. leading ahead, leading to port) |
| ==Growing== | The cable is coming taut and leading in the direction the ship is moving |
| ==Clear anchor== | The anchor is free of the cable, buoy line or any foul (general) |
| ==Foul anchor== | The anchor is caught by its own cable or by something on the bottom (general) |
| ==Veer== | Let more cable out under control |
| ==Short stay== | Cable is only a little longer than the depth (general) |

## Weighing anchor

1. The bridge orders heave in. Engage the windlass, release the brake and heave. Report the ==shackles remaining==
   and the way the cable is leading as it comes in.
2. ==Up and down==: when the cable is vertical the ship is above the anchor. Report "up and down".
3. ==Anchor aweigh==: when the anchor breaks out and leaves the bottom, report "anchor aweigh". The ship is now
   free and the bridge is told so it can use the engine and helm.
4. ==Anchor in sight==, then ==anchor home== (housed in the hawse pipe). Report each stage. The wording differs by
   company (general).
5. ==Secure for sea==: put the brake hard on, fit the ==hawse pipe cover==, secure the ==bow stopper==, and set the
   ==devil's claw== with its ==bottle screw== tensioned, with lashings through the crown D shackle where used. For
   emergency use the anchor can be freed quickly by letting the brake off after clearing these.

## Dragging

A ship drags when the anchor fails to hold against the wind, current or sea.

- The watch checks the ship's position regularly by ==bearings==. A change in the bearings of fixed marks means the ship is
  moving. Report it at once; the master is called.
- On the cable you may see it jerking, leading in a new direction or coming up and down; a hand on the cable may
  feel ==jerks and jolts==, and on a drift lead the line goes tight and leads steadily forward.
- Signal flag ==Y== means "I am dragging my anchor". An anchored vessel shows the anchor light or ball (Rule 30; see
  `colregs.rule-30.anchored-aground` in the COLREGs module).
- The anchor watch itself is in `bridge-watch.anchor-and-port-watch`.

## Small vessels: the minimum rode

For small vessels the Small Vessel Regulations set a minimum length of cable, rope or chain to carry with the anchor.
For ==pleasure craft== and ==passenger-carrying vessels==:

| Vessel length | Anchor and rode |
|---|---|
| Up to 9 m | ==15 m== of cable, rope or chain (or a manual propelling device instead of an anchor) |
| More than 9 m up to 12 m | ==30 m== |
| More than 12 m | ==50 m== |

These are minimums for carrying; the scope you actually use depends on the depth and weather.

## Exam traps

- A shackle is **27.5 m (15 fathoms)**, not a 15 m or 30 m figure. The 15, 30 and 50 m figures are the **small-vessel minimum rode**, a different thing.
- **Aweigh** is the anchor just off the bottom; **up and down** is the cable vertical (anchor still on the bottom). Do not swap them.
- Using **water depth only** for scope. Add the height of the hawse pipe, and allow for the tide.
- Rounding **down** the number of shackles.
- Saying all ships mark chain the same way. Learn your ship's scheme.
- Calling **brought up** the same as **aweigh**. Brought up means it has taken hold.
