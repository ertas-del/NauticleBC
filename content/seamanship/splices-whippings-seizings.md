# Splices, whippings and seizings

**Study aid only.** Splicing is a hands-on skill. This lesson gives the names, jobs and rules. Your instructor and
practice with a fid or marlinspike turn it into a skill.

## What you need to know

- The course standard lists ==three-strand: short, long, cut, eye, back==; plus ==eight-strand eye== and ==braided eye==
  splices.
- ==Short splice== = strong but ==thicker==, joins two ropes. ==Long splice== = same ==diameter==, so it ==runs through
  a block==.
- ==Eye splice== = ==permanent loop== at the end. ==Back splice== = finishes an end so it cannot unlay (general).
- ==Whipping== stops a ==rope end unlaying==.

## Why splice at all?

A **knot** is quick but weakens the rope and can jam. A **splice** works the strands of the rope into itself (or into
another rope) so the join or eye is permanent, much neater, and loses less strength. A **whipping** and a **seizing**
use twine to bind.

Basics of splicing three-strand rope:

1. ==Unlay== the strands for a set length. To stop the strands unlaying further, put a ==whipping or a stop== (a few
   turns of twine) where the unlaying will end.
2. Use a ==marlinspike== or fid to open the lay so a strand can pass through.
3. ==Tuck== each strand ==over one and under one== of the standing part, in the direction with the lay of the rope,
   and haul it snug.
4. Repeat for several tucks, tapering the last ones by halving the strands, then roll the splice under your foot and
   trim the ends.

Never withdraw the spike until the tuck is made and the slack of the strand has been drawn through (a rule from older
texts: it stops the lay springing back).

## The splices

| Splice | What it does |
|---|---|
| ==Eye splice== | Makes a ==permanent loop== at the end of a rope: for a mooring eye, a lifting sling or fitting over a bollard |
| ==Short splice== | ==Joins two ropes== end to end. It is strong but ==thicker than the rope==, so it ==will not pass through a block==. Used where the rope does not need to run |
| ==Long splice== | Joins two ropes ==without increasing the diameter==, so it ==runs through a block== just like unspliced rope. Needs about ==four times== as much rope unlaid as a short splice; each pair of strands is laid in the gap left by the other, ==with the lay== |
| ==Cut splice== | Forms a ==loop between two ropes== (a loop part-way along rather than at the end) |
| ==Back splice== | Finishes a rope end by tucking its own strands back into the rope, so it cannot unlay (general) |
| ==Eight-strand eye splice== | An eye splice in eight-strand plaited rope (general: learn the method in the practical class) |
| ==Braided eye splice== | An eye splice in braided rope, where the splice is made by passing the strands through the braid (general: learn the method in the practical class) |

(Rows marked "general" are not covered by the references read for this lesson. Check them against your course notes.)

### Worked example: short or long splice?

A rope that will run through a pulley block has been cut and must be joined.

1. A short splice makes the join thicker than the rest of the rope.
2. A thicker join will jam in the sheave of the block.
3. So use a ==long splice==: it keeps the original diameter.
4. Remember it needs about four times as much rope unlaid. Starting with 1 m unlaid for a short splice, a long splice
   needs roughly 4 x 1 = 4 m unlaid on each rope. (Arithmetic verified in a script.)

### Safety and wire rope

- Wear gloves when handling wire; broken wire strands cut.
- ==Wire rope== is spliced with ==care to prevent kinks==. A ==kink== cannot be strained out and makes the wire unsafe.
- Wire splices are tucked with the lay and finished with the protruding ends cut and covered. Learn it on a practice
  length with your instructor.

## Whippings

A **whipping** is a tight wrap of twine round the end of a rope, so the strands do not fray or unlay. Any new cut end
should be whipped at once. (A fused end on synthetic rope is a short-term fix only.)

| Whipping | What it is |
|---|---|
| ==Common whipping== | The simplest: a series of turns of twine, with the ends finished by pulling a loop under the turns; ==stops the end fraying== |
| ==Palm-and-needle whipping== | A ==more permanent== whipping, sewn through the rope strands with a sailmaker's palm and needle |
| ==West Country whipping== | Made with a twine middle laid round the rope, with a half knot at every half turn, finished with a reef knot; neat and symmetrical |
| ==American whipping== | Like a common whipping, with both ends finished with a reef knot in the middle; sometimes used for hawser ends |

### Whipping to keep a rope end tidy: worked example

You cut a 12 m length of nylon line off a drum.

1. A raw end of laid rope will unlay in use.
2. Choose a common whipping for a temporary job or a palm-and-needle whipping for a permanent end.
3. Place the whipping close to the end, keep the turns tight, and finish with the ends under
   the turns so no loose twine is left.
4. Check that it is tight and cannot slide off.

## Seizings

A **seizing** binds two ropes (or two parts of one rope) together with twine, wire or small stuff. It is used for
example to make an eye in the end of a rope without a splice, to secure the bights of a sheepshank to the standing
parts, or to attach a block to a stay. Typical types are flat, round and racking seizings. (General seamanship: the
types and uses are not detailed in the references read for this lesson.) The course expects you to know that a
seizing is a lashing made with several turns of twine, tied off so it does not work loose, and to be able to make one
in the practical class.

## Exam traps

- Calling a short splice suitable for running through a block. Use a **long splice**.
- Forgetting that a long splice needs about **four times** more rope unlaid than a short splice.
- Mixing up a **cut splice** (a loop part-way along) with an **eye splice** (a loop at the end).
- Thinking a whipping is only decoration. It **stops the end unlaying**.
- Straining out a **kink** in wire. It cannot be removed and the wire is unsafe.
- Tucking against the lay: splices are made **with the lay**.
