# Man overboard: approach, recovery and aftercare

**Study aid only.** The look-out's actions and the three standard turns (Williamson, Anderson, Scharnow) are in
`bridge-watch.man-overboard`. This lesson is what comes after the turn: the approach, getting the person out of the
water, caring for them, and the reports.

## What you need to know

Every minute counts. In cold water, according to the Canadian Safe Boating Council, you have about ==1 minute of cold
shock==, about ==10 minutes of useful movement== and about ==1 hour== before hypothermia leads to unconsciousness
(the ==1-10-1 principle==). In BC the water is cold all year, so the plan is to get the person out in minutes, and the
person's lifejacket and your rescue plan are what keep them alive until then. The Canada Shipping Act, 2001 (==s. 132==)
also requires the master to ==render assistance to every person found at sea and in danger of being lost== (the wider duty; see
`canadian-regulations.distress-assistance-collision`).

## The Transport Canada procedure

Transport Canada's template for small vessels gives ten steps:

1. Whoever sees the fall ==raises a loud alarm and points at the person==.
2. The spotter ==keeps pointing without looking away== until the vessel reaches the person: losing sight of them is the
   danger.
3. ==Throw buoyant objects== the person can hold.
4. The skipper ==turns the wheel toward the side the person fell from==, which moves the propeller away from them, and
   steers where the spotter indicates.
5. Alert nearby vessels so they can help, without endangering the person.
6. ==Approach slowly under power, heading into the wind==, going to the lowest speed near the person.
7. ==Stop the engine== (use the kill cords) when the person is within reach.
8. If swells are significant, ==throw a heaving line or lifebuoy== so the vessel does not drift down on the person.
9. Bring the person aboard.
10. Complete an incident report and notify the company and authorities as required.

## The bridge: what the Coast Guard adds

The Canadian Coast Guard's guidelines for its own rescue boats (TP 11637, 1993; a concept source) add several points that
suit any vessel:

- The first person to see calls it and the side; the caller becomes the ==pointer==.
- ==Mark the position== at once. (The 1993 text mentions a Loran-C memory function; today use the plotter's or GNSS
  man-overboard function.)
- The helmsman begins the manoeuvre, the danger signal sounds and a ==lifebuoy is thrown to the person's side==.
- The ==coxswain assigns recovery crew== and ==chooses the approach and recovery method==.
- ==Approach slowly and as close as is safe without striking the person==, and ==stop the propeller rotation during
  recovery==, especially on the retrieving side.
- ==Inform Coast Guard Radio== as soon as practical without interfering with recovery.
- Recovery crew take their stations; a ==rescue swimmer wears flotation and a harness with a safety line tended== by a
  crew member.

The guidance on the best way to physically lift a person aboard (ladder, sling, scramble net, boarding platform) varies by
vessel. The sources read give no method, so learn yours from your own procedure and from drills.

## Who first, and aftercare

When several people are in the water, the Coast Guard guidance has ==persons without flotation or hypothermia
protection recovered first==. A person who may be hypothermic is ==lifted gently and horizontally== (not upright). After recovery, shelter and warm the person and watch their breathing and consciousness.
(The sources read give the horizontal lift; the rest is general first aid, so confirm it with your MED course.)

## Equipment

For a passenger-carrying vessel of up to 15 GT with 12 passengers or fewer (SVR Part 4, ==s. 409==):

- A ==lifejacket of appropriate size for every person==.
- A ==reboarding device==, ==unless the climb to reboard is 0.5 m or less==.
- Throwing gear by length:

| Length | Additional appliance |
|---|---|
| Up to 6 m | ==buoyant heaving line of at least 15 m== |
| Over 6 m to 9 m | heaving line, or a lifebuoy on a 15 m buoyant line |
| Over 9 m to 12 m | heaving line, and a lifebuoy on a 15 m buoyant line |
| Over 12 m | heaving line, and a lifebuoy with a self-igniting light or on a 15 m buoyant line |

The heaving line has a buoyant mass at one end to help it carry. For the 1-10-1 reasons above, the lifejacket's job
is to keep the person afloat through cold shock and incapacitation.

## Report it

Under the Transportation Safety Board Regulations ==s. 3(1)(b)== a ==person who falls overboard== from a ship other than a
pleasure craft is a ==reportable marine occurrence==, even if they are recovered unharmed. A ==death or serious injury==
from falling overboard is also reportable (s. 3(1)(a)). The available facts go in ==as soon as possible by the quickest
means==, the rest within 30 days.

## A BC example

An invented example. A 12 m commercial vessel is running near Hornby Island on a cool April day when a deckhand slips on
the foredeck and goes over the starboard side. The other deckhand shouts and points; the skipper puts the wheel to
starboard, marks the position on the plotter, and calls the Coast Guard on VHF. He approaches slowly into the light wind,
stops the engine when the deckhand is alongside, and the crew recover him using the reboarding ladder, lifting him
horizontally because he is shivering and slow to answer. The skipper reports the occurrence to the TSB and writes up the
drill points afterwards.

## Common mistakes

- The spotter **looks away** to help.
- Turning the wheel to the **wrong side**, so the stern swings toward the person.
- Approaching **too fast**, or with the **propeller turning** at the moment of recovery.
- Recovering someone who has a lifejacket **before** someone who does not.
- Lifting a cold person **upright**.
- Skipping the **TSB report** because the person was not hurt.
- Mixing up the **15 m** throwing gear requirement with the **0.5 m** reboarding limit.

## Check yourself

1. Why does the skipper turn the wheel toward the side the person fell from?
2. What does the spotter do, and for how long?
3. Which person is recovered first when two are in the water?
4. What is the reboarding device exemption in SVR s. 409?
5. Is a recovered, unhurt person who fell overboard reportable to the TSB?
