# Rope types, construction and strength

**Study aid only.** Rope strength figures depend on the maker, size, age and condition. Always use the figures marked
on or supplied with your own rope and equipment.

## What you need to know

- Rope families: ==natural fibre==, ==synthetic fibre==, ==modern high-strength (high-modulus)== and ==wire==.
- ==Breaking strength== is when it parts; ==safe working load (SWL)== is much less. The ratio is the ==safety factor==.
- For stage ropes the safety factor is at least ==six==.
- ==Nylon stretches a lot== (stores energy, snap-back danger); ==polypropylene floats==; ==manila shrinks when wet==.

## The four families

### Natural fibre

Made from plant fibres: ==manila== (the classic ship's rope), also sisal, hemp, cotton and coir. They have good grip
and handle well, but they rot, are weaker than synthetics, and swell and shrink with water.

- ==Manila== ==shrinks when wet== and is not good in salt water. Dry it before stowing, or it will rot.

### Synthetic fibre

Made from man-made fibres. They are stronger and do not rot. The three common ones for ship work:

| Fibre | Main points |
|---|---|
| ==Nylon== | The ==strongest for its size==. ==Stretches a lot== (up to about 40 per cent). ==About 10 to 15 per cent weaker wet==. Can be stored wet. Good as a shock absorber (anchor or mooring) but a parting line can ==snap back== |
| ==Polyester== | ==Not as strong or stretchy as nylon==. Can chafe. Low stretch makes it good where stretch is a nuisance |
| ==Polypropylene== | ==Floats==. ==Cheapest==. ==Can cut the skin== when running, ==deteriorates in sunlight==, limited heat and friction resistance |

**Snap-back.** A stretched line holds stored energy like a spring. If it parts, the broken end can whip back across
the deck at great speed. Stay out of the line of the pull ("the bight") and never stand in line with a loaded rope.

### High-modulus (modern high-strength) rope

Made from advanced fibres such as ==HMPE== (high-modulus polyethylene, sold under names such as Dyneema and Spectra)
and aramids such as Kevlar. These are very strong for their size. They are expensive and need special handling
and splicing. Your course will cover where your employer uses them. (Details on stretch and handling are general
knowledge and are not sourced here, so check them with your instructor.)

### Wire rope

Steel wire laid into strands over a core. ==Very strong with little stretch==. It needs ==lubricating== and regular
checks for ==corrosion and kinks==. Once a kink is formed it cannot be taken out by strain, and the rope is unsafe.
Wire rope is covered in more detail in a later lesson.

## Construction

### Laid rope

Fibres are twisted into **yarns**, yarns into **strands**, strands into **rope**. Each stage is twisted the opposite
way to the one before, so the rope holds together and does not untwist. The usual ship's rope is ==three-strand==,
right-handed (==hawser-laid==). Four strands around a heart is shroud-laid, and three hawser-laid ropes laid up
left-handed make a nine-strand ==cable-laid== rope.

### Braided rope

Modern ropes are often ==braided==: ==single braid== (commonly ==8 or 12 strands==), ==double braid== (a braided core
inside a braided cover) and others. Braided rope is smooth and does not twist or kink as easily as laid rope.

## Strength: breaking strength, SWL and safety factor

- **Breaking strength** (breaking load) is the load that will part a new rope in a test.
- **Safe working load (SWL)** is the most you may use in service. It is ==well below the breaking strength==, to leave
  a margin for wear, knots, shock and weakening with age.
- **Safety factor** = breaking strength divided by SWL.

Rules from Canadian law:

- Under the Tackle Regulations, cargo gear must ==not be loaded beyond its SWL==.
- Under the Maritime Occupational Health and Safety Regulations (s. 14), the supports, ropes and tackle for a stage must
  have a ==safety factor of at least six==.

### Worked example 1: SWL from breaking strength

A new rope has a breaking strength of 12 tonnes. It will be used for a stage support, which needs a safety factor of
at least 6.

1. SWL = breaking strength / safety factor = 12 / 6 = ==2 tonnes==.
2. So the rope may carry at most 2 tonnes in this use.

### Worked example 2: choosing a rope

A stage and two people weigh 1.5 tonnes in total. What breaking strength is needed with a safety factor of 6?

1. Breaking strength needed = load x safety factor = 1.5 x 6 = ==9 tonnes==.
2. Choose a rope (or tackle) with a breaking strength of **at least** 9 tonnes after allowing for how it is rigged.

### Worked example 3: the snatch-load trap

A load of 2 tonnes lifted steadily is 2 tonnes on the rope. An old textbook says that a jerk can put three or four
times the steady load on the rope. A jerk of 3 times gives 3 x 2 = 6 tonnes and 4 times gives 4 x 2 = 8 tonnes. A rope
rated at 2 tonnes SWL would then be loaded far above its SWL. That is why you lift smoothly and take up slack gently.

(Old rules of thumb such as "one-fifth of breaking strength" appear in old books. Do not use them as a modern standard.
The figure you use is the one in the regulation or the equipment's marked SWL.)

## Looking after rope

- ==Dry== rope before you store it.
- Keep it ==clean and tidy== and out of the way.
- Protect it from ==chafe and abrasion== (use chafing gear), and ==replace worn or frayed line==.
- ==Whip== the ends so they do not unlay.
- Keep it out of sunlight and away from chemicals, and check it before use. (The last two are standard practice; a
  fibre such as polypropylene is damaged by sunlight.)
- Wire: keep lubricated and check for corrosion and kinks. Never load a kinked wire.

## Exam traps

- Reading **SWL** as the breaking load. SWL is **much less**.
- Forgetting the **factor of six** for stage ropes and tackle.
- Saying nylon is weak because it stretches. It is the **strongest of the common ropes for its size**, but it stretches and is weaker wet.
- Expecting polypropylene to sink or to last in the sun. It **floats** and is damaged by sunlight.
- Standing in the bight of a loaded line.
- Treating an old rule of thumb ("one-fifth") as a regulation.
