# Lateral buoyage (IALA B) and direction of buoyage

**Study aid only. Not for navigation.** Use current charts, the Coast Guard's *The Canadian Aids to Navigation System*
and the *List of Lights, Buoys and Fog Signals* for real navigation.

## What you need to know

- Canada uses ==IALA Region B==. Lateral marks are ==red to starboard, green to port== when you go ==upstream==.
- ==Upstream== means ==from seaward toward the headwaters of a river or into a harbour==.
- Number and light follow the colour: ==green, odd, can-shaped== to port; ==red, even, cone-shaped== to starboard.
- Always ask: which way am I going, upstream or downstream? The colours only mean something once you know that.

## The idea behind a lateral mark

A lateral mark marks the **side of a channel**. Think of a road with a kerb on each side. The buoy tells you "the
safe water is on this side of me". Because a mark's meaning depends on which way you are travelling, we need a rule
that fixes the direction. That rule is the **direction of buoyage**.

The Canadian Coast Guard defines the ==upstream direction== as the direction a vessel takes ==proceeding from seaward
toward the headwaters of a river or into a harbour==. When you travel upstream:

- ==starboard hand aids are kept to starboard== (on your right), and
- ==port hand aids are kept to port== (on your left).

The old rhyme is **"red right returning"**: coming in from sea, keep red on your right. It is a memory aid, not the
official wording. Heading the other way (downstream, going out to sea), everything flips: you keep red on your *left*.

### The direction of buoyage on the BC coast

The Coast Guard states the upstream definition for Canadian waters in general, and BC is part of Canada, so the same
definition applies. On a coast with long inlets and island channels, it is easy to ask "upstream from where?". The
sources I read did not give a single coast-wide rule such as "north is upstream", and the Sailing Directions
(PAC 200) send you to the *List of Lights, Buoys and Fog Signals* rather than stating one. So in practice:

1. Work out which way is **from seaward toward the head of the channel, inlet or harbour** you are in.
2. Check that against the chart and the **numbering**: the buoys count up in the upstream direction.
3. If the two disagree, stop and sort it out before you commit to a side.

(Reviewer note: a certified instructor may give a BC coast-wide convention. This lesson does not claim one.)

## Port hand and starboard hand marks

| Feature | Port hand | Starboard hand |
|---|---|---|
| Colour | ==Green== | ==Red== |
| Unlit buoy shape | ==Flat-topped (can)== | ==Conical== |
| Topmark (if fitted) | Single ==green cylinder== | Single ==red cone, point up== |
| Numbers | ==Odd== | ==Even== |
| Light (if lit) | Green ==Fl 4s or Q== | Red ==Fl 4s or Q== |
| Retroreflective material | Green | Red |

Only lateral marks are numbered (they may also carry letters). Numbers ==increase in the upstream direction==. Because
the two sides are not always the same length, numbers are ==omitted where needed== so that an odd number on the left
sits roughly opposite the even number on the right.

### Worked example: reading a channel

You are entering an invented harbour from the sea. You see, in order: a green can with the number 3 and a green light,
a red cone numbered 4 with a red light, then green 5 and red 6.

1. Entering from sea is upstream, so green is on the port side and red on the starboard side.
2. Odd numbers (3, 5) are green: both are port hand marks. Even numbers (4, 6) are red: starboard hand marks.
3. The numbers go 3, 4, 5, 6 as you go in: this fits the upstream rule.
4. You pass 3 and 5 on your left and 4 and 6 on your right, keeping to the channel between them.

If the numbers were falling as you went in (6, 5, 4, 3), you would suspect you were going downstream, and you should
re-check your chart before trusting colours.

## Bifurcation marks: where the channel splits

A **bifurcation** (or junction) mark shows that the channel divides into two. It looks like a lateral buoy of one
colour with a ==broad band of the other colour==. The main colour is the side of the **preferred channel**.

- ==Port bifurcation==: green with a broad red band, flat-topped, green cylinder topmark. Keep it on your ==port== side to take the preferred channel.
- ==Starboard bifurcation==: red with a broad green band, conical, red cone topmark. Keep it on your ==starboard== side to take the preferred channel.
- Light (if lit): the group-flashing colour of the main body, ==Fl(2+1) 6s or 10s==. That means a group of two flashes, then one flash, then dark. The pattern "two plus one" is unlike any ordinary lateral light.

Read the chart to see which way each branch goes before you choose.

## Fairway (mid-channel) buoys

A **fairway** mark shows safe water all round it. It marks a landfall, the entrance to a channel, or the centre of a
channel.

- Colour: ==red and white vertical stripes== of equal width.
- Shape: ==spherical==; topmark, if fitted, a single ==red sphere==.
- Light: white ==Mo(A) 6s== (Morse letter A, short then long) or white ==LFl 10s== (one long flash every 10 seconds).
- Passing: the Coast Guard says to keep it on your ==port side==, whichever way you are going. Fairway marks carry letters only, never numbers.

## Don't trust one buoy

The Coast Guard cautions mariners:

- Buoy positions on a chart are ==approximate==.
- Aids to navigation can be ==damaged, off position or not working==, from ice, storms, ship strikes or power failure.
- Do not rely on buoys alone: use bearings or angles from fixed marks ashore, soundings or satellite positions where
  possible, and do not go too close to a buoy (its mooring chain or the danger it marks).
- The distance to a light cannot be judged from how bright it looks.

## Exam traps

- Mixing up the colours: in Canada **green is port, red is starboard** when upstream. (Region A countries, for example in Europe, are the opposite.)
- Treating the colours as fixed without asking which way you are going. Going out to sea, the starboard hand aids are on your **left**.
- Forgetting that **odd is green** and **even is red**.
- Thinking a fairway buoy is passed on either side: it is kept to **port**.
- Reading a bifurcation mark's colour band as the preferred side: the **main (larger) colour** is the preferred-channel side.
- Using a buoy as an accurate fix.
