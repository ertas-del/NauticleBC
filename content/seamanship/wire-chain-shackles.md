# Wire rope, chain, shackles and clips

**Study aid only.** Hands-on instruction on a real ship or in a training yard is the only way to learn to rig safely.
Follow your ship's procedures, the manufacturer's markings and your supervisor's orders.

## What you need to know

Wire, chain and shackles are the heavy end of a deck rating's gear. TP 10936 asks you to know wire rope construction,
use, markings, maintenance and stowage, and the fittings that join it together. The exam-sized facts are short:

- ==Wire rope== is made of steel wires twisted into strands, and the strands are laid round a ==core==. The core is
  ==fibre==, a ==wire strand== or an ==independent wire rope core (IWRC)==. The ==IWRC is the most durable==.
- Wire is ==very strong with little stretch==. It needs ==lubrication== and regular checks for ==corrosion== and
  ==kinks==. A ==kink is permanent==.
- A ==bulldog clip== (wire rope clip) goes on with the ==saddle on the live (load-bearing) part== and the ==U-bolt on the
  dead end==. Memory aid: ==never saddle a dead horse==.
- A ==D shackle== (chain shackle) is for a pull in line; a ==bow shackle== (anchor shackle) copes with pulls from
  several directions. Pins are ==moused==.
- A ==Kenter shackle== joins lengths of anchor chain.
- Never exceed the ==safe working load (SWL)==. A ==stage== is hung on ropes with a ==safety factor of at least six==.

## Wire rope

Rope made of wire does what fibre rope cannot: it takes very heavy loads at a small diameter, hardly stretches, and
does not rot. In exchange it is stiff, it can injure hands, and it can be ruined by careless handling.

**Construction.** Thin wires are twisted into a ==strand==; several strands are laid round a ==core==. The core
supports the strands and keeps their shape.

| Core | What it is | Note |
|---|---|---|
| ==Fibre core== (natural or synthetic) | A fibre rope in the middle | Flexible; holds lubricant; crushes more easily (general; check with your instructor) |
| ==Wire strand core== | One more strand of wire in the middle | Stronger, stiffer (general) |
| ==IWRC== | A small wire rope of its own as the core | The ==most durable== in hard service |

Wire rope for heavy deck work is often built with a steel core. Exact constructions (such as 6 x 19 or 6 x 37, meaning
strands by wires per strand) are for your instructor; they were not checked for this lesson.

**Care.**

1. ==Lubricate== it, because oil keeps the inside wires from rusting and from rubbing each other.
2. ==Inspect== it before use for rust, broken wires, flattened or crushed lengths, and ==kinks==.
3. ==Never let it kink==. A kink cannot be straightened out: it is permanent damage and the rope is weaker there.
4. Coil it with the lay, keep it dry and off the deck, and stow it clean (general seamanship; check with your
   instructor).
5. Wear gloves. Broken wires ("meat hooks") cut hands (general seamanship).
6. Avoid ==jerk loads==. A load that is dropped onto a rope can reach about ==three or four times== the steady
   load, and wire does not stretch to absorb it (period figure from a 1921 text; treat as a warning, not a calculation).
7. Remove it from service if it is badly damaged. The scrap limits are in your ship's planned maintenance system
   (general; not checked).

## Wire rope clips (bulldog grips)

A clip turns the end of a wire into an eye or joins two wires. It has a ==U-bolt==, a ==saddle== (the flat, forged
part with the nuts' seat) and two nuts. The saddle supports the wire that carries the load; the U-bolt bites the
tail.

- The saddle goes on the ==live end== (the part that takes the load).
- The ==U-bolt== goes on the ==dead end== (the short tail).
- ==Two or more clips== are used, and the bigger the wire the more clips. As many as eight may be needed for a rope 2
  inches (about 51 mm) across.
- Clip spacing, nut torque and the exact number for each size come from the manufacturer's table and your ship's
  procedure. They are not in this lesson (not checked).
- Re-tighten after the first load, because the wire settles (general seamanship; check with your instructor).

```
   LIVE end ----[saddle]--[saddle]--[saddle]---- looped back, dead end (tail)
                U-bolt over the tail side only
```

"Never saddle a dead horse" is the way to remember it: put the saddle on the live part, never on the dead one.

## Shackles

A ==shackle== is a U-shaped piece of metal closed with a pin. It is the usual way to join a wire, chain, block or lifting
eye to a fitting.

| Type | Shape | Best for | Watch out for |
|---|---|---|---|
| ==D shackle== (chain shackle) | Narrow D | A pull in line with its axis | ==Bends under side load== |
| ==Bow shackle== (anchor shackle) | Wide round bow | Pulls from several directions | Weaker overall than a D shackle of the same size |
| ==Screw-pin shackle== | Pin threaded into one eye | Everyday rigging | Pin can work loose: ==mouse== it |
| Round-pin shackle | Pin held by a cotter pin | Where the pin may rotate | Cotter pin must be fitted |
| ==Snap shackle== | Spring release | Quick, light jobs | ==Can release under load==; less secure |

**Mousing** means tying the pin to the shackle body with a few turns of ==seizing wire== (or marking wire) so that it
cannot unscrew with vibration. A moused pin is checked each time the gear is inspected.

**Joining chain.** Anchor chain is made in lengths that are joined with ==joining shackles==. The usual type is the
==Kenter (lugless) joining shackle==, which has no lugs to snag, so it passes smoothly over the cable lifter. A lugged
D joining shackle is also used. (The lengths themselves are covered in the anchoring lesson.)

**Slips.** A pelican hook or a senhouse slip is a quick-release hook, used for example to hold the bitter end of an anchor
cable so that it can be let go in an emergency. Details vary by ship (check with your instructor).

## Safe working load and safety factor

Every piece of lifting or mooring gear has a ==breaking strength== (the load that will break it) and a much smaller
==safe working load (SWL)==, the most it may be asked to carry in use.

- The SWL is ==marked== on the equipment or on a label fastened to it.
- Gear must ==never be used above its SWL== (Maritime Occupational Health and Safety Regulations; the Ships' Tackle
  Regulations carry the same rule for cargo gear).
- The ratio of breaking strength to the load actually carried is the ==safety factor==. A stage (a working platform hung
  from above) must have ropes and supports with a ==safety factor of at least six==.

### Worked example (invented numbers)

A wire has a breaking load of 60 kN. A safety factor of 6 means the working load must not exceed 60 / 6 = 10 kN.
A supervisor says a staging with two people and tools will put 3.2 kN on each of two ropes. 3.2 kN is below 10 kN, so
the rope is acceptable on strength. If one rope were to carry the full 6.4 kN after the other failed, it would still
be below 10 kN. (The numbers are made up to show the method.)

## Exam traps

- Putting the **saddle on the dead end**. The saddle belongs on the live part.
- Thinking that **more stretch** is a wire rope virtue. Wire has little stretch; nylon is the stretchy one.
- Believing a **kink can be pulled out**. It is permanent.
- Using a **D shackle** for a sideways pull, or a **snap shackle** for a heavy hoist.
- Forgetting to **mouse** a screw pin.
- Mixing up **SWL** (the allowed load) and **breaking strength** (the failure load). SWL is far lower.
- Saying a stage needs a safety factor of **four**. Four is the scaffold figure (supports four times the likely load);
  a **stage** needs **six**.
