# Vessel types and who does what on board

**Study aid only.** Crew titles and duties vary between companies and between ocean-going and coastal vessels. Check
the actual job and the regulations for your vessel.

## What you need to know

- The Bridge Watch Rating course standard (TP 10936) lists ==ten== vessel types: ==passenger, container, ro-ro,
  tanker, bulk carrier (self-unloader), general cargo, tug, oilfield service, special purpose, fishing==.
- The ==master== commands; the ==chief mate== runs the deck department; the ==second mate== looks after
  ==navigation equipment and publications==; ==third mates== stand watches and navigate.
- A ==Bridge Watch Rating== is a ==rating== (not an officer) qualified to ==keep a look-out and steer==.

## Vessel types

The type of a ship is named from what it carries or what it does. The wording below is plain-language, general
seamanship. The names themselves are the ones in the course standard.

| Type | In plain words |
|---|---|
| ==Passenger== | Carries people: ferries, cruise ships, small passenger vessels and water taxis |
| ==Container== | Carries standard steel boxes stacked on deck and in holds, handled by shore cranes |
| ==Ro-ro== (roll-on, roll-off) | Vehicles drive on and off over a ramp (car carriers, vehicle ferries) |
| ==Tanker== | Carries liquid cargo in tanks (oil, chemicals, liquefied gas) |
| ==Bulk carrier== | Carries loose dry cargo such as grain, coal or ore; a **self-unloader** has its own conveyor to discharge itself |
| ==General cargo== | Carries mixed or packaged cargo in holds, often with its own cranes or derricks |
| ==Tug== | A small powerful vessel that pushes or tows other vessels or barges |
| ==Oilfield service== | Supports offshore oil and gas work (supply, standby, anchor handling) |
| ==Special purpose== | Built for a particular job such as research, survey, dredging or cable laying |
| ==Fishing== | Catches or processes fish |

Why the type matters: it changes the cargo hazards, the deck equipment, the number of crew, the rules that apply and
the way the ship is handled. A tug and tow, for example, has different lights and manoeuvring from a container ship.

## The departments

A merchant ship is run in departments, each with its own head:

- **Deck department**: navigates the ship, keeps watch, handles mooring, anchoring, cargo and deck maintenance.
  It is divided into ==deck officers and ratings==.
- **Engine department**: runs and maintains the machinery.
- **Catering and hotel staff**: on ships that have them, feed and look after the crew (and passengers).

The ==master== holds overall command and is responsible for the ship, the people and the cargo.

## The deck officers

| Rank | Main job |
|---|---|
| ==Master== (captain) | Overall command; final authority on board |
| ==Chief mate== (first officer) | Head of the deck department, reports to the master; plans and supervises work, quality, coordination with other departments; often in charge of cargo and stability |
| ==Second mate== | Looks after ==charts and navigational publications==, the ==gyrocompass== and navigation equipment, and keeps voyage logs |
| ==Third mate== | Handles navigation and ==pilotage in coastal waters==, stands a navigational watch |

(The last words on the chief mate's cargo duties and the third mate's other tasks are general practice; check the
company's structure.)

## Ratings

**Ratings** are the crew members who are not officers. On deck they include:

- **Able Seafarer (AS)**: an experienced deck rating, qualified for the full range of deck work.
- **Bridge Watch Rating (BWR)**: a rating qualified to take part in the navigational watch, usually as look-out and
  helmsman, working under the officer of the watch.
- **Ordinary seaman** or deck hand: entry-level deck rating.
- **Boatswain (bosun)**: the senior deck rating, who supervises the other ratings (title and duties vary).

### Where the Bridge Watch Rating fits

Under the Marine Personnel Regulations the ==Bridge Watch Rating is a certificate of competency==. The regulations
require that on a vessel of ==500 GT or more== an additional person on the deck watch hold an ==Able Seafarer or
Bridge Watch Rating== certificate. So a BWR is exactly the kind of rating the rules expect on the bridge at that size.
A BWR is not an officer: the **officer of the watch** remains responsible for the safe navigation of the ship.

### Worked example: a typical bridge watch

On an invented 5000 GT cargo ship the 4-to-8 watch is the third mate (officer of the watch) and an AB or BWR who
steers or keeps look-out. The master is in his cabin and is called if the third mate has any doubt. The second mate
has been correcting the chart folio and the publications in the chartroom before the watch.

Questions to ask: Who is in charge of the watch? (the third mate.) Who looks after charts? (second mate.) Who is the
rating? (the AB or BWR.)

## Exam traps

- Putting the **second mate** in charge of cargo. He looks after **charts, publications and navigation equipment**.
- Believing the chief mate commands the ship. The **master** commands; the chief mate heads the **deck department**.
- Thinking a Bridge Watch Rating is an officer certificate. It is a **rating's** certificate of competency.
- Mixing up **ro-ro** (vehicles on a ramp) with **container** (boxes lifted by crane).
- Forgetting that a **self-unloader** is a type of bulk carrier.
