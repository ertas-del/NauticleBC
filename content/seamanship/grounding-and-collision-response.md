# Grounding and collision: what the master does

**Study aid only.** This lesson is the practical side. The legal duties after a collision and to answer distress are in
`canadian-regulations.distress-assistance-collision`; the Rules that try to prevent both are in the COLREGs module.

## What you need to know

A grounding or collision is both a safety emergency and a legal event. The master has three jobs at once: look after the
people, find out what is wrong with the hull, and make the right calls and reports. The master's general duty is in the
Canada Shipping Act, 2001, ==s. 109==: take all reasonable steps to ensure the safety of the vessel and of persons on board.

## Grounding

**Immediately.** Stop the engine or take the way off, keep calm and assess. A page from the BoatUS Foundation puts the
first steps this way: work out what you hit and how deeply the vessel is bedded; check for ==injuries, leaks and damage==;
and decide whether it is a ==soft grounding== (you can probably free yourself, usually with little damage) or a ==hard
grounding== (rock, reef or piling), which can be far more serious. If you find the hull holed, use the flooding procedure
in `seamanship.flooding-and-damage-control`.

**If hard aground.** ==Stay with the vessel==, ==put on lifejackets== and ==call for help==. If she may sink or lives are
at risk, call the ==Coast Guard on VHF channel 16==. Stop leaks where you can.

**Refloating.** The BoatUS page lists these in order:

1. ==Set an anchor out as far as possible== from the vessel (use the tender if you have one), so she is not driven further
   aground.
2. ==Check the tide tables for the next high tide==; waiting for high water is safe, if slow, and especially so if the hull is
   damaged.
3. ==Kedge off==: heave on the anchor to pull her toward it.
4. Watch for waves, tide and wakes from other vessels which may lift her.
5. ==Back off carefully, if appropriate==. Check the bottom first: ==prop wash astern can push sand or mud toward the bow== and
   bed her more firmly. Have someone push sideways if possible and ==shut the engine down if the temperature rises quickly==.
6. If still stuck, call for professional help.

The same page notes that the Coast Guard generally will not tow you unless you are in immediate danger, and it warns
against backing off over coral or sea grass. Use the tide, not power, as your first tool (see the tides and chartwork modules).

**Before you pull her free.** The Coast Guard's 1993 guidelines for rescue boats say that when refloating another vessel
you must ==make sure she will not sink once free==. The same applies to your own: it is possible (general seamanship, not from the sources) that the ground is
plugging a hole, so check the hull and bilges before you let her off.

## Collision

Section ==148== of the Canada Shipping Act, 2001 says that if vessels collide, the master of each ==must==, so far as they
can do it without endangering their own vessel, crew or passengers:

- ==render the other vessel, its master, crew and passengers the assistance necessary to save them from danger caused by
  the collision==, and ==stay by the other vessel until satisfied it needs no further assistance==; and
- ==give the name of their vessel, the name and address of its authorized representative== and any other prescribed
  information to the master of the other vessel.

First check your own people and your own hull (flooding, steering, fire), then your duty to the other vessel. If either
vessel is in danger, the distress rules apply (s. 131; see the regulations lesson).

## What is reportable

Under the Transportation Safety Board Regulations ==s. 3(1)(d)== the master, operator and crew with direct knowledge of a
vessel other than a pleasure craft must report:

- a ==collision or a risk of a collision==;
- going ==aground==;
- ==unforeseen contact with the bottom without going aground==;
- being ==anchored, grounded or beached to avoid an occurrence==;
- ==damage that affects seaworthiness== or renders the ship unfit for its purpose;
- also sinking, fire, and total machinery or steering failure (other lessons).

The report goes in ==as soon as possible by the quickest means==, with the remainder ==within 30 days==. Note "risk of a
collision" and "unforeseen contact with the bottom": a near miss and a bump that did not stop you are both reportable.

### Aids to navigation and hazards

CSA ==s. 129(1)==: if a vessel, or anything it tows, ==runs down, moves, damages or destroys an aid to navigation==, the
person in charge must report ==without delay== to a ==marine communications and traffic services officer==, or, if that is
not feasible, to a ==Canadian Coast Guard officer==. ==Section 129(2)== requires the same report when the person in charge
discovers an ==uncharted hazard== or an aid that is ==missing, out of position or malfunctioning==.

## A BC example

An invented example. A 9 m vessel feeling her way into a narrow anchorage on the west coast of Vancouver Island at near-low
tide touches a rock shelf and stops. The skipper stops the engine, checks the three people on board and the bilges, and
finds no leak. The tide is rising, so she sets the kedge from the tender, waits for the water to lift her and backs off
gently once she is afloat, checking the engine temperature. Because the unforeseen contact is a reportable occurrence she
later sends the TSB report. She also reports the shoal as an uncharted hazard (s. 129(2)).

## Common mistakes

- **Backing off at once** without checking what you hit or the bottom behind you.
- Forgetting the **tide**: a falling tide can leave you stranded and a rising one will lift you.
- Failing to **stay by** the other vessel after a collision, or not giving the **authorized representative's** name and
  address.
- Thinking a **bump that did not stop you** or a **near miss** need no report.
- Pulling a vessel off a rock without checking if the **rock is plugging a hole**.
- Not reporting damage to a **buoy or beacon**.
