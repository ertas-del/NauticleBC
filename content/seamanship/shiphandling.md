# Handling a small vessel: propeller, rudder, pivot point, wind and current

**Study aid only.** You learn to handle a boat by handling a boat, with an experienced person beside you. This lesson
gives the vocabulary and the physics so the oral makes sense. Several statements here are general seamanship taken from
non-government pages, and one source disagreed with another; see the notes marked **check**.

## What you need to know

The Master, Limited oral is held in person with an examiner and may be held on board with manoeuvres (see
`sixty-ton.oral-exam-guide`). Shiphandling is one of the reported topics. For a small vessel with one propeller
and one rudder, five things decide what happens in the last few boat-lengths before a berth: ==propeller walk==, the
==rudder==, the ==pivot point==, ==wind== and ==current==.

## Propeller walk

A turning propeller pushes the stern sideways as well as pushing the vessel ahead or astern. This is
==propeller walk==, also called transverse thrust. A page by Sail Magazine explains it by the shaft angle: the blades
move more water on one side of the arc than the other, and the water thrown against the hull adds to it.

- It is ==strongest going astern==, at ==slow speed== and at ==high revolutions==. Going ahead it is much less noticeable, because the
  rudder easily compensates.
- With a ==right-hand propeller== (one that turns clockwise when seen from astern while going ahead) the stern
  ==walks to port when going astern==. A ==left-hand== propeller does the opposite. The bow therefore swings the other way.
- Larger, higher-pitch and three-blade propellers walk more; a saildrive, whose shaft is parallel to the surface, shows none.

**Check:** one study page read for this lesson said the stern of a right-hand single screw walks to starboard in reverse.
The Sail Magazine page and normal teaching say port. The lesson uses port; confirm it with your instructor and by trying
it on your own vessel, because the direction depends on which way your propeller turns.

Use it (general seamanship, **check** with your instructor): if the berth is on the side the stern walks to when
you back, a short burst astern swings the stern toward the berth.

## The rudder

A rudder steers because the water flows past it. The US Navy's *Seaman* training manual notes that a ship ==turns
faster and responds sooner at higher speed== and ==needs more rudder angle at low speed==. A small vessel creeping up
to a berth has little water flowing over the rudder, so its steering is slow.

The study page on boat handling adds that astern the vessel steers poorly until propeller walk and the stream take
effect, so in reverse the walk, not the helm, steers the stern at first.

## The pivot point

Think of a vessel as a board with a pin in it. When she makes headway the pin, the ==pivot point==, is about
==one-third of her length from the bow==. The bow swings one way and the stern swings the other about that point. This is
why the stern swings out when you turn toward a dock, and why you can hit a pile with the stern while the bow is clear.
When she goes astern the pivot point moves ==aft of midships==; when she is stopped it sits ==near midships==.

## Wind and current

Wind pushes on the part of the vessel above the water; current pushes on the part below. Neither pushes the vessel
about the pivot point in the same way as the propeller does.

- ==Wind can cancel or add to propeller walk==, depending on its direction and strength. Look at the wind before you pick
  the side to berth.
- A ==flood (rising tide) current can set a vessel down the face of a pier==, so you cannot rely on reverse alone to stop her.
- It is general seamanship, not from a source read, that you usually approach into the stronger of wind and current,
  because you then keep steerage with the least speed over the ground (**check** with your instructor).

## A safe approach

1. Look at wind, current, other traffic and the berth before you commit. Brief the crew on lines and fenders.
2. ==Approach at a shallow angle== with ==minimum headway== that still gives steerage. A steep angle at speed leaves
   no room to correct.
3. ==Plan the astern move early==. Reverse gives little steering until prop walk and the stream take effect, and a
   crowded fairway leaves no room for a late decision.
4. Use a spring or the bow line to check headway and turn the stern in (see `seamanship.mooring-operations`).
5. ==Allow for passenger load==: it can change how the vessel responds.

The Sail Magazine page gives a worked docking example for a sailboat (about 1 to 1.5 knots, 30 to 40 degrees to
the dock, aiming a third of a boat length aft of the target for the bow). Treat such numbers as one skipper's method, not
a rule, and practise in open water first.

## Handling in an emergency

In a person-overboard situation the skipper turns the wheel ==toward the side the person fell from==. That swings the
stern, and the propeller, ==away from the person==. Then approach slowly under power, head into the wind, and stop the
engine (or pull the kill cord) when the person is within reach, as in the Transport Canada template (see
`seamanship.man-overboard-recovery`).

## A BC example

An invented example. A 10 m single-screw vessel with a right-hand propeller must berth port side to a float at Ucluelet
on a rising tide with a light wind on the starboard bow. The skipper wants the stern to walk toward the float when she
backs, so port side to is right. She approaches at about 30 degrees with only steerage speed, puts the helm over
with a short ahead burst to bring the bow round, then gives a short astern burst. The stern walks in and the flood
sets her gently toward the float. A forward spring on the cleat holds her there.

## Common mistakes

- Saying ==propeller walk is strongest ahead==; it is strongest ==astern at slow speed==.
- Mixing up ==right-hand and left-hand==, or which way the stern walks (**right-hand astern: stern to port**).
- Putting the pivot point at the bow or at midships when the vessel is making headway.
- Approaching a berth too fast and at too steep an angle.
- Relying on ==reverse alone== in a strong current.
- Forgetting that wind can ==cancel or add to== propeller walk.
