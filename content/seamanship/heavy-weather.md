# Heavy weather and following seas

**Study aid only.** Heavy weather is where seamanship, stability and weather forecasting meet. Read the forecast
lessons first; this lesson is about what you do with the vessel when the forecast was right, or wrong.

## What you need to know

The best heavy-weather decision is made ashore: the forecast, the vessel's limits, a place of refuge and a sail plan
(TP 14070 lists all four in its voyage planning chapter). The second best is made early, with the crew briefed and the
vessel secure. The hardest is made in a following sea, where the vessel runs along with the waves and the danger
comes from behind, out of the helmsman's sight.

The law has two points. The master may not operate a passenger-carrying vessel in circumstances that ==exceed its
design limitations== (Small Vessel Regulations ==s. 404(2)==), and the master must warn others of certain dangers
(Canada Shipping Act, 2001 ==s. 112==, below).

## The master's duty to warn: CSA s. 112

If the master of a Canadian vessel meets any of these, the master must give notice to ==all vessels in the vicinity and
the prescribed authorities on shore==:

- dangerous ice, a dangerous derelict or other direct danger to navigation;
- a tropical storm;
- ==winds of force 10 or more== on the Beaufort scale for which ==no storm warning== has been received;
- ==subfreezing air temperatures with gale-force winds== causing ==severe ice accretion== on the superstructure.

In practice this is done by radio through the Marine Communications and Traffic Services centre (general; see the
radio module for the calls). Failing to give the notice is an offence under s. 121(1)(k).

## Preparing before it arrives

The Canadian Coast Guard's guidelines for its own search and rescue boats (TP 11637, 1993, so a concept source, not
a rule) give a heavy-weather checklist that fits any small vessel:

1. ==Secure loose gear and openings== on deck and below.
2. Rig ==lifelines and harnesses==; crew wear them on deck.
3. Dress the crew in ==protective clothing== (the manual adds helmets at the coxswain's discretion).
4. Check the ==engine room and fuel==.
5. Prepare the ==charts== and the plan for shelter.
6. ==Brief the crew==.

TP 14070 adds that the plan should include a place of refuge you can reach without GPS, and a responsible person ashore
who knows your plans.

## Following and quartering seas

When waves come from astern or the quarter, the vessel is carried along with them. The Food and Agriculture
Organization's guide to small-vessel stability warns that ==stability can be considerably reduced== when the vessel
travels at about ==the same speed and direction as the waves==. The stability lessons
explain the righting lever behind this (`stability.righting-lever-gz-curve`).

What can happen, from the Coast Guard manual:

- ==Broaching==: a steep overtaking wave can swing the vessel broadside to the sea (general definition), and she may capsize.
- ==Pitchpoling==: in extreme cases the bow dives under the wave in front of the vessel.

**What to do.** If the vessel starts to heel or yaw excessively, ==slow down or change course== (FAO). Coast Guard
advice for its own boats: when a large wave is about to overtake, ==reduce speed sharply and point the bow into the
wave== and avoid carrying speed on the front face of a big wave, where control is lost. When running in, ==match the speed
of the waves==; ==do not try to overtake the wave ahead== and ==do not let a breaker overtake you==. Slowing down, or
using a sea anchor, helps avoid broaching. These are cautious rules of thumb for very different vessels; exactly what
works on your own boat is a matter for your training and your own trials.

### What raises the risk

The Coast Guard manual lists conditions that raise capsize risk: ==shallow water==, ==a falling tide==, ==a strong
opposing current==, ==towing==, ==darkness==, ==low fuel== and ==water taken aboard==. Note how many of these are about
the sea state meeting something else: a bar, a pass or a river mouth with the ebb running against a swell.

## Water on deck

Water that comes aboard must go overboard fast, or it acts as a heavy free surface and cuts stability.

- Keep ==freeing ports and scuppers clear==. Do not lock freeing port covers shut; if locks are fitted, the release
  must be easy to reach (FAO).
- Keep ==slack tanks to a minimum== (free surface; see `stability.free-surface`).
- Check hatches and openings are weathertight before you cross a bar or pass.
- On a tug, SVR ==s. 521(e) and (f)== also require a decked vessel to drain water rapidly overboard and an open vessel to
  have positive buoyancy when swamped or a gunwale high enough to resist swamping.

### Ice

In subfreezing gale conditions, ice forms on the rigging and superstructure, raising weight high up. FAO advice: clear
ice from freeing ports and scuppers as soon as it forms, and if ice causes a list, ==remove it from the lower side
first==. See `stability.icing`.

## A BC example

An invented example. A 12 m charter vessel returns toward Port Hardy from the outer coast with a southwest swell building
behind her. The skipper has checked that hatches are closed and scuppers clear and has briefed the two crew. As the
seas steepen and the stern starts to yaw, she slows so the swells pass under her instead of surging beside the hull. The
skipper tells the passengers to stay seated and wear lifejackets. Later, a drifting log field makes her slow further. She
reports the logs to the Coast Guard by radio, which is her duty to warn others.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking a **following sea** is the safe sea. It reduces stability and is the one most likely to **broach** you.
- Overtaking a wave to "get home faster": ==match the wave speed==, do not overtake it.
- Locking ==freeing port covers== shut to keep water out.
- Clearing ice from the **high** side first; clear the **lower** side first if there is a list.
- Forgetting the duty in ==s. 112== to warn others about winds of Beaufort 10 with no warning received.
- Leaving the ==checklist== until the first big wave arrives.
