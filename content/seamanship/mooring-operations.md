# Mooring lines and berthing operations

**Study aid only.** Mooring kills and maims more deck crew than almost any other routine job. Learn the terms here,
then learn the work from a qualified bosun or mate on the ship you join. Your master's and officer's orders and your
company's mooring plan come first.

## What you need to know

TP 10936 asks you to know the ==function of the mooring lines==, how each works as part of the whole system, the order of events
for making fast and letting go, and the ==SWL and breaking strength== of lines, winches, windlasses, capstans, bitts,
chocks and bollards. In practice an examiner will ask three things: ==what each line is called and does==, ==what the
words mean==, and ==where you stand==.

- The six lines: ==head line==, ==forward breast line==, ==forward spring==, ==aft spring==, ==aft breast line==,
  ==stern line==.
- Never stand in a ==bight== or in the ==snap-back zone==. ==Nylon== stores energy and can ==snap back==.
- Use a ==stopper== to take the load off a line while you move it.

## The six lines

Picture a ship alongside a wharf, bow to the left of the page:

```
   bow                                          stern
   head line   fwd breast       aft breast   stern line
        \         |                 |           /
   =====[==================SHIP==================]=====  wharf
        /   fwd spring ->     <- aft spring       \
```

| Line | Where it leads | What it mainly does |
|---|---|---|
| ==Head line== | From the bow, well forward to the wharf | Holds the bow and stops the ship moving astern (general) |
| ==Forward breast line== | From the bow, nearly square to the ship | ==Holds the bow in== to the berth |
| ==Forward spring== | From the bow (or forward part), leading aft | ==Stops the ship moving ahead== |
| ==Aft spring== | From the stern (or after part), leading forward | ==Stops the ship moving astern== |
| ==Aft breast line== | From the stern, nearly square to the ship | ==Holds the stern in== to the berth |
| ==Stern line== | From the stern, well aft to the wharf | Holds the stern and stops the ship moving ahead (general) |

A good way to remember it: ==breasts hold her in, springs stop her sliding fore and aft, head and stern lines hold the ends==.
The simple geometry is that a line resists movement along the direction it pulls. The reasoning behind each line's
job above is derived from this geometry; a source for the exact wording used by Transport Canada examiners was not
found, so check the names and jobs against your course notes and ship's mooring plan.

**Why the springs matter.** Wind and current push a ship along the berth as well as onto or off it. Two springs set
against each other keep her from surging, and the breasts keep her close. Tide and loading change the angle of every
line, so lines are ==tended== (slackened or hove in) as the ship rises, falls or loads. A ship that is left on tight
lines while the tide drops can hang from them (general seamanship).

**Tug lines** are towing lines used when a tug assists berthing; they are handled as the master or pilot directs and
are part of the same system (general; not checked). The ==order of events== for making fast and letting go (heaving
lines out, messengers, eyes over bollards, slack taken in, all fast) is learned on the ship you serve on.

## Fittings

- ==Bitts== are posts, usually in pairs, on the deck; a line is led round them in a figure of eight and held by its
  friction. Bitts take a line ==quickly== and let it go ==quickly==.
- ==Bollards== are the strong posts on the wharf (shore side), together with ==rings== and ==cleats==, where the eyes
  of the ship's lines go.
- A ==fairlead== or ==chock== guides a line through the bulwark without chafe. A ==warping drum== or ==winch==
  takes the strain when lines are hove in. A ==capstan== (vertical) or ==windlass== (horizontal) does the same on
  some ships (see the deck machinery lesson).
- Each has a ==safe working load== marked or known. Never load any of them beyond it.

## Line-handling words

Orders on deck are short and are ==repeated back== so that both sides know what is happening. The exact words differ
between companies; learn yours. These are traditional meanings (general seamanship; check with your instructor):

| Word | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ==Stand by== | Be ready; do nothing yet |
| ==Heave== / ==heave away== | Haul in the line (by winch, capstan or by hand) |
| ==Hold== / ==hold on== | Keep the strain; do not let more line go |
| ==Check== | Ease the line round the bitts, letting it render slowly under control |
| ==Slack away== / ==ease== | Let line out |
| ==Avast== | Stop (stop heaving) |
| ==Belay== | Make fast the line on the bitts or cleat (also used to mean "stop that order") |
| ==Single up== | Reduce to one line at each position before leaving |
| ==Let go== / ==cast off== | Release the line from the shore fitting |

Always ==keep clear of the bight==, keep your feet away from the turns on a drum, and keep hands and clothing out
of the coils. Never turn your back on a line under strain.

## Safe practice and snap-back

A mooring line under load is a long, stretched spring. If it parts, it can fly back along its own path.

- ==Nylon== stretches a lot (up to 40 per cent) and stores energy. A ==highly stressed nylon line can part
  catastrophically== and cause ==snap-back==, which can ==fatally injure== bystanders. The effect is like stretching a
  rubber band to its limit and being hit by its broken ends.
- Stay out of the ==snap-back zone== (the area in line with a line under strain, particularly just beyond a fairlead,
  bitts or drum) and out of any ==bight== (a loop or curve of line that could close or straighten).
- ==Reduce the load at once== if the line or its fittings show excessive strain.
- Use ==chafe gear== and check lines for wear (general).
- Mooring lines and fittings must be kept within their ==SWL==.

**Taking the load off with a stopper.** A ==stopper== is a short line (or chain) made fast to the mooring line with a
==rolling hitch==, which grips the line and does not slip in the direction of pull. The stopper's tail is made off on
a bitt or cleat. The load now sits on the stopper, and the main line can be eased off the drum, shifted to the bitts
or renewed.

**If a line parts.** Shout a warning, stay clear of the bight and the line's path, make sure nobody is hurt, and report to
the officer in charge. The watch then puts out replacement lines (general seamanship; MCA guidance covers the zone
but not a full procedure).

## Leaving the berth

Before the ship leaves, the Navigation Safety Regulations tie the deck's work to two bridge duties:

- A ==VHF continuous listening watch== begins ==15 minutes before the vessel is underway==, and ends when the vessel is
  securely anchored, moored or made fast and is not a hazard to other vessels.
- A vessel listed in column 1 of ==Schedule 4== of the Regulations makes a ==navigation safety call== when it will
  depart from a berth: ==15 minutes before== and again ==immediately before== departure.

The deck crew's part is to be ready on time and to repeat the orders for ==single up==, ==slack away== and ==let go== exactly.

### Worked example (invented numbers)

A ship will leave a berth at 14:30. The bridge must start its continuous listening watch no later than 14:30 minus 15 minutes
= 14:15. If the ship is in Schedule 4, the first navigation safety call is due at 14:15 and the second immediately
before she leaves. A deck party told "stand by singling up at 14:20" must be at stations by then.

## Exam traps

- Swapping **forward spring** and **aft spring**: the forward spring stops her going **ahead**; the aft spring stops her going **astern**.
- Saying **breast lines** stop fore-and-aft movement. They hold the ship **in** to the berth.
- Standing in the **bight** "just for a second" while a line comes taut.
- Believing nylon is safe because it is stretchy. It stores energy and is the snap-back danger.
- Taking a heavily loaded line straight off the drum. Use a **stopper**.
- Confusing **bitts** (ship's deck) with **bollards** (shore).
