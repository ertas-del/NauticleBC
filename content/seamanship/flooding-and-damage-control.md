# Flooding and damage control

**Study aid only.** Flooding is the emergency where seconds and a cool head count most. Learn the order below, know where
your vessel's seacocks, pumps and plugs are, and practise with your crew.

## What you need to know

Water coming into a small vessel does three harm at once: it adds weight and lowers freeboard, it can move about and cut
stability (free surface), and it can reach the machinery and electrics. The cure is the same list every time: ==find it,
stop it, pump it, tell someone, prepare to leave==. The only question is the order, and Transport Canada's template
gives it.

## The Transport Canada procedure

The template for "taking on water" on small vessels runs like this:

1. ==Call out the location==: "Water in or on the [place]". Everyone knows where.
2. ==Start the pumps== or have someone begin ==bailing==.
3. ==Find where the water is entering==.
4. If the vessel is at risk, ==notify authorities and nearby vessels== of the situation and position.
5. ==Stop or slow the leak==. If you cannot bring it under control, ==consider grounding the vessel==.
6. If ==stability is threatened==, ==prepare to abandon ship==.
7. ==Keep authorities informed==.
8. ==Complete an incident report== and notify the company and authorities as necessary.

The point of steps 2 and 3 coming first is that the pumps buy time while you look.

## Finding and stopping the leak

A boating-course page (BOATsmart!, a Canadian site) adds practical advice:

- First decide whether people and the vessel are in danger.
- ==If the vessel is moving, stop her completely==: this ==reduces water pressure on the hull== and slows the inflow. Turn on
  the bilge pump and find the source.
- ==Stop the leak== with ==tapered wooden plugs==, a ==hull patch kit==, or towels, rags or other malleable material, and
  carry these as a standing kit.
- ==Remove the water== with a bailer, manual pump or the bilge system.

A second page, from Boating Magazine, adds that a burst hose can be stopped by ==closing its seacock==, and that moving
crew and gear to the side opposite the hole raises it and slows the inflow. Treat the second idea with care: moving weight
changes stability, and water on board adds a free surface. Check the effect with your instructor and the stability lessons.

## Stability: why water is dangerous

Water sloshing in a compartment is a free surface: it moves to the low side as the vessel heels and cuts the righting
effect (see `stability.free-surface`). The more water on board, the lower the freeboard, and the sooner the deck edge goes
under (`stability.watertight-integrity-and-freeboard`). That is why the procedure says to prepare to abandon if stability
is threatened, not to wait.

## Run for the beach?

The template says to ==consider grounding the vessel== if the flooding cannot be controlled, and Boating Magazine puts it
more bluntly: ==intentionally grounding on a bar or beach may beat sinking==, as long as you avoid ==jagged rocks and high
surf==. This is a master's judgement: a grounded vessel is easier to find and to leave than a sunk one, but a hard grounding
on rock will make the damage worse. It is also a reason to know your local shoreline before you need it.

## When she may sink

If sinking cannot be stopped, the BOATsmart! and Boating Magazine advice, and the template, line up:

- ==Lifejackets on== for everyone, and ==account for everyone== (see `seamanship.abandon-ship-cold-water`).
- ==Call for help==: a MAYDAY on VHF channel 16 giving ==position, persons on board and the nature of the distress==.
  See `radio.distress-urgency-safety`.
- Look for nearby vessels and prepare the life raft.
- Whether to stay with the vessel is a judgement. Boating Magazine says to stay with a boat that floats when capsized, because it is easier to see and keeps you out of the water; BOATsmart! says to swim to safety if sinking cannot be stopped. Decide on whether the hull will float and whether a life raft is ready.

## What the regulations make you carry

- ==SVR s. 602==: every vessel needs the means to ==shut off underwater penetrations== (wet exhausts excepted).
- ==SVR s. 609==: every watertight compartment needs a ==means of pumping or access for bailing==.
- ==SVR s. 412== (passenger-carrying vessels): up to 9 m a ==bailer or manual bilge pump==; over 9 m a ==manual bilge
  pump==.
- TP 14070 adds a 0.91 L/s pump capacity and an 84 dB high-bilge-level alarm for some vessels; check the guide for which.

## Reporting

A ship other than a pleasure craft that ==sinks, founders or capsizes==, or that ==sustains damage that affects its
seaworthiness or renders it unfit for its purpose==, is a reportable occurrence under TSB Regulations ==s. 3(1)(d)==. The
available facts go to the Board ==as soon as possible by the quickest means==; the rest ==within 30 days==.

## A BC example

An invented example. A 10 m charter vessel strikes a submerged log near Nanoose Bay. The deckhand finds water over the cabin
sole and shouts "Water in the forward cabin." The skipper slows the vessel, a deckhand starts the bilge pump, and another finds a split in the hull planking near the bow and packs it with a plug and rags. The skipper keeps
passengers to the open deck in lifejackets and calls the Coast Guard to report. The inflow slows enough for the pump to
win, and she returns to Nanoose at low speed, then files the incident report and a TSB report.

## Common mistakes

- Looking for the hole while the water is rising and **no one is pumping**.
- Not calling out the **location** of the water.
- Forgetting a **seacock** can be closed on a burst hose.
- Running **hard aground on rock** as a way to save the vessel.
- Leaving the **decision to abandon** until the vessel has no stability left.
- Forgetting to report **damage that affects seaworthiness** to the TSB.
