# Ship terms, directions and structure

**Study aid only.** Terms vary a little between ship types and companies. Check your course glossary.

## What you need to know

- ==Fore== and ==aft==: toward the bow and toward the stern. ==Abaft== means "behind some object".
- ==Amidships== is the middle; ==athwartships== means across the ship at ==right angles== to the centreline.
- ==Inboard== is toward the centreline; ==outboard== is ==outside the hull== or away from the centreline.
- ==Watertight== keeps water out under pressure from ==either side==. ==Weathertight== keeps water out in ==any sea
  conditions== but only needs to resist pressure from ==outside==.

## Directions and positions

Picture a ship seen from above with a line down the middle from bow to stern. That line is the **centreline**.

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ==Bow== (forward, fore) | The front end of the ship |
| ==Stern== (aft) | The after-most (back) part of the ship |
| ==Port== | The left side looking forward |
| ==Starboard== | The right side looking forward |
| ==Amidships== | Halfway along the length, or between the two sides |
| ==Abaft== | Toward the stern, relative to something: "abaft the beam" is behind the widest part |
| ==Athwartships== | Right angles to the centreline, from side to side |
| ==Inboard== | Toward the centreline, inside the ship |
| ==Outboard== | Outside the hull, or away from the centreline |
| ==Beam== | The width of the vessel at its widest point |

"Port" and "starboard" never change, however you turn. Left and right do. That is why every seaman uses port and
starboard in orders and reports.

### Example: using the words

"The boat is stowed on the starboard side, abaft the funnel, outboard of the rail." Translation: on the right side of
the ship looking forward, toward the stern from the funnel, outside the guard rail.

## Hull and structure

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Hull | The body of the ship below and including the main deck |
| ==Deck== | A floor of the ship; the ==main deck== is the highest continuous deck |
| ==Bulwark== | A solid plate wall around the edge of the weather deck that stops water and people going over |
| ==Freeing ports== | Openings in the bulwark that let water that has come aboard drain off the deck |
| ==Scuppers== | Small openings or drains at the deck edge that let water run overboard |
| ==Hatch== | An opening in a deck with a cover, used for cargo or access |
| ==Tween deck== | A deck between the main deck and the bottom of a cargo hold ("between decks") |
| Forecastle | A raised structure or deck at the bow |
| Accommodation | The part of the ship where the crew live |
| ==Rudder== | The movable blade at the stern that turns the ship |
| ==Propeller== | The device that drives the ship through the water |
| ==Draft== | The depth of the ship below the waterline, read on the ==draft marks== on the bow, stern and amidships |
| ==Freeboard== | The height of the deck above the waterline |
| ==Load line== | The mark that shows the deepest a ship may be loaded |
| ==Porthole== | A small round window in the hull or superstructure |

(These definitions are general seamanship. Most are not individually sourced in the references used for this
lesson, so check them against your course notes.)

## Mooring and handling gear

| Term | What it does |
|---|---|
| ==Bitts== | A post or pair of posts on deck for ==making lines fast== |
| ==Bollard== | A strong ==vertical post== (on a quay or deck) to which mooring lines are made fast |
| ==Fairlead== | A fitting that guides a line in the right direction and reduces chafe |
| ==Chock== | An open or closed fitting at the deck edge through which mooring lines pass |
| ==Windlass== | A machine for handling the anchor cable (and often mooring lines) at the bow |
| ==Capstan== | A vertical-drum machine for hauling in lines or cable |
| ==Winch== | A machine with a horizontal drum for hauling or paying out rope or wire |
| ==Derrick== | A boom (pole) with rigging used to lift cargo |
| ==Crane== | A pillar-mounted lifting machine that turns |
| ==Block== | A pulley wheel in a housing, used with rope for lifting or changing the lead |
| ==Gangway== | A passage by which people go on and off the ship |
| ==Accommodation ladder== | A ==portable flight of steps== down the ship's side |
| ==Landing boom== | A boom on the side of the ship (used with the gangway or to hold boats off) |
| ==Valves and pumps== | Valves control flow in pipes; pumps move liquids (bilge, ballast, fuel) |

## Watertight and weathertight

These two words describe doors, hatches and other closing fittings.

- ==Weathertight==: in ==any sea conditions water will not penetrate into the ship== (the Load Lines convention
  wording). A weathertight fitting only has to resist pressure from the ==outside==. Typical: a door in a deckhouse
  that leads to the accommodation.
- ==Watertight==: must resist water pressure from ==both sides==. Typical: a door in a bulkhead that divides the
  ship below the waterline. Both kinds need to be about as strong as the bulkhead or deck they are fitted in.

Why it matters: a watertight door stops one flooded space from sinking the ship. A weathertight door stops waves
that wash across the deck from reaching the spaces inside. Neither should be left open at sea unless the master or
company rules allow it.

### Worked example

A deck rating is told: "Close the weathertight door on the starboard side aft of the accommodation, and the watertight
door in the engine room."

1. Starboard is the right-hand side looking forward.
2. "Aft of the accommodation" is behind (toward the stern from) the crew living space.
3. The weathertight door keeps out sea spray and green water from outside only.
4. The watertight door stops flooding spreading from one compartment into the next, from either side.

## Exam traps

- Confusing **abaft** with **aft**. "Aft" is a direction; "abaft" is a position relative to an object.
- Using **athwartships** to mean "along the ship". It is **across** the ship.
- Calling a bollard and bitts the same. Bitts are on the **ship**, often a pair; a bollard is a single strong post, often on the **quay**.
- Thinking **watertight and weathertight** are interchangeable. Watertight resists pressure **both ways**.
- Reading "draft" as the height of the ship above water. That is **freeboard**. Draft is the depth **below** water.

## Check yourself

1. A line is led to a cleat "outboard" of the winch. Which side of the winch is that?
2. A door must resist water pressure from either side. Is it watertight or weathertight?
3. Is "abaft the beam" toward the bow or toward the stern?
4. What is the difference between a bollard and bitts?
