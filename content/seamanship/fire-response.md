# Fire on board: the master's response

**Study aid only.** The machinery module teaches what burns and how extinguishers work. This lesson is the master's side:
what you order, what you refuse to let people do, and what the regulations make you carry. Fire drills on your own vessel
are where you learn it.

## What you need to know

Fire is among the most dangerous emergencies on a small vessel: there is little space to retreat to and fuel is
usually aboard. The Canadian Coast Guard's search and rescue guidelines (TP 11637, 1993) give the order of priorities:
==save lives first==, ==stop the fire endangering other people or vessels second==, ==reduce property damage third==.
They also say to rescue people in order of greatest peril and not to go into a burning vessel with no one left to
rescue. Those are written for rescuers but they match what the master should decide on board: lives, then spread, then
the vessel.

## The first minutes: the Transport Canada procedure

Transport Canada publishes a written fire-fighting procedure template for small vessels. Its sequence is a good model for
the master's orders:

1. The person who sees the fire ==calls out its location==. Crew not at the helm go there with an ==extinguisher or
   hose==.
2. The master calls the fire department if the vessel is in port, or, underway, ==informs the shore authorities of the
   vessel's position and situation== once the fire has been assessed.
3. Someone sizes up the fire (is it electrical or something needing special handling?) and reports to the master.
4. While that happens, other crew ==close windows and vents to cut off air==, ready extinguishers, hoses and first aid, and
   prepare lifesaving equipment in case of abandoning ship.
5. If it is small and no one can be trapped, ==fight it==. Do not fight it if it makes ==heavy smoke==, is near ==other
   flammable material==, is ==too big for one extinguisher== or ==blocks the way out==.
6. Use the extinguisher from at least ==six feet== away, with the ==exit behind you==, and use ==PASS==: ==P==ull the
   pin, ==A==im at the base, ==S==queeze the handle, ==S==weep side to side. Work it across the whole fire, from near to far.
7. Use a hose if there is one, but ==not on electrical, grease or oil fires==.
8. If it is out, post a watch in case it ==reignites==. If the extinguisher is empty or the fire is too big, get out,
   shut the door and use the fixed system.
9. The master ==manoeuvres to keep the fire downwind== and ==decides whether the risk of spread or explosion requires
   abandoning ship==.
10. Keep the authorities and the company informed, then write an incident report.

## Fire in the engine space

TP 14070 gives the underway response: use extinguishers or buckets, send a distress signal, position the boat so the fire
is downwind, have everyone not fighting the fire put on lifejackets, and, if the fire is in the engine space, ==stop the
engine and shut off the fuel==. Consider evacuation if the fire spreads quickly. (The sources do not fix the order of
"point the fire downwind" and "stop the engine"; follow your own written procedure.)

The Small Vessel Regulations (SVR) build the engine-space response into the vessel:

- A passenger-carrying vessel of ==up to 6 m== with an enclosed engine space must have a ==provision to discharge an
  extinguisher into the engine space without opening the primary access== (==s. 415==), clearly marked, unless a fixed system is
  fitted.
- An extinguisher meant for this must hold ==1.2 kg of CO2 per cubic metre== of gross enclosed engine space volume (or a
  clean agent giving the same protection), ==discharge completely in not more than 60 seconds== (CO2) or ==10 seconds==
  (clean agent), and ==be in addition== to the extinguishers required by s. 414 (==s. 416==).
- A non-pleasure vessel ==over 6 m== with an enclosed engine space needs a ==fixed fire extinguishing system== or the
  direct-discharge provision (==s. 741==), and must be fitted with a ==fire alarm panel==, a dual action rate-of-rise and fixed temperature
  detector in each engine space and detectors in accommodation and service spaces (==s. 740==). Up to 6 m the Regulations require a hard-wired heat
  detector and an 84 dB alarm at the operating position, plus detectors in accommodation (==s. 418==).
- Non-pleasure vessels over 6 m also need two means of escape from each accommodation, service and engine space, with the
  usual exceptions (s. 742).

## Extinguishers by length (SVR s. 414)

| Length | Portable extinguishers |
|---|---|
| Up to 6 m | ==1A:5B:C== (a second one if there is a fuel-burning cooking, heating or refrigerating appliance) |
| Over 6 m to 9 m | ==2A:10B:C== (second one at fuel-burning appliance) and a ==10B:C at the engine space entrance== |
| Over 9 m to 12 m | 2A:10B:C, one at each access to a fuel-burning appliance space, 10B:C at the engine space, a ==fire axe== and a ==fire bucket== |
| Over 12 m | ==2A:20B:C==; one at each access to an appliance space and each accommodation space; ==20B:C at the engine space entrance==; a ==fire pump outside the engine space==, ==hose and nozzle==, an axe and ==two buckets== |

Extinguishers must be mounted in a clamp or bracket with a quick, positive release, and an extinguisher stowed in an
accommodation space must not contain a gas extinguishing agent (s. 414(3) and (4)).

## Report it

Under the Transportation Safety Board Regulations ==s. 3(1)(d)(iii)== a ==fire or explosion== on a ship other than a
pleasure craft is a reportable marine occurrence. Send what you know ==as soon as possible by the quickest means== and the
rest ==within 30 days==. A fire that causes a death or serious injury is also reportable as such.

## A BC example

An invented example. A skipper on an 11 m passenger vessel in Johnstone Strait smells smoke from the engine space. The
deckhand shouts "Fire in the engine room." The skipper orders passengers to the open deck and into lifejackets, shuts
down the engine and the fuel valve, and the deckhand closes the engine hatch and vents to cut the air, then fires the
engine-space extinguisher. The skipper calls MAYDAY with the position, turns the bow so the smoke blows away from the
passengers, and stands the lifesaving gear ready. When the temperature falls the crew keep a watch for reignition and
the skipper prepares the TSB report.

## Common mistakes

- Ordering the crew to fight a fire that is **blocking their way out**, or that makes **heavy smoke**.
- Using a **hose on an electrical, grease or oil fire**.
- Forgetting to **close windows and vents** to cut off air.
- Opening the engine-space door to attack the fire, instead of using the **direct-discharge point** or fixed system.
- Confusing the extinguisher ratings by length (**1A:5B:C up to 6 m**; **2A:20B:C over 12 m**).
- Treating the **CO2 1.2 kg/m3** and **60 seconds** figures as the capacity of the required extinguishers; the direct-discharge
  extinguisher is **in addition**.
- Not reporting the fire to the TSB.
