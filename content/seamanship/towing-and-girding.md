# Towing safely and girding

**Study aid only.** Towing is the commonest way a small BC vessel is lost with its crew. This lesson sets out what the
law and Transport Canada's guidance say; your towing procedures and training must come from your company and an
experienced tug master.

## What you need to know

Towing changes a vessel's whole stability problem. The towline pulls from a point on the tug, usually near the middle of
the hull, and if the pull comes from the side, it heels the tug. The worst case is ==girding==, which Transport
Canada's Ship Safety Bulletin 16/2020 describes as the situation where the ==towline pulls the tug broadside and the tug
cannot get out of that position==. It can lead very quickly to ==capsizing or sinking==. The TSB has recorded ==26 girding
situations that led to 21 capsizings== between 2005 and 2018.

## How girding happens

A tug girds when the tow (a barge, say) gets beside or ahead of it and the line pulls sideways. The bulletin lists these
risk factors; learn them as a checklist:

- A ==shortened towline==, which makes the line steeper so the tow pins no longer hold the line in place.
- The ==tug close to the tow==, especially in a narrow channel or busy water.
- ==Changes of speed or course==, by the tug or by the tow.
- The ==tow overtaking the tug==; if the tow is catching up, treat it as a warning.
- ==Current== and weather; the bulletin mentions river flow in particular.
- ==Open penetrations== in the hull, which let water in when the tug heels.
- The ==winch clutch engaged== while towing.
- Excessive ==heel==: a bridge angle-of-heel limit should warn the master, who must act before the accident.

The bulletin gives no numbers for towline length or angle. Those limits belong in the tow plan and the vessel's own
procedures.

### A real BC case

On 13 August 2018, in the north arm of the Fraser River, the tug *George H. Ledcor* was towing the loaded gravel barge
*Evco 55* while the tug *Westview Chinook* pushed on the tow. The barge overtook the tug, which girded and capsized,
and all four crew were rescued from the overturned hull. The master had applied full rudder and full throttle, and
attempts to abort the tow failed. The TSB found gaps in awareness, informal practices and training.

## Before the tow: the tow plan

The master prepares a tow plan, in addition to the company's procedures. The bulletin lists what it should consider:

- the ==size, type and limits of the tow==;
- ==towline length== and the ==tug's position==, and the ==towing point==;
- the tug's ==power and bollard pull==, and the best towing gear (lines, wires);
- ==stability of tug and tow together==, and the number of trained crew needed;
- ==connection and disconnection== arrangements, and whether a gob wire is fitted;
- the ==route== (narrows, bridges, traffic), limits of area, currents, weather and local knowledge;
- fuel, water and spares.

## The emergency release

The bulletin says the abort or emergency release must be ==tested regularly==: every place it can be operated is
included, the ==winch brake and clutch release==, and the system ==resets== afterward. The release procedure is set before
every tow, shared with the crew and ==posted near the towing equipment==. Transport Canada's tug guidance (TP 15491,
2024) says:

- each steering position has a control that ==immediately reduces tension== on a winch towline, and the winch has no
  ratchet preventing paying out;
- if the line is on a hook, each steering position has a ==hook release==;
- the line can be ==severed or released at the bollard==, or a ==clearly marked axe== is stowed there.

The Canadian Coast Guard's 1993 SAR guidelines add a ==towing watch== who watches the tow and gear, with a way of cutting the
line close by.

## The rules for small vessels

The Small Vessel Regulations (SVR) have specific rules.

- ==SVR s. 404(3)==: except in an emergency, a ==passenger-carrying vessel that tows must have no passengers on board
  the vessel or the tow==, and must meet the s. 521 requirements.
- ==SVR s. 521== for a workboat that tows. It must:
  - keep ==two lifejackets in the wheelhouse== and ==two in the engine space== if normally occupied;
  - have a means to ==release or cut the tow line immediately==;
  - have ==two means of escape== from the wheelhouse, one available if heeled;
  - keep the deck ==clear aft of the towing point== so the line swings freely;
  - if decked, be ==watertight aft of the towing point== (or the engine space, whichever is further forward) and ==drain
    water rapidly overboard==;
  - if not decked, have ==positive buoyancy when swamped== or a gunwale high enough to resist swamping.
- ==SVR s. 601(2)==: adequate stability for the intended operation. Tug guidance recommends a stability assessment that
  includes towing loads (required for tugs over 5 GT), and a re-assessment after changes.

## Equipment and signals

- ==Navigation Safety Regulations, 2020, s. 121==: a vessel designed and constructed for towing, of more than 5 GT,
  must have ==two searchlights== mounted to sweep ==360 degrees== together on an exclusive circuit, except a vessel that tows only to salvage logs.
- Lights: a ==tow of more than 200 m== is shown by ==three masthead lights== in a vertical line and a ==diamond shape== by
  day (TP 15491; see `colregs.rule-24.towing-pushing`).

## During the tow: precautions

From the bulletin and tug guidance: know the towline length, tug position and towing point; remember that ==speed or
course changes increase girding risk==; ==never tow with the clutch engaged except when adjusting length==; ==pay out
more wire to freshen the nip== on a long tow; watch the tow's size, momentum and pivot point; watch for the tow overtaking
the tug; check regularly with deck crew and other tugs; close all openings; and review heaving-to or shelter plans for bad
weather.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking girding is only a big-tug problem; it happens to small tugs working barges.
- Towing with the **winch clutch engaged**.
- Shortening the towline in a narrow channel without thinking of the **steeper angle**.
- Carrying **passengers** on a tug or tow (**s. 404(3)**).
- Thinking the **200 m** threshold is for the length of the tug.
- Testing the release only once, at one steering position.
- Believing the bulletin gives a **minimum towline length**; it does not.
