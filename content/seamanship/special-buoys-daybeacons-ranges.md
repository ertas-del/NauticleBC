# Special buoys, daybeacons, ranges and radar aids

**Study aid only. Not for navigation.** Check the current *Canadian Aids to Navigation System* and your chart.

## What you need to know

- ==Special marks are yellow== and carry ==yellow== lights (==Fl 4s==). They tell you something other than "the channel
  is here". The one exception is ODAS: ==Fl(5)Y 20s==.
- ==White with orange== marks give information, hazard, control or keep-out messages.
- Daybeacons are ==fixed, unlit== aids: ==square== to port, ==triangle== to starboard (upstream).
- A ==range== is a ==pair of daymarks== you line up. A ==RACON== answers radar with its own signal.

## Special marks (yellow)

Special marks are ==yellow== and show a ==yellow== light, ==Fl 4s==, if lit. Their **shape has no meaning**. They are not
mainly aids to navigation: they tell you about something in the area. Examples:

- **Cautionary** buoy: yellow, yellow X topmark. Warns of things such as firing ranges, racing courses, seaplane
  bases, underwater structures, aquaculture or a place with no safe channel.
- **Anchorage** buoy: yellow with a ==black anchor symbol==, yellow X topmark.
- **ODAS** (Ocean Data Acquisition System) buoy: ==yellow==, marks scientific, weather or oceanographic stations, yellow
  X topmark, and a different light: ==Fl(5)Y 20s== (a group of five yellow flashes every 20 seconds).

## White-and-orange marks

These carry information in the form of orange shapes and bands on white. They show a yellow light if lit (Fl 4s).

| Buoy | Look | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| ==Information== | White, orange squares (two opposite sides) and orange bands | Black words or symbols give the message |
| ==Hazard== | White, orange diamond outline, orange bands | Warns of a hazard; details may be written on it |
| ==Control== | White, orange open circles and bands, black symbols | A restriction (the Vessel Operation Restriction Regulations) |
| ==Keep-out== | White, orange diamond with a cross, bands | Keep out of the area (the same regulations) |
| ==Mooring== | White and orange (orange over the top third above the waterline) | Marks a mooring (check the chart and local rules before using or anchoring near it) |
| ==Swimming== | White | A swimming area; may not be charted |
| Diving | White with a red and white diving flag | Divers down; not normally charted; follow the Collision Regulations |

Some of these may not be on the chart, so use what you see and slow down.

## Daybeacons and daymarks

A **daybeacon** is a ==fixed structure== (a post or frame on a rock, shore or pile) with a ==daymark== painted on it. It
has ==no light==. It is a lateral mark, read like a buoy.

- **Port hand** daybeacon: a ==square== daymark (green centre on a white background). Keep it to port going upstream.
  Odd numbers.
- **Starboard hand** daybeacon: a ==triangular== daymark (red centre on a white background). Keep it to starboard going
  upstream. Even numbers.
- **Bifurcation** daybeacon: a diamond marking a channel split, with an indicator for the preferred route.
- **No-anchorage** mark: a square with a black anchor and a red diagonal stripe.

(The older black-and-white or black-square port markings still exist on some aids. If you see an older style, the
shape is what matters: square on the port hand, triangle on the starboard hand.)

## Ranges

A **range** is ==two daymarks, a front one and a rear one==, set a known distance apart so that the rear mark is higher
and farther from you. When you see them ==in line, one above the other==, you are on the line of the range, which marks
the safest water or the right entry into a channel. If the front mark appears to move to the left of the rear mark,
you have moved right of the line, and the other way round. Keep them lined up and you are tracking the centreline.

Ranges use trapezoid daymarks with vertical stripes of contrasting colour so they stand out against the shore. Some
ranges have lights for night.

## Radar aids

- A **radar reflector** is a ==passive== device that bounces radar back so the aid or small boat is more visible on a
  radar screen.
- A **RACON** (radar beacon) is ==active==: when a vessel's radar hits it, it ==transmits a coded reply== that shows on
  the screen, usually a Morse letter beside the echo. This tells you exactly which aid it is. RACON listings are in
  *Radio Aids to Marine Navigation*, not in the buoy publication.
- **AIS aids to navigation** (AIS AtoN) are electronic marks shown on AIS displays. Details are in the Canadian
  e-Navigation portal.

## Fog signals

Shore fog signals are operated when ==visibility is less than 2 nautical miles==. Buoy bells and whistles need wave
action to sound at all. Two cautions: ==do not judge distance== from how loud a fog signal is, and do not assume one is
not working just because you cannot hear it.

## Worked example: reading a mixed scene

Your boat is crossing a bay. You see a yellow buoy with a yellow X, a white buoy with orange squares and a green
square on a rock.

1. Yellow with an X is a ==cautionary== mark: something in the area needs care. Read the chart.
2. White with orange squares is an ==information== mark; read the black words on it.
3. A green square on a rock is a ==port hand daybeacon==: keep it on your port side when going upstream.
4. None of these is a lateral buoy, so do not use the yellow or white ones to find the channel.

## Exam traps

- Calling a yellow buoy a "channel marker". Special marks are not channel marks.
- Using shape to read a special mark. **Shape has no meaning**; colour and symbols do.
- Confusing the ODAS light (**Fl(5)Y 20s**) with the usual special-mark light (**Fl 4s**).
- Swapping daybeacon shapes: **square is port, triangle is starboard.**
- Saying a RACON is passive. It is **active**; a radar reflector is passive.
- Judging how far off a fog signal is by how loud it is.

## Check yourself

2. A buoy is white with an orange diamond containing an orange cross. What is it?
3. What shape is a starboard hand daybeacon?
4. What do you see when two range marks are in line?
