# Engine, fuel and bilge systems: the master's checks

**Study aid only.** You do not have to be an engineer to command a small vessel, but you do have to know when the engine
space is about to hurt you. The machinery module teaches how the systems work; this lesson is the short list of
things a master checks, enforces and reports.

## What you need to know

The Canada Shipping Act, 2001 puts the safety of the vessel and everyone aboard on the master (==s. 109==). In the
engine space that mostly means vapour, fuel, electrical sparks, water coming in and keeping the machinery running. The
Small Vessel Regulations (SVR) Part 6, ==Critical Safety Requirements==, applies to a small vessel that is not covered
by the detailed construction rules of Part 7, and sets the floor. Part 4 and Part 5 add the pumps and bailers.

## Ventilation and the blower

Petrol (gasoline) vapour is heavier than air. It settles in the bilge of an engine space and one spark can make an
explosion. SVR ==s. 603== says an enclosed gasoline engine space must have ==powered ventilation== added to its natural
ventilation to remove combustible vapours. Transport Canada's small commercial vessel guide, TP 14070, tells operators to
run the blower for ==at least four minutes== (more if the manufacturer says so) before starting the engine. Make it
a habit that cannot be skipped: blower on, count the time, sniff the bilge, then start.

A diesel engine does not make the same kind of explosive vapour, but diesel vessels still need fresh air for the
engine, and a fire or fuel leak in a closed space is still dangerous (see the fire module).

## Fuel and gas

SVR Part 6 gives the rules you can check with your eyes:

- ==No gaseous, propane (LPG), compressed natural gas or naphtha appliance== may be installed on a passenger-carrying
  vessel (==s. 604(2)==). No such appliance may be installed so that gas can enter or be trapped below deck (s. 604(3)).
- No fuel tank or fuel system may be installed or kept so that ==fuel can leak or spill== (==s. 605==).
- A fixed fuel tank needs a way to ==vent combustible vapours overboard== (==s. 606==).
- An inboard gasoline engine installed below deck, or enclosed by boxing, needs a ==flame arrestor== on its carburetor
  or throttle-body fuel injector (==s. 607==).

Refuelling is where most fuel trouble starts. Follow your company's written refuelling procedure; Transport Canada
publishes a refuelling template for small vessels that an operator can adapt.

## Electrical sparks

SVR ==s. 608== requires electrical components to be ==ignition-protected== to ==SAE J1171== or ==UL 1500==, with three
exceptions: the vessel burns only diesel, the component is isolated from fuel sources (engines, cooking appliances, vent
or fill fittings, fuel tanks), or it sits in a compartment whose only vapour source is an LPG or CNG fitting and that
compartment is open to the atmosphere or is an accommodation space. A master's practical rule: no household switch,
charger or heater in a bilge that can hold petrol vapour.

## Keeping water out and getting it out

Two rules protect the hull from the water around it.

- ==SVR s. 602==: every vessel needs the means to ==shut off underwater penetrations== positively (seacocks and
  valves), with ==wet exhausts excepted==. If a shut-off is in a space where fire is a risk, it must not be made of a
  material that fire damages (a plastic valve that melts is no shut-off). There is an exception for a vessel with
  level flotation and other means of stopping water.
- ==SVR s. 609==: every watertight compartment needs ==a means of pumping or access for bailing==, unless the vessel
  cannot hold enough water to capsize or the compartment is sealed and not readily accessible.

For a passenger-carrying vessel of up to 15 GT with 12 passengers or fewer, ==s. 412== sets the minimum: a bailer or
manual bilge pump up to 9 m, and a ==manual bilge pump== over 9 m. TP 14070 also states a pump capacity figure (at
least 0.91 litres per second) and an 84 dB high-bilge alarm for vessels with sleeping quarters; check the guide for the
vessels these apply to, as the sections read did not confirm that.

Know where every seacock is, that each handle works by hand, and which one you would close first if a hose bursts. The
flooding lesson picks up from here.

## When machinery or steering fails

Loss of power is usually a navigation problem before it is an engineering one. Think in this order: safety of
people, then the vessel's position, then the repair.

1. Tell the crew and, if there is traffic, show the right signals: a vessel that cannot manoeuvre as the Rules require
   is a "vessel not under command" (Rule 27, see `colregs.rule-27.nuc-ram`).
2. Decide whether you are drifting toward danger. If so, the call for help may be an urgency or distress call (see
   `radio.distress-urgency-safety` and the emergency-command lesson in this module).
3. Only then work the fault, using the engine-room lesson on faults (`machinery.engines.start-run-and-faults`).

Afterward, remember the reporting rule. Under the Transportation Safety Board Regulations ==s. 3(1)(d)(x)== the master,
operator and crew with direct knowledge must report a ==total failure of the main or auxiliary machinery==, and a total
failure of ==propulsion, steering or deck machinery or navigation equipment if the failure threatens safety==. Report
the available facts as soon as possible by the quickest means, and the rest within 30 days (s. 3(3)).

## Pre-departure systems check

TP 14070 gives an 18-item pre-departure list; the machinery items include fuel, oil and coolant levels, bilge pump
function, navigation lights and VHF. A master's version, in a sensible order:

1. Blower run and bilge sniffed (gasoline).
2. Fuel quantity for the trip plus a reserve, no smell, no leaks.
3. Oil, coolant and belts looked at by eye.
4. Bilge dry or the level known; bilge pump and alarm tested.
5. Seacocks open or closed as they should be; handles free.
6. Steering moved hard to hard; engine controls free; kill cord working where fitted.
7. Batteries secure, terminals tight, nothing loose in the bilge.
8. VHF, lights and horn work.

## A BC example

An invented example. A skipper of a 9 m petrol-powered water-taxi in Howe Sound finishes refuelling at a fuel dock, steps aboard and turns
the key at once to keep to schedule. A faint petrol smell hangs in the engine space. If he has skipped the blower
(four minutes) a spark from the starter can ignite the vapour. A disciplined skipper switches the blower on, opens the
engine hatch, sniffs the bilge, waits out the four minutes and only then starts, and his written refuelling procedure
says so, so a new deckhand does it too.

## Common mistakes

- Starting a gasoline engine before the ==blower has run for four minutes==.
- Thinking Part 6 ventilation applies to diesels the same way; the powered ventilation rule is for ==enclosed gasoline== spaces.
- Fitting a propane stove on a passenger-carrying vessel; ==s. 604(2)== prohibits it.
- Forgetting that a ==wet exhaust is the exception== to the underwater shut-off rule, not seacocks in general.
- Treating a steering or engine failure as only a maintenance matter and not a ==reportable occurrence==.
- Assuming a spare plastic seacock is acceptable in the engine space where fire is a risk.
