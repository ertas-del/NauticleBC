# Charts: types, projections and how reliable they are

A chart is a picture of the sea made for navigation. It shows depths, dangers, marks, land and the scales you use to plot. Knowing how it is built tells you how far to trust it.

## Nature and function

A chart shows ==water depths==, ==hazards==, ==aids to navigation==, ==coast features== and ==tides and currents== information. You plan with it, fix your position on it and check your track against it. It is a basic aid to safe navigation.

## Mercator charts

- ==Meridians== (north-south lines) and ==parallels== (east-west lines) are straight and cross at right angles.
- A ==rhumb line== (constant course) is a ==straight line== on the chart. This makes plotting simple.
- The ==scale grows with latitude==. A nautical mile is one minute of latitude, so ==measure distance on the latitude scale at the same level as the track==.
- Areas look larger away from the equator. This ==distortion== is acceptable for coastal use but matters at high latitudes.

## Polyconic charts

Some large-scale Canadian charts use the ==polyconic== projection. On a small area the distortion is tiny. The meridians curve slightly, so measure a ==true direction== with the ==compass rose nearest the track== or against the meridian near the middle of the line.

## Numbering and presentation

Each chart has a ==number==, a title, a ==scale== (large scale means more detail for a smaller area), a ==datum==, a ==compass rose== with variation, notes and cautions, and a ==source diagram== showing survey dates. Symbols and abbreviations are explained in ==Chart No. 1==. Depths are in metres from ==chart datum==. Edition date and the date of the latest correction are shown in the margin.

## Reliability

Ask:

- ==How old== is the survey? Old surveys may miss changes.
- ==How was it done==? Modern surveys are denser and more reliable than lead-line surveys.
- ==Is the area fully surveyed==? Blank or sparse soundings warn of poor data.
- ==What scale== is it? Use the ==largest scale== chart available.
- ==Is it corrected== to the latest Notices to Mariners?
- ==Natural change==: river mouths, sandbars and rocks can move or be uncovered.
- Items marked doubtful, reported or position approximate.

## A BC example

Planning a trip into a bay on the west coast of Vancouver Island, the fisherman finds the only chart of the bay is a small-scale one with a 1950s survey. He treats the sounding as a guide, goes in slowly at half tide with the sounder running, and does not rely on the chart's clear water.

## Common mistakes

- Measuring distance on the longitude scale.
- Trusting a small-scale chart for close pilotage.
- Using an uncorrected chart.
- Ignoring the source diagram.

## Check yourself

1. Why is distance measured on the latitude scale?
2. What does the source diagram tell you?
3. Why use the largest scale chart?
