# Tide vocabulary

**Study aid only.** The examples use invented numbers. They are not real tide data and not for navigation. Use current
CHS publications for real heights and times.

## The two ideas to keep apart

- **Tide** is the water moving **up and down**. It is measured as a height.
- **Tidal stream** (or tidal current) is the water moving **sideways**. It is measured as a speed and direction.

Both come from the same cause, but they do not always keep step with each other. Lesson 6 covers this in detail. For
now: when someone says "the tide", check whether they mean height or flow.

## Height words

**High water (HW)** is the top of the rise. **Low water (LW)** is the bottom of the fall.

**Range** is the difference in height between a high water and the low water next to it. Example: HW 3.9 m and LW 0.7 m
give a range of 3.2 m. Range changes through the month (springs and neaps, lesson 2).

**Rising** and **falling** describe the height of the water between low and high. A rising tide is climbing toward HW.
A falling tide is dropping toward LW. The time from LW to HW is the **duration of rise**, and from HW to LW the
**duration of fall**. Each is a little over six hours in a regular tide, but they are not always equal.

**Stand** is the short spell near high water or low water when the height barely changes before it turns. If you are
watching a post on a dock, the stand is when it seems to stop moving.

## The reference level: chart datum

A height is only useful if you know "height above what?" On Canadian charts and in Canadian tide tables, the
reference is **chart datum**:

- It is a **low water level**. The chart uses lower low water, large tide (LLWLT). Older charts and books call this
  lowest normal tide (LNT).
- It is chosen so the water **seldom** falls below it. It is *not* the lowest the water can ever go. A strong
  offshore wind or high pressure can occasionally push the sea below it.
- Charted depths (soundings) are measured **down from chart datum**.
- Tide table heights are measured **up from chart datum**.

**Height of tide** is the height of the water surface above chart datum at a particular moment. Putting these together:

> depth of water = charted depth + height of tide

Example with invented numbers: a sounding on the chart is 2.5 m. The height of tide is 1.8 m. The water is about
2.5 + 1.8 = **4.3 m** deep there. At chart datum (height of tide 0.0 m) it would be 2.5 m.

Heights of islands and clearances (such as under a bridge or cable) in tidal waters are measured from a high water
level, not from chart datum, so you also need the **current** height of tide to know how much room you really have.
The chart notes and publications say exactly which reference they use. Always read those notes before relying on any
clearance.

## Stream words

**Flood** is the stream going toward the shore or up an inlet, the one that goes with the rising tide in simple
cases. **Ebb** is the stream going away from shore or down an inlet. Be careful: people also say "flood tide" and
"ebb tide" loosely to mean rising or falling. In this module we keep "rising" and "falling" for the **height** and
"flood" and "ebb" for the **stream**.

**Slack water** is when the stream is nearly zero as it turns between flood and ebb. Slack is a statement about the
**current**.

**Stand** is a statement about the **height**. They can happen at different times. In many places and especially in
narrow channels, slack water does **not** line up with high or low water. Never assume. Look up the current tables.

## Predicted versus actual

A tide table is a prediction based on the sun and moon only. Wind, air pressure and river outflow can make the actual
height higher or lower than the table. Treat the table as the baseline and watch what the water is really doing.

## Common mistakes

- Using "height above chart datum" and "depth" as if they were the same thing.
- Assuming the water cannot go below chart datum.
- Saying "slack at high water" as a rule. It depends on the place.
- Mixing up which way a table's heights go: they are measured upward from chart datum.
