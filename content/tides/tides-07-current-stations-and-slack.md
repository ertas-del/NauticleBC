# Current stations, slack water and turn times

**Study aid only. Not for navigation.** The stations, times and speeds in this lesson are invented. For any real passage use the current CHS Canadian Tide and Current Tables for the area and follow the instructions in them. The layout of the pages can change from edition to edition.

## What you need to know

The tidal currents lesson explained why the stream and the tide are different things. This lesson is about the book that predicts the stream. In Canada the Canadian Hydrographic Service (CHS) prints tide and current tables in regional volumes. Volume 5 covers Juan de Fuca Strait and the Strait of Georgia; Volume 6 lists Campbell River and the Seymour Narrows current station, among others. Because the stream is so strong in those passes, the current tables are the book you reach for before any narrow channel.

A tide table gives ==heights==. A current table gives ==times and speeds==:

- the times of ==slack water==, when the stream is not running;
- the time and speed of each ==maximum flood== and ==maximum ebb==, in ==knots==.

There are normally two floods and two ebbs a day, so two high-water slacks and two low-water slacks a day in a semidiurnal regime. Read your edition for what it prints.

## Flood, ebb, and the signs

==Flood== is the horizontal movement of water linked with the rising tide, and ==ebb== the horizontal movement linked with the falling tide. In the CHS tables a ==plus sign marks flood== and a ==minus sign marks ebb==. The sign means flow from the ocean inland (plus) or back toward the ocean (minus). It does not mean north or south; the compass direction of flood differs from place to place, and you take it from the chart, the Sailing Directions or the station notes.

## Slack is not high or low water

It is tempting to say that slack water is at high or low tide. Both NOAA and the CHS tables say they do not necessarily coincide. At one place the stream may turn well before high water. At another the stream may keep running for an hour or more after the tide has begun to fall. That is why ==current tables exist separately from tide tables==, and why you never time a pass from a tide table.

## Reference stations and secondary stations

The structure is the same idea as for tides.

- A ==reference current station== has its own daily table of slack times and maximum rates.
- A ==secondary current station== has no daily table of its own. The book gives ==time differences== from a nearby reference station for slack and for maximum, and the maximum speed is given either as a ==percentage of the reference station's speed== or as a ==fixed value==.

Secondary stations often rest on only a month or less of observation, so their times and speeds are less reliable than a reference station's. Treat them with extra caution, and arrive early at a pass that depends on one.

### Worked example (invented)

Reference station "Example Narrows" for one day, standard time:

| Event | Time | Speed |
|---|---|---|
| Slack | 0412 | 0.0 |
| Max flood | 0705 | +4.8 kn |
| Slack | 1010 | 0.0 |
| Max ebb | 1318 | −5.2 kn |
| Slack | 1625 | 0.0 |

Secondary station "Example Bay" (invented corrections): slack time difference −0 h 25 min; maximum time difference +0 h 10 min; maximum speed 60 per cent of the reference.

1. Slack after the flood: 1010 − 0:25 = ==0945==.
2. Max ebb time: 1318 + 0:10 = ==1328==.
3. Max ebb speed: 5.2 × 0.60 = ==3.1 knots== (ebb, so −3.1).
4. If daylight saving time is in effect, add one hour to every standard time: slack 1045, max ebb 1428.

Do exactly what your edition's instructions say about signs and about which differences go with which event. A differences table may give different values for slack and for maximum, and may give separate figures for flood and ebb.

## The time standard

Times in the tables seen are ==standard time on a 24-hour clock==, shown as ==PST== at the heading, and you ==add one hour when daylight saving time is in effect==. This is the same trap as the tide tables: in summer, the clock on the bridge is an hour ahead of the table.

## What the numbers do not tell you

- ==Position.== A prediction applies at a stated point. In the CHS book the coordinates are in a table of stations, and in a narrow pass the prediction is for the middle of the channel. The stream can change strongly over a few metres, so a spot near the edge of the channel can behave differently from the middle.
- ==Wind.== Strong, prolonged wind can advance or delay slack water by a considerable amount. Currents are described as sensitive to the wind.
- ==Weak streams.== If a following flood is weak, the stream may not reverse at all and there may be no true slack.
- ==Pressure and river flow.== Like tide heights, currents are predicted from the sun, moon and local data. A large change in atmospheric pressure shifts the water level, and heavy runoff changes the flow in estuaries. One CHS source gives a pressure change of about 30 mb as moving sea level by about 0.3 m.
- ==The slack is a period, not a point.== The table gives a single time, but the stream is weak for a while either side of it. How long depends on the place, and you must not assume it is long.

## BC example

On a summer morning, a skipper planning to run north through a narrow pass reads the current table for the nearest reference station. Her pass is a secondary station, so she applies the time difference to the reference slack and notes that the observations behind it are short. She then adds an hour for daylight saving. She puts the result on her plan as "slack, from the book, plus one hour" and decides to arrive at the entrance 30 minutes early rather than risk the stream already running. She also notes the wind forecast: a strong wind from the north would be against a flood flowing north and would make the sea steeper. The next lesson works through a whole plan.

## Common mistakes

- Using a tide table for the time of slack.
- Forgetting the daylight saving hour, or applying it to the time difference as well as to the final time.
- Reading a minus as "going south" or "going west". It means ebb.
- Treating a secondary station as if it were as reliable as a reference station.
- Using the maximum speed of the reference station at a secondary station without applying the percentage.
- Assuming the prediction is exact when the wind is strong.
