# Spring and neap tides

**Study aid only.** This lesson explains the science. It is not a navigation aid. Use current CHS publications for
real tide information.

## Two tide makers

The last lesson showed that the moon is the main tide maker and the sun adds a smaller one, roughly half the size.
Each of them raises its own pair of bulges. What you see at the coast is the **sum** of the two. Whether they add up
or partly cancel depends on where the sun and moon are relative to the Earth.

## Spring tides: sun and moon pulling together

At ==new moon== and at ==full moon==, the sun, Earth and moon sit roughly ==in a line==. The sun's bulges and the moon's
bulges land on the same places, so they stack up. The result is the largest tides of the month:

- ==high waters higher== than usual,
- ==low waters lower== than usual,
- a big **range** (the difference in height between high and low water).

These are **spring tides**. The name has ==nothing to do with the season==.

## Neap tides: sun and moon at right angles

About a week after springs, the moon is at ==first quarter== or ==last quarter==. Seen from the Earth, the sun and
moon are now at roughly ==a right angle==. The sun's high bulge falls where the moon's low spot is, and the two
partly cancel. The result is moderate tides:

- ==high waters not so high==,
- ==low waters not so low==,
- ==a small range==.

These are **neap tides**.

## The monthly rhythm

| Moon phase | Sun, Earth, moon | Tides |
|---|---|---|
| New moon | in line | spring |
| First quarter | right angle | neap |
| Full moon | in line | spring |
| Last quarter | right angle | neap |

There are ==two sets of springs and two sets of neaps== in each lunar month, so the range swings between big and small
about every week. The timings above are the rule of thumb. The exact peak of a spring or neap in your area is in the
tables.

## What else changes the range

**Distance of the moon.** The moon's orbit is an ellipse, so its distance from the Earth varies by tens of thousands of
kilometres over a month (NOAA gives about 31,000 miles).

- At **perigee** (closest), the tide-raising effect is stronger and ==ranges are above average==: **perigean tides**.
- At **apogee** (farthest), ==ranges are below average==: **apogean tides**.
- When a ==new or full moon coincides with perigee==, expect an especially big spring tide.

**Distance of the sun.** The Earth is closest to the sun in early January and farthest in early July. The sun's
contribution is slightly larger in January.

**Angle of the moon and sun.** The moon and sun do not stay over the equator. They move north and south of it, an
angle called ==declination==. When the moon is far north or south of the equator, the two high waters of the day can be
quite different heights, and the same happens with the two lows. This is called the ==diurnal inequality==, and the
lesson on tide types builds on it. For the Strait of Georgia, one DFO source notes that ranges are larger around
June and December and smaller around March and September.

## Why a mariner cares

- Springs give the deepest water at high tide and the shallowest at low tide, and ==usually stronger tidal currents==.
- Neaps give less change in depth and ==usually weaker currents==.
- A planned anchorage, shoal crossing or beach landing can be fine at neaps and a problem at springs, or the other way
  round.
- You do not need to guess the range. The tide tables give each high and low water for each day, so you can see the range
  directly and compare the day's range against others.

## Common mistakes

- "Spring tides happen in spring." They happen about twice a month, all year.
- "Neap tides are low tides." A neap tide is a small-range tide: the highs are lower **and** the lows are higher.
- "Spring tide means a bigger high water only." The low water is lower as well.
- "The moon phase is all that matters." Perigee, apogee and the sun's distance add to it.
