# Types of tide

**Study aid only.** The examples use invented numbers. They are not real tide data and not for navigation. Use current
CHS publications for real heights and times.

## Three patterns

Tides do not look the same everywhere. Count the highs and lows in a lunar day (about 24 hours 50 minutes) and
compare their heights:

| Type | Highs and lows in a lunar day | Heights |
|---|---|---|
| **Semidiurnal** | ==two highs, two lows== | the two highs are ==about equal==, and the two lows are about equal |
| **Diurnal** | ==one high, one low== | not applicable |
| **Mixed** | two highs, two lows (some days only one of each) | the ==two highs differ, and the two lows differ== |

"Semi" means half, "diurnal" means daily: a semidiurnal tide repeats about twice a day.

Some sources split mixed tides further into "mixed, mainly semidiurnal" (two highs and two lows, with fairly small
differences) and "mixed, mainly diurnal" (one high and one low most days, with two of each at times).

## Higher high water and lower low water

In a mixed tide, the two highs in a lunar day are not the same height. We name them:

- **Higher high water (HHW)**: the ==higher of the day's two high waters==.
- **Lower high water (LHW)**: the lower one.
- **Lower low water (LLW)**: the ==lower of the day's two low waters==.
- **Higher low water (HLW)**: the higher one.

The ==difference in height between the two highs (or the two lows)== of the same day is the ==diurnal inequality==.
Planning matters here: you cannot assume that the next high is bigger than the last one, or that the afternoon low
matches the morning low.

## Why the types differ

Two main reasons:

- **The moon's angle.** When the moon (and sun) are ==well north or south of the equator==, the two bulges sit at
  unequal angles and the two highs differ. When they are near the equator, the highs are more alike. So the
  inequality swells and shrinks through the month and the year.
- **Continents and basins.** ==Land blocks the bulges==, and each ocean basin and coastline answers the forcing in its own
  way. That is why neighbouring coasts can have different patterns.

## What the sources say about British Columbia

This is a place where sources differ a little. Be ready to say which statement you are relying on.

- A CHS booklet on Canadian tides groups tides into semidiurnal, mixed mainly semidiurnal, mixed mainly diurnal and
  diurnal, and describes the Pacific coast as **semidiurnal** in its tide-type figure.
- A DFO chapter on tides and tidal streams is more detailed. It describes the region as **mixed**, with
  **Vancouver, Tofino and Prince Rupert** listed as **mixed, mainly semidiurnal**. It lists ==Victoria and Sooke== as
  ==mixed, mainly diurnal==. It says pure diurnal or pure semidiurnal days occur only a few days a month.
- For comparison, a NOAA tutorial describes the US West Coast as mixed semidiurnal.

A safe summary for study: the BC coast is generally a ==mixed tide, mostly semidiurnal in character==, with two highs and
two lows of unequal height most days. Some southern stations lean more toward one high and one low a day. Do not
treat this as a rule for a given port. A given port and day can be different, and the tables are what you use.

The types matter for the day's plan:

- On a mixed day, the lower of the two lows may be much lower than the other. ==Check which low you care about==.
- On a mixed day, the lower of the two highs may give much less water than the higher one.
- The range quoted for a day depends on which high and low you pair up.

## Common mistakes

- Treating every day as two equal highs and two equal lows.
- Assuming "mixed" means irregular. It is predictable. It is just unequal.
- Memorising "BC is semidiurnal" and then being caught by a day with a large inequality.

## Check yourself

3. A day's two low waters are at 0.4 m and 1.9 m. Which one is the lower low water?
