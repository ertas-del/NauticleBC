# What causes tides

**Study aid only.** This lesson explains the science. It is not a navigation aid. Real tide heights and times come
from current Canadian Hydrographic Service (CHS) publications.

## Why this matters

Most people on the water never learn why the tide moves, so it feels like magic that sometimes works against them.
It is not magic. Tides follow a few simple rules, and once you know the rules the tables make sense and the
surprises get smaller.

## What a tide is

A tide is the regular rise and fall of the sea surface. It is caused by the pull of gravity between the Earth, the
moon and the sun. Think of it as a very long, slow wave that crosses the ocean and reaches the coast, not as water
"coming in" the way a wave at the beach does.

## Gravity, and why distance beats size

Gravity pulls harder when two bodies are heavier, and weaker the farther apart they are. For the tides, distance
turns out to matter even more than weight:

- The sun is vastly heavier than the moon, and its pull on the Earth is far stronger than the moon's pull.
- But the sun is about 390 times farther away. What raises tides is not the pull itself but the **difference** in pull
  from one side of the Earth to the other, and that difference shrinks very quickly with distance.
- So the sun's tide-raising effect comes out at a bit under half of the moon's. Another way to say it: the moon's
  effect is roughly twice the sun's.

The moon is the main driver of tides. The sun is the second voice that sometimes helps and sometimes works against it
(next lesson).

## Why there are two bulges

Picture the Earth covered by one deep ocean, and the moon overhead.

- The side of the Earth facing the moon is closer to it, so the water there is pulled toward the moon a little more
  strongly than the Earth as a whole. The water heaps up under the moon: **bulge one**.
- The far side is pulled a little less than the Earth as a whole. The solid Earth is drawn toward the moon slightly
  more than the water on the far side is, so the water is left behind and heaps up on the side facing away from
  the moon: **bulge two**.

Some explanations describe the far-side bulge as water trying to carry straight on (inertia) while gravity
is weaker there. It is the same result in different words. What matters for you: **two bulges, on opposite sides of
the Earth, lined up with the moon.**

## Turning Earth, turning tides

The bulges stay roughly lined up with the moon, and the Earth spins underneath them. A place on a coast is carried
into a bulge (high water), out of it (falling to low water), into the other bulge (second high water), and out again.
That gives **two high waters and two low waters** in a little over a day.

## Why about 24 hours 50 minutes, not 24

The Earth turns once in 24 hours relative to the sun. But the moon is also moving around the Earth, in the same
direction the Earth spins. By the time you have come round to the same spot under the moon, the moon has moved on,
and the Earth has to turn a bit further to catch up. That takes about **50 more minutes**, so the full cycle is a
**lunar day of about 24 hours 50 minutes**.

What follows from that:

- Two high waters in a lunar day means high waters about **12 hours 25 minutes** apart.
- High water to the next low water is a little over **6 hours** (about 6 hours 12 minutes).
- Each day the tides arrive about **50 minutes later** than the day before. If high water is at 0930 today, expect it
  around 1020 tomorrow.

## The simple picture, and its limits

The two-bulge picture is a starting point. Real oceans have continents in the way, so the bulges cannot simply sweep
around the world. The shape and depth of each basin and coast, and local weather such as wind and air pressure,
change the actual height and timing. That is why tide predictions are made from long local observations, not worked
out from the moon's position alone, and why you use the published tables for your area.

## Common mistakes

- "The moon pulls water only on the side facing it." The far-side bulge is real too.
- "High tide is at the same clock time every day." It slips about 50 minutes later each day.
- "The sun is bigger, so it must matter most." Distance reduces its tide-raising effect to under half of the moon's.
- "Tides are a wave rolling in." A tide is a slow rise and fall of the whole sea surface.
