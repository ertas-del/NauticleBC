# Tidal currents

**Study aid only. Not for navigation.** This lesson explains why currents behave as they do. It does not give you
slack times or speeds for any real place. For any passage through a narrows or rapid, use the **current** CHS current
tables (and the other current CHS publications for that area). Never rely on this app, or on a rule of thumb, for slack
water.

## Tide and stream are not the same thing

- The **tide** is the vertical movement of the water: its height.
- The **tidal stream** (also called tidal current) is the horizontal movement of the water: its speed and direction.

The same forces drive both, but the timing of one does not give you the timing of the other. Tide predictions and
current predictions are produced separately, and you look in the current tables for currents.

## Where the water goes

When the tide rises, water has to come from somewhere. In bays, inlets and channels the water flows in as a **flood**
stream and out as an **ebb** stream. If a big inlet fills through a narrow entrance, the water has to squeeze through, and
the stream through the entrance can be strong.

## Why slack water is not simply at high or low water

Many people learn "slack water is at high tide and low tide". That is true in some places and false in others. The
timing depends on the shape of the coast and how the tide wave behaves there. There are three simple cases:

1. **Standing-wave pattern.** Slack water is near high and low water, and the strongest streams are about half way
   between. This is the pattern many people have in mind.
2. **Progressive-wave pattern.** The tide travels along like a wave. The strongest streams are near high and low
   water, and slack is about half way between. This is the opposite of case 1.
3. **A channel connecting two bodies of water whose levels differ** (a "hydraulic" current). Water flows from the higher
   side to the lower side. A DFO source says that in such cases slack water can fall roughly midway between high
   and low water.

You cannot tell which case applies from a general rule. NOAA states that the relationship between the times of high and
low water and the times of maximum current or slack water is unique to each place and cannot be found from a rule of thumb.
That is why separate current tables exist.

## Why BC has famous tidal rapids

The BC coast has many narrow, island-strewn channels with large tide ranges. Two things combine:

- **Squeezing.** A large volume of water has to pass through a narrow gap in the time between tides. In a gap, the stream
  speeds up.
- **Tides arriving from both ends, out of step.** In Johnstone Strait, a tide comes in from the north (Queen Charlotte
  Sound side) and another from the south (Strait of Georgia). One source says the northern tide arrives about three hours
  later at the narrow parts; another gives a lag of about two hours. Either way, the water levels at the two ends are
  often different, so water is forced through at speed.

Narrows named by DFO sources as having very strong alternating streams include **Seymour Narrows, Okisollo, Surge,
Hole-in-the-Wall, Yuculta and Arran Rapids**. One DFO source reports streams at Seymour Narrows above 7 m/s, about
13 knots. These are far faster than most small vessels can make through the water. Eddies, whirlpools and standing
waves can form.

Passing a rapid is a planned event, timed for slack water or for a stream the vessel can safely handle, using the
current tables for that particular rapid. If you miss the slack, you may have to wait hours for the next one.

## Reading the current tables

Current tables give speeds in knots, not heights. In the tables used on this coast, as described in university
notes, flood is marked with a plus and ebb with a minus, and slack times are shown in bold. Check which time standard
the current tables use, as you do for the tide tables, and remember the daylight saving hour (lesson 5). Layouts and
conventions can change from edition to edition. Follow the instructions in the current publication.

## Wind against the stream

When the wind blows **against** the stream, the waves get shorter and steeper, and the sea can turn from
manageable to rough. This can be worst in channels and around headlands. A calm-looking channel with a bit of wind can
turn rough when the stream turns against the wind. When you plan, look at the forecast wind **and** the stream direction
at the time you will be there. Slack water, or a stream running with the wind, will be gentler than a strong stream
against the wind.

## Pulling it together

- Tide height tells you how much water there is. Current tables tell you how fast it is moving.
- The two are linked but out of step by an amount that varies from place to place.
- Narrows and rapids on the BC coast need their own current predictions and careful planning.
- The prediction is not the whole story. Wind, rain and river outflow can change the real stream and sea state.

## Common mistakes

- Planning a rapid from the tide table instead of the current table.
- Assuming slack is at high or low water everywhere.
- Treating "flood" as always meaning "toward the north" or toward the same place. Flood and ebb are about toward or
  away from the head of the water, and directions differ from place to place.
- Forgetting wind against the stream.
