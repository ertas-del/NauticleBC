# Charts: reading them and plotting a position and course

A chart is a map of the water. It shows depths, hazards, marks, land and the scales you need to find your way.

## The main features

- ==Title and number==, ==scale==, and the ==edition and correction date==.
- ==Soundings==: depths in metres, measured from ==chart datum==.
- ==Depth contours== join points of the same depth.
- ==Land heights== shown above a high-water datum.
- ==Symbols== for buoys, lights, rocks, wrecks, cables and anchorages. A booklet called ==Chart No. 1== explains them.
- ==Compass roses== showing true and magnetic north.
- ==Notes and cautions==: read them. They contain warnings about hazards, restricted areas and local rules.

## Chart datum and soundings

Charted depths are measured from ==chart datum==, a low-water level. On Canadian charts it is close to the lowest normal tide. The real depth at any moment is the ==charted depth plus the height of the tide==. Drying heights show rocks that uncover at low tide.

## Latitude and longitude

- ==Latitude== lines run east to west and are read on the ==side scale== (left and right edges).
- ==Longitude== lines run north to south and are read on the ==top and bottom scale==.
- Degrees are divided into ==60 minutes==. ==One minute of latitude is one nautical mile== (about 1852 metres).
- Measure distance using the ==latitude scale at the same level as your track==, never the longitude scale.

## Plotting a position

To plot 48° 24.5′ N, 123° 22.0′ W:

1. Find the line for 48° 24.5′ on the side scale and draw a light pencil line across.
2. Find 123° 22.0′ on the bottom scale and draw a line up.
3. Where the two lines cross is your position. Mark it with a small circle and write the time.

## Course and distance

1. Draw a line from your start to your destination.
2. Use a ==plotter or parallel rule== to transfer the line to the ==compass rose== and read the ==true course==.
3. Use ==dividers== to measure the distance on the latitude scale. Read it in nautical miles.
4. Work out the time: ==time = distance ÷ speed==. For example, 12 NM at 6 knots takes 2 hours.

## Bearings

A bearing line from an object shows the direction to it from you. Two bearings, or a bearing and a range, cross at your position.

## Requirement to carry charts

You must carry ==up-to-date charts== of the area you operate in, and the required nautical publications. Check which charts and publications your vessel must carry. A paper chart is the backup when electronics fail.

## Safety

Check the chart's ==correction date== and get recent updates. Don't rely on an old chart. Pencil lightly so you can erase without damaging it.

## A BC example

You plan a trip from Sidney to Ganges Harbour. You draw the track on the chart, read a true course of 340° on the rose, measure 11 NM with the dividers on the side scale, and at 5.5 knots you work out 2 hours. You check the chart for rocks near the track and add a margin.

## Common mistakes

- Using the longitude scale to measure distance.
- Forgetting that depths are from chart datum, not the current water level.
- Using an old, uncorrected chart.
- Not reading chart notes.

## Check yourself

1. Which scale do you use to measure distance?
2. How long does 15 NM take at 5 knots?
3. What is chart datum?
