# Radar: how it works and how to use it

Radar lets you see targets in the dark and in fog. It is a tool, not a replacement for a lookout.

## The principle

A radar antenna rotates and sends out short pulses of radio energy. When a pulse hits a target (a boat, land, a buoy) some of it bounces back as an ==echo==. The radar measures:

- ==Range==: the time between the pulse and the echo.
- ==Bearing==: the direction the antenna was pointing when the echo came back.

The display shows echoes as spots at the right range and bearing, with your own boat at the centre.

## Start-up

1. Switch on the power and ==allow warm-up== (the magnetron or transmitter may need a minute or two).
2. Set the radar to ==standby==, then to ==transmit==.
3. Select a suitable ==range== scale.
4. Adjust the controls (below) to get a clear picture.

## Main controls

- ==Range==: how far the picture shows. Use a short range for close-quarters and a longer range for early warning.
- ==Gain==: sets the receiver sensitivity. Too high and the picture is noisy. Too low and weak targets vanish.
- ==Sea clutter (STC)==: reduces the echoes from waves near the boat. Too much hides small targets.
- ==Rain clutter (FTC)==: reduces rain echoes.
- ==Tuning==: gets the strongest echo from the receiver.
- ==Brilliance==: screen brightness.
- ==Range rings== and ==variable range marker (VRM)==: measure range.
- ==Electronic bearing line (EBL)==: measures bearing.
- ==Orientation==: ==head-up== shows your heading at the top. ==North-up== shows north at the top and needs a compass input.

## Reading the picture

- ==Land== shows as large, steady, solid returns.
- ==Other vessels== appear as moving spots.
- ==Buoys== and small boats give weak, sometimes intermittent echoes.
- ==Rain squalls== give a patchy fuzzy area.
- Beware of ==false echoes== and ==shadow areas== behind large objects.
- A boat made of wood or fibreglass, or a small one, may not show at all. A ==radar reflector== helps.

## Using radar for position

- Use ==two or more ranges== to prominent objects, with arcs on the chart.
- Use ==ranges== in preference to bearings, because range is more accurate.
- Choose targets that are ==clear and identifiable== on the chart, such as steep headlands or islands.
- A radar bearing and range from one object gives a fix, but with less accuracy.

## Using radar for collision avoidance

- Watch an echo over time. If its ==bearing stays the same and the range reduces==, there is risk of collision.
- Use the EBL and VRM to measure bearing and range repeatedly.
- Radar tells you something is there. It doesn't tell you what the vessel is doing. Combine it with a visual lookout.
- Don't make small alterations. Make a bold change early.
- In fog, reduce speed and use fog signals as well.

## A BC example

In thick fog off Race Rocks, you slow down and switch the radar on. A strong, steady echo appears 2 miles off on a steady bearing and the range is falling. You alter course to starboard, keep sounding fog signals, and watch the echo pass down your port side.

## Common mistakes

- Setting the sea clutter too high and losing small boats.
- Relying on radar alone.
- Using bearings instead of ranges for a fix.
- Leaving the radar on a long range with no close-up check.

## Check yourself

1. What does the radar measure to give range?
2. What does too much gain do?
3. What tells you there is risk of collision on the screen?
