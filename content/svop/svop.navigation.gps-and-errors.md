# GPS: using it and knowing its limits

GPS is the most-used navigation tool on small boats. It is accurate and easy to use, but it can also mislead you if you trust it blindly.

## How GPS works

A GPS receiver picks up signals from several ==satellites==. It calculates how far it is from each, and from this it works out your ==position==, ==speed== and ==course over the ground==. It needs signals from at least ==four satellites== to give a full three-dimensional position.

## Starting up

1. Switch on and let the receiver ==acquire satellites==. A cold start can take a few minutes.
2. Wait for a ==fix== before relying on it. Check the satellite strength and status.
3. Check the ==settings==: position format (degrees and minutes), units, and ==datum==.
4. Check your ==position against your known location== to confirm it is reasonable.

## What it tells you

- ==Position== in latitude and longitude.
- ==SOG==: speed over ground.
- ==COG==: course over ground (not the heading of the boat).
- ==Waypoints==: stored positions you can navigate to.
- ==Distance and bearing== to a waypoint.
- ==Cross-track error==: how far you are off the planned track.

## Datum

The GPS and the chart must use the same ==datum==. Canadian charts use ==NAD83==, which is the same as WGS84 for practical purposes. A wrong datum can give an error of many metres.

## Sources of error

- ==Poor satellite signal== from a blocked antenna, heavy cover or poor positioning.
- ==Loss of signal== or interference or jamming.
- ==Wrong settings== or datum.
- ==Waypoint entry mistakes==: a typing error in a waypoint can send you to the wrong place.
- ==Electronic failure== or power loss.
- ==Accuracy limits==: normally within about 10 metres, which can still matter in tight channels.

## Always double-check

Never rely on GPS alone. Cross-check with:

- The ==chart== and visual marks.
- The ==depth sounder== and the chart's depths.
- ==Radar== ranges and bearings.
- The ==compass== heading.
- Keep a ==paper chart== and know how to plot a position by hand.

A GPS that is off by a small amount can still put you on a rock.

## A BC example

Approaching the narrow entrance to a harbour in Nootka Sound in rain, the GPS shows you well off the entrance. You check the depth, radar and visible rocks, and find the GPS has lost its fix. You slow down and use the radar and visual marks to enter safely.

## Common mistakes

- Trusting the GPS without checking.
- Not entering waypoints carefully.
- Forgetting the datum.
- Not carrying a paper chart as backup.

## Check yourself

1. How many satellites does a GPS need for a full fix?
2. What datum do Canadian charts use?
3. What should you cross-check the GPS against?
