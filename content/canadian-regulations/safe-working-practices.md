# Safe working practices and PPE

**Study aid only.** Paraphrased from the Maritime Occupational Health and Safety Regulations, SOR/2010-120 (MOHS), and the Safe Working Practices Regulations, C.R.C., c. 1467 (SWPR). Standards named in the regulations (CSA numbers) are not reproduced here. The SWPR consolidation I read is an older instrument last amended in 2007, so a reviewer should confirm how it sits beside MOHS today.

## What you need to know

Two regulations spell out how work on a ship must be done. The MOHS are made under ==Part II of the Canada Labour Code== and apply to ==employees on Canadian-registered vessels== (s. 3). The SWPR are an older, shorter set of rules about working areas on ships. Both put the main duty on the employer, but both also give duties to the person doing the job, and the second kind is the part a deck rating is most often tested on.

## Who is responsible for what

The employer must ==arrange work so it does not endanger anyone's health or safety== and must adopt procedures to reduce the risk of injury. The employer or owner must also make sure that a ==qualified person is in charge of every work area== and inspects the work areas, structures, machinery and equipment to confirm safe conditions are kept up (MOHS s. 7; SWPR ss. 4 to 6). Structures or equipment that have been taken apart and put back together may not be used until a qualified person has examined them (MOHS s. 7(4); SWPR s. 8(2)). Under the SWPR a "qualified person" is the holder of any licence or certificate the law requires for the work or, if none is required, someone the employer considers has the knowledge and experience.

The worker's duties come from SWPR s. 7. Every person ==takes all reasonable and necessary precautions for their own safety and that of their fellow workers== and ==uses the protective clothing and equipment provided==. Four more worker rules:

- ==Report an unsafe condition immediately to the person in charge of the working area== (s. 11(1)). Only a qualified person corrects it (s. 11(2)). In an emergency anyone may do what is needed to prevent loss of life or injury (s. 11(3)).
- Any accident or injury is reported at once to the person in charge (s. 13).
- ==No one may tamper with a safeguard or safety device==, and ==no one may obstruct an emergency exit== (ss. 9 and 10).
- No one may be permitted in a working area whose ability to work is, in the person in charge's opinion, ==impaired by alcohol or a drug== (s. 14).

## The order of controls

Wearing protective equipment is not the first answer to a hazard, it is the third. MOHS s. 124(1) requires the employer to address hazards in this order: ==(a) eliminate the hazard==, including by engineering controls; ==(b) reduce it, including by isolating it==; ==(c) provide personal protective equipment==; and ==(d) use administrative procedures== such as work permits. The practical meaning for a deck rating is that PPE is what you wear when the job cannot be made safe in any better way.

## Protection equipment (MOHS Part 10)

Protection equipment must be used when it is not feasible to eliminate or control a hazard within safe limits and the equipment may prevent or reduce injury (s. 138). The equipment must suit the hazard, ==must not itself create a hazard==, and must be maintained and inspected by a qualified person. The Part sets rules by hazard: protective headwear for head injury hazards (s. 139), protective footwear and, for slipping hazards, non-slip footwear (s. 140), eye or face protectors (s. 141), respiratory protection for oxygen-deficient or contaminated air (s. 142), shields or body covering for skin hazards (s. 143), and securing loose clothing, long hair, dangling jewellery and similar items (s. 145). A defect in any protection equipment is reported as soon as feasible, and the employer must ==tag it unsafe and remove it from service== (s. 150).

## Fall protection (MOHS s. 144)

The employer must provide a fall-protection system to anyone who has access to:

- an ==unguarded work area more than 2.4 m above the nearest permanent safe level==, above any moving machinery parts or other surface that could injure on contact, or above an open hold;
- a structure covered by Part 2 that is ==more than 3 m== above a permanent safe level; or
- a ==ladder more than 2.4 m above the nearest permanent safe level== when the work lets the person use only one hand to hold on.

The numbers to remember: the ==anchor must withstand 17.8 kN==, and a system that arrests a fall must keep the ==peak arrest force to 8 kN or less== and the ==free fall to 1.2 m or less==.

## Drowning hazards (MOHS s. 147)

If the work creates a drowning hazard (other than abandonment drills), every person with access must be given ==a life jacket or other flotation device that meets the buoyancy standard, or a safety net or fall-protection system==. In addition ==emergency equipment must be held in readiness==, a ==qualified person to operate it must be present and ready to intervene==, a vessel provided if appropriate, and ==written emergency procedures== prepared.

## Tools, machinery and lifting

A tool or machine found defective is reported as soon as feasible, and the employer ==marks or tags it unsafe and removes it from service== (MOHS s. 204). A machine with exposed moving or hot parts must have a guard that keeps people from touching them; it may not be used with the guard off, and repair with the guard off is done only once the machine is inoperative (if that is not feasible, only under procedures the employer has set up) (MOHS ss. 206 and 207). Tools used in a fire hazard area have a non-sparking exterior (MOHS s. 203(1)).

For lifting, the person operating a lifting appliance must be ==qualified==, and the owner must see that a notice of its ==safe working load== is permanently fixed to it (SWPR ss. 16 and 17). No one may ride on a lifting appliance in operation unless safe accommodation is provided or it is essential for maintenance with safe access (SWPR s. 18). A load must not be left hanging from lifting machinery unless a qualified person is present and in charge (MOHS s. 213(3)).

Hazardous substances have their own rules: employees who handle them are trained on labels and safety data sheets (MOHS s. 253), and signs warn of their presence (s. 251). Openings to ==holds, tanks and compartments deeper than 1.5 m== must be ==covered or fenced to 900 mm when not in use, unless they have a coaming of at least 760 mm== (SWPR s. 25).

## A BC example

On a 19 m workboat anchored in Howe Sound, a deckhand is asked to climb to the mast platform, 3.5 m above the deck, to replace a masthead light, and then to scrape paint on the hull from a small float alongside. The platform is unguarded and more than 2.4 m above the deck, so a fall-protection system is needed, with an anchor strong enough (17.8 kN). Working over the side from a float is a drowning hazard: the deckhand wears a life jacket, rescue equipment is ready, a qualified person is watching and ready to act, and there are written emergency procedures. Before leaving the deck, the deckhand notices the harness lanyard is frayed. The right step is to report it so that it is tagged unsafe and removed from service, not to use it.

## Common mistakes

- Treating PPE as the first line of defence. The legal order puts elimination and isolation ahead of it.
- Remembering 2.4 m for every height. Structures are 3 m, and the ladder rule has its own one-hand test.
- Mixing up the fall numbers: 17.8 kN is the anchor strength, 8 kN is the peak force, 1.2 m is the free fall.
- Fixing an unsafe condition yourself. You report it; a qualified person corrects it (except in an emergency).
- Forgetting that drowning protection is more than a life jacket: emergency equipment, a person ready to act and written procedures are also required.
