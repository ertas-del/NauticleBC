# Enclosed spaces, work permits and hot work

**Study aid only.** Paraphrased from the Maritime Occupational Health and Safety Regulations, SOR/2010-120 (MOHS), and the Safe Working Practices Regulations, C.R.C., c. 1467 (SWPR). Exact standards (CSA numbers) are not reproduced. Where the two overlap, a reviewer should confirm which one an examiner expects you to quote.

## What you need to know

Some jobs on a vessel kill people quickly: going into a closed tank, working with a flame near fuel, working on live electrics. The law answers them with one tool, the ==written work permit==, and a short list of conditions that must be met first. As a deck rating you may be the person who is handed the permit, the person standing at the hatch, or the person who has to say no because the permit is not there.

## When a permit is required

The employer assesses certain work to decide whether it can cause death or serious injury: work on live electrical equipment that cannot be isolated, hot work, work involving hazardous substances above the exposure limits, and anything else that might be that dangerous (MOHS s. 165). Then, before the work starts, the employer ==issues a written work permit to a qualified person== for ==work that requires entry into a confined space== and for any other work assessed as that dangerous (s. 166). Entering a confined space without a permit is flatly prohibited (s. 169).

A permit records who issued it and who received it, ==the periods it is valid==, the work and its location, and the assessment of the hazard with the instructions that follow from it: procedures, lockout of equipment, safety tests, tags or signs, protection equipment, emergency procedures, and any other permit that could affect them (s. 167). The employer signs it, the holder has the terms ==explained and signs==, it is available for employees to see while the work goes on, and it is kept ==two years== after the work (s. 168). Locked-out equipment stays locked out while the work is done (s. 168(3)).

## What a confined space is

The definition has three tests, and a space must meet all of them (MOHS s. 1). It is ==enclosed or partially enclosed==; it is not designed or intended for people to be in it except to do work; it has ==restricted means of access and egress==; and it may become hazardous because of its design, location or atmosphere, what is in it, or other conditions. Ballast tanks, void spaces, cofferdams and fuel tanks are the typical examples. The employer must also assess a cargo hold to decide whether it is a confined space, and again whenever conditions change (s. 123(2)).

## The assessment and the oxygen limits

Before anyone is authorized to enter, the employer appoints a ==marine chemist or other qualified person== to assess hazardous substances and specify the tests (MOHS s. 171(1)). That person must verify at least that:

- chemical agents are not above the exposure limits;
- other airborne hazardous substances are not hazardous to the person;
- ==oxygen is between 19.5 and 23 per cent by volume== at normal pressure, and the ==partial pressure of oxygen is not below 148 mm Hg==; and
- these values can be held for as long as the person will be inside (s. 171(2)).

The result goes into a ==signed written report== that includes the vessel, the location, the test results, the instruments used and when they were last calibrated, a hazard evaluation, the procedures, the protection equipment, and the emergency procedures, including ==immediate evacuation if an alarm sounds or the readings change significantly== (s. 171(3)). The report is kept on the vessel two years (s. 172). The person about to enter has it explained and signs a dated copy (s. 173(2)).

## Preparing the space

Before the permit is issued the employer must (MOHS s. 173(1)): obtain the report; remove any liquid a person could drown in and any free-flowing solid that could trap a person; prevent anything from flowing in, by secure disconnection or ==blank flanges==; lock out electrical and mechanical equipment that is a hazard; make sure the opening is big enough for a person wearing protection equipment; and ==establish an entry control system==.

If the atmosphere or preparation conditions cannot be met, the employer must at a minimum provide (s. 173(3)): a qualified person outside, in communication with the entrant and holding a suitable alarm device; a ==safety harness on a lifeline anchored outside== and controlled by that person; ==two or more employees== nearby to help (the qualified person may be one); and one of those helpers trained in the emergency procedures, holding a first aid certificate and equipped. If the work could produce a hazardous substance, the space is ventilated or everyone uses respiratory protection (s. 174(1)). Where ventilation is what keeps the air safe, the fan needs an alarm or a person watching it, and enough time to escape if it fails (s. 174(2)).

Nobody ==closes off a confined space== until a qualified person has checked that no one is inside (s. 173(4)), and the SWPR says the same to the person in charge of the working area (s. 32).

## The older SWPR rules for tanks

The SWPR have their own, shorter version for ==pump rooms, cofferdams, ballast tanks, oil tanks== and similar compartments. No one enters without breathing apparatus (s. 30(1)) unless the compartment has never held a substance that gives off hazardous gas and has been ventilated to a qualified person's satisfaction, or it has held such a substance and has been gas-freed, tested and found safe (s. 30(2)). A certificate or statement is completed and available to anyone entering (s. 30(3)), ventilation continues while people are inside (s. 30(4)), and ==another person must attend at the entrance== for as long as anyone is in (s. 31).

## Hot work

==Hot work is any work where a flame is used or a source of ignition may be produced== (MOHS s. 189). It includes welding, burning and grinding that throws sparks. For hot work, a qualified person patrols the working area and the areas next to it and keeps a ==fire watch== for the duration of the work and, if that person decides it is necessary, ==30 minutes afterwards==; enough extinguishers are provided (s. 190). Hot work is not done where flammable gas, vapour or dust may be present unless the area is freed of gas, tested and found safe by a marine chemist or other qualified person (s. 191). Cables and gas cylinders are kept clear of vehicle areas, cylinders are kept upright, and unattended equipment must be left safe (s. 192 and s. 193). If the work may produce a hazardous substance, the space is well ventilated or people wear respiratory protection (s. 194). Oxygen is never used to ventilate a space, to blow out pipelines, or to clean (SWPR s. 35).

## A BC example

A tug in dry dock at a Vancouver Harbour yard needs a void space repaired. The yard supervisor and the master agree that entry and welding are both involved, so two permits are needed. The marine chemist tests the void: oxygen is 18.6 per cent. That is below 19.5, so nobody enters; the space is ventilated and retested until it reads inside the 19.5 to 23 limits and can be held there. The entrant has the report explained and signs it, a deckhand takes the attendant's place at the hatch with a radio and an alarm, and the harness line is anchored outside. After the welding stops, the fire watch stays on, because the qualified person judges that smouldering is possible, for the 30 minutes.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a permit as a formality. No permit, no entry.
- Believing 21 per cent is the limit. The entry window is 19.5 to 23 per cent.
- Leaving the hatch to fetch something. The attendant stays until the person inside has left, except in an emergency involving that person.
- Using oxygen to freshen the air in a tank. It is prohibited.
- Stopping the fire watch the moment the arc goes out. The qualified person may require 30 minutes more.
