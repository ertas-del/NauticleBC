# Crew MED, familiarization and first aid

**Study aid only.** Paraphrased from Part 2, Division 2 of the Marine Personnel Regulations, SOR/2007-115 (MPR), mainly ss. 205 and 206, and s. 103. The table in s. 205(3) is long. This lesson gives its logic and the small-vessel rows. Check the live table for your vessel.

## What you need to know

Training is the law's answer to a simple fact: on a small boat everybody on board is part of the emergency response, whatever their rank. The MPR set two layers. First, **familiarization**: a new person learns this particular boat before doing anything on it. Second, **Marine Emergency Duties (MED)** training, a recognised course that every crew member needed for safe manning must complete early in their sea career.

The duty falls on the master and the authorized representative jointly. If the first layer is missing, neither of them can say it was somebody else's job.

## Familiarization before the first duty (s. 205(1) and 206)

The master and authorized representative must ensure that ==any person assigned a function on the vessel receives the on-board familiarization and safety training set out in TP 4957 before they start any duty==. A new deckhand does not stand watch, handle lines or sit in the wheelhouse until this is done.

Section 206 backs it up with paperwork:

- The authorized representative gives the master ==written instructions== so that every member of the complement, ==before being assigned any duty==, becomes familiar with the ==shipboard equipment specific to the vessel==, the ==operational instructions specific to the vessel== and their ==assigned duties==, and can perform duties vital to safety or pollution prevention effectively.
- The master ensures each crew member is trained on these at the beginning of employment and that their knowledge is kept up to date.
- The master keeps a ==record of training== with four items: the ==name== of each person trained, the ==equipment== they were trained on, the ==subject-matter== and the ==days== on which they were trained. It must be available to a marine safety inspector, ==on board==, or, if the vessel does not travel more than ==5 nautical miles== from its home port, ==at the home port==.

## MED certificates (s. 205(2) to (6))

Every member of the complement who is required to be on board for the vessel to meet safe manning must, ==before acquiring a total of 6 months of sea service==, obtain one of the MED training certificates the table in s. 205(3) lists for that vessel type and voyage. The three MED types that appear in the table are:

| MED certificate | Where it appears |
|---|---|
| MED with respect to ==basic safety== | Every row of the table |
| MED with respect to ==small passenger vessel safety== | Every row of the table |
| MED with respect to ==small non-pleasure vessel basic safety== | Rows for smaller vessels, including non-fishing vessels of not more than 15 GT or under 12 m on voyages not more than 25 nautical miles from shore; not the catch-all row for other vessels on sheltered or near coastal Class 2 voyages |

A few small-vessel rows also accept a ==Pleasure Craft Operator Card==. Examples are non-passenger vessels of not more than 8 m other than fishing vessels or tugs, on sheltered waters or voyages less than two nautical miles from shore. Any other vessel on a sheltered waters or near coastal Class 2 voyage falls in the catch-all row, which lists the first two types only. Where the table does not apply, s. 205(2) falls back on the STCW basic safety MED.

A passenger vessel that is not a ferry, runs on sheltered waters only between March 31 and December 1, and has unberthed accommodation has its own seasonal MED (s. 205(4)).

Two specialist rules for the people on the muster list:

- A person assigned to a **fire team** needs MED basic safety on a sheltered waters vessel that must carry a fireman's outfit, or one of the table MED certificates on a sheltered waters vessel that does not (s. 205(5)).
- A person assigned to ==prepare or launch survival craft==, other than a manually launched life raft, needs an MED certificate for ==proficiency in survival craft and rescue boats other than fast rescue boats== (s. 205(6)).

## First aid and medical care (s. 205(8) and (9))

The person designated to provide first aid must hold:

| Voyage class | Certificate |
|---|---|
| Sheltered waters or near coastal Class 2 | ==Marine basic first aid or marine advanced first aid== |
| Unlimited or near coastal Class 1 | ==Marine advanced first aid== |

Someone must also be designated to take charge of medical care: a physician or a person who has completed the approved marine medical care course (s. 205(8)). Minimum complement rules require that designated first aid person to be among the persons on board (s. 207(3)(g)).

## Five years and then what? (s. 103)

A certificate or endorsement that needs an MED course may be issued only if the course was completed ==within the 5 years before the application date==. If it was older, the regulation accepts: a refresher course taken within the five years; at least 12 months of service on board performing deck or engine room duties within the five years, supported for MED by a testimonial of competent participation in emergency drills; or at least 3 months of such service in the 12 months before the application, with the same testimonial. First aid courses are also not valid for life.

## A BC example

A new deckhand joins a 14 m water taxi in Vancouver for the summer. On day one, before touching a line, the skipper walks them through the boat: where the extinguishers and lifejackets are, how the bilge alarm works, how to launch the life raft, what to do in a man overboard. The skipper writes the name, equipment, topics and date in the training record, kept at the home dock because the boat never travels more than 5 nautical miles from it. The deckhand then has until they reach 6 months of sea service to take an approved MED course. When the deckhand later wants to apply for a Master, Limited certificate, the MED must be less than 5 years old or covered by one of the substitutes.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the 6 months as a delay for the first day. Familiarization comes before any duty; MED comes before 6 months of sea service.
- Thinking one MED covers every vessel. The table sets which types are accepted by vessel and voyage.
- Using a basic first aid certificate on an unlimited or Class 1 voyage. Those need marine advanced first aid.
- Leaving out the "days trained" or "equipment" in the record. All four items are required.
- Forgetting that the record may be kept at the home port only if the vessel does not travel more than 5 nautical miles from it.
