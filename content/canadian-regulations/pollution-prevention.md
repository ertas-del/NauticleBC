# Pollution prevention and reporting a discharge

**Study aid only.** Paraphrased from the Canada Shipping Act, 2001, Part 9 (ss. 185 to 193), and the Vessel Pollution and Dangerous Chemicals Regulations, SOR/2012-69 (VPDCR). The VPDCR are long and cover tankers, noxious liquids and air emissions that a small-vessel deck crew never meets; this lesson keeps to what a deck rating or a master of a small vessel is likely to face. Which BC waters are "Section I" and which are "Section II" turns on other instruments that were not read; check before relying on a distance or concentration rule.

## What you need to know

One sentence in the Act drives everything here: ==no person or vessel shall discharge a prescribed pollutant== except as the regulations allow (CSA 2001 s. 187). "Discharge" is broad. It includes spilling, leaking, pumping, pouring, emitting, emptying, throwing and dumping (s. 185). The regulations prescribe three pollutants for this rule: ==oil and any oily mixture==, ==garbage==, and ==organotin compounds that act as biocides== (VPDCR s. 4). The regulations then say when each may lawfully go over the side. If no rule allows it, the answer is no.

## The excuses

Section 5 of the VPDCR lists the only situations in which an otherwise prohibited discharge is excused. They are:

- ==saving lives, securing the safety of the vessel or preventing its immediate loss==;
- ==damage to the vessel or its equipment in an accident of navigation==, unless the accident happened because of an action outside the ordinary practice of seafarers;
- a ==minimal and unavoidable leakage of oil from underwater machinery==;
- an ==accidental loss of a synthetic fishing net== when every reasonable precaution was taken; and
- garbage released from damage to the vessel, with reasonable precautions taken before and after.

An excuse does not remove the reporting duty, as explained below.

## The master's duties

The master of any vessel must take ==all reasonable measures to protect the marine environment== (CSA 2001 s. 186.3(1)). If the master is told a pollutant may have been, is being or may be discharged, the master must take reasonable measures to protect the water, ==including eliminating the risk if feasible==. On a Canadian vessel the master must also notify the authorized representative (the owner's responsible person) unless the risk is gone (s. 186.3(2)). The authorized representative must develop procedures to prevent a discharge and deal with emergencies, and see that ==the crew are trained in emergency procedures== and ==crew and passengers in environmental protection== (s. 186.1(1)).

## Oil

An oily mixture, such as bilge water, may be discharged only if the vessel is ==en route==, the mixture is processed through ==oil filtering equipment that produces effluent of not more than 15 ppm==, and no chemicals have been added to hide the oil (VPDCR ss. 30 and 31). In the inland waters of Canada the alarm and discharge-stopping device must act at ==5 ppm== (s. 30(1)(c)(iii)). A vessel that has no approved equipment cannot use these authorizations, so its oily bilge water stays aboard to be landed ashore. Equipment of this kind must be of an approved type and kept in good working order, and nobody may operate it once it no longer meets the requirements (s. 9). Larger ships have more: vessels of 400 GT or more carrying oil as fuel or cargo need an oil pollution prevention certificate (s. 25) and a shipboard oil pollution emergency plan (s. 27), which the vessel must take reasonable measures to implement in an incident (CSA 2001 s. 188). Fuel transfers in Canadian waters have their own rules, and some (two-way communications, for instance) do not apply to vessels under 400 GT (VPDCR s. 32).

## Garbage

Where the regulations allow garbage to be discharged (Section II waters and beyond), the conditions are about distance (VPDCR s. 101(1)). ==Floating dunnage, lining or packing without plastics: at least 25 nautical miles from the nearest land==. ==Other non-plastic garbage: at least 12 nautical miles==. Garbage ==comminuted to pass a 25 mm screen: at least 3 nautical miles==. Plastics are not in any of those categories, so ==plastics are never discharged==. Every vessel ==12 m or more in length overall must post placards== telling crew and passengers the rules (s. 103). A vessel of ==400 GT or more, or certified to carry 15 persons or more==, keeps a garbage management plan (s. 104) and a Garbage Record Book kept for two years after the last entry (s. 105 and s. 106(3)); the master gets a receipt from any reception facility and keeps it one year (s. 107).

## Sewage and greywater

Sewage must pass through a marine sanitation device and meet a ==fecal coliform limit of 250 per 100 mL==, or ==14 per 100 mL in a designated sewage area== (VPDCR s. 96(1)(a) and (b)). A small vessel (under 400 GT, certified for no more than 15 persons) in Section I or II waters, but not in inland waters or a designated area, has further options: treated sewage at least ==1 nautical mile from shore==, or, with no treatment required, at least ==3 nautical miles from shore while en route at the fastest feasible speed== (s. 96(1)(e)), with extra steps if the water is less than 6 nautical miles wide. The treated-sewage options must leave no visible solids, film, sheen or discolouration (s. 96(2)), and the other options must leave no visible solids on the shoreline (s. 96(3)). Greywater (sinks, showers, laundry, dishwashers) must not leave ==solids or a sheen== (s. 131.1(4)).

## Reporting

The master must ==report any discharge or anticipated discharge== that is prohibited, or that is excused for safety, accident, net loss or damage (VPDCR s. 132(1)). The report is made ==as soon as it occurs or is anticipated==, or as soon as feasible if the master is busy saving lives, securing the vessel or limiting damage (s. 132(3)). In waters under Canadian jurisdiction the report goes to a ==marine safety inspector or a marine communications and traffic services officer== (s. 132(7)); in practice the VHF call to MCTS is how a small vessel does this. It states who, when and where, what and how much, and what has been done (s. 132(5)), and further essential information follows in a supplementary report (s. 132(8)). If the owner or authorized representative is ashore and knows no report was made, they must make it immediately (s. 132(4)). The penalty on summary conviction is up to ==$1,000,000 or 18 months or both==, and whether the discharge was reported is something the court may weigh (CSA 2001 s. 191(2) and (4)(d)).

## A BC example

A 14 m charter vessel is bunkering at a marina in Nanaimo. The deckhand sees a rainbow sheen spreading from the vent. The deckhand shuts the fuel valve, tells the master, and the master orders the transfer stopped and the spill contained. Oil on the water is a discharge of a prescribed pollutant, so the master has a duty to take reasonable measures and then to report it to a marine safety inspector or an MCTS officer, giving the vessel, time, position, the product and a quantity estimate, and what is being done. The next day, a passenger throws a plastic water bottle over the side. There is no distance at which that is allowed, and because the vessel is 12 m or more, a placard on board must say so.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking small vessels are exempt. The ban in the Act applies to everyone, and the 15 ppm route needs equipment a small vessel may not have.
- Mixing up the garbage distances: 25 nautical miles for floating non-plastic packing, 12 for other non-plastic garbage, 3 for ground garbage.
- Believing an excuse means no report. Excused discharges for safety, accident, net loss or damage still have to be reported.
- Waiting for certainty. An anticipated discharge must be reported too.
- Treating greywater as harmless. It must not leave solids or a sheen.
