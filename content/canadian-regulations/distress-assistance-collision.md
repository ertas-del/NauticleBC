# Duties to assist and after a collision

**Study aid only.** Paraphrased from the Canada Shipping Act, 2001 (CSA 2001), Part 5 (ss. 130 to 133) and Part 6 (ss. 148, 151 and 152). Check the live text on Justice Laws.

## What you need to know

The sea has an unwritten rule that you help. In Canada it is also written law. Three situations create duties for a master: hearing a distress signal, finding someone in danger at sea, and being in a collision. A fourth, a direction from a search and rescue coordinator, can redirect your voyage. The rules apply to the master of a vessel in Canadian waters, and to a master who is a Canadian citizen, permanent resident or Canadian corporation (a "qualified person") in any waters.

## Answering a distress signal (s. 131)

On receiving ==a signal from any source== that a person, a vessel or an aircraft is in distress, the master ==shall proceed with all speed to render assistance== and, ==if possible, inform the persons in distress or the sender of the signal==. "Any source" matters: the signal does not have to come from the vessel in trouble. A relay from the Coast Guard, another ship or a shore station counts.

There is an exit. If the master is ==unable==, or in the special circumstances ==considers it unreasonable or unnecessary== to go, the master does not have to proceed, but must ==enter the reason in the official log book==. A 9 m boat that cannot safely reach a casualty in a gale is the obvious example. The duty to write the reason down is what the exam likes.

The vessel in distress also has a power. Its master ==may requisition== one or more of the answering vessels, and a requisitioned vessel must ==continue to proceed with all speed==. This stops a swarm of volunteers from replacing the right help. A master is released from the duty in two ways:

| Release | Section |
|---|---|
| The master learns that another vessel is complying with a requisition | s. 131(4) |
| The persons in distress, or the master of another vessel that has reached them, say assistance is no longer necessary | s. 131(5) |

Notice the difference: being released because someone else was requisitioned is automatic once you learn of it; being released because help is no longer needed depends on being told by someone who is in a position to know.

## Persons in danger at sea (s. 132)

Separate from a distress signal, the master must ==render assistance to every person who is found at sea and in danger of being lost==. There is no signal and no call; you simply find a swimmer, a drifting kayak or a capsized skiff. The same duty applies to aircraft on or over Canadian waters (s. 133).

## Search and rescue coordinators (s. 130)

The Minister designates search and rescue mission coordinators to organize search and rescue operations. When informed that a person, vessel or aircraft is in distress or missing in Canadian waters, the Canadian exclusive economic zone, or on the high seas off Canada's coasts in circumstances suggesting distress, a coordinator may:

1. direct all vessels in a specified area to ==report their positions==;
2. direct any vessel to ==take part in a search or otherwise render assistance==;
3. give other directions the coordinator considers necessary; and
4. use any lands if necessary to save the life of a shipwrecked person.

Every vessel and person on board a vessel in Canadian waters ==must comply==, and so must any qualified-person master in any waters. If a coordinator calls you, you answer and do as directed.

## After a collision (s. 148)

If vessels collide, the master or person in charge of each vessel must, ==so far as they can do so without endangering their own vessel, crew or passengers==:

- ==render assistance to the other vessel, its master, crew and passengers== needed to save them from danger caused by the collision, and ==stay by the other vessel until it has no need of further assistance==; and
- ==give the name of their vessel==, the ==name and address of its authorized representative==, and any other prescribed information to the master or person in charge of the other vessel.

The limit is the first clause. A master is not required to put their own people at risk to help, but is required to do everything else. The second duty is separate: you do not drive off once you are sure nobody is hurt.

Penalties show how seriously it is taken. Failing to render assistance after a collision is punishable on summary conviction by a fine of up to ==$1,000,000== or imprisonment for up to 18 months, or both (s. 151). Failing to give the required information carries a fine of up to ==$25,000== (s. 152). You need to know which of the two duties is the heavier one.

## A BC example

Mid-afternoon in Haro Strait, you are running a 13 m charter boat when a MAYDAY relay comes over VHF about a sailboat dismasted and taking water off Sidney. You are about 6 nautical miles away. Section 131(1) tells you to proceed with all speed and tell the sender you are coming. Ten minutes later the sailboat's skipper calls that a Coast Guard vessel has arrived and assistance is no longer needed. Under s. 131(5) you are released, because you have been told by the persons in distress. If instead the sea state made it unsafe for your boat to go, you would call in your position and situation and enter the reason in the log book.

A week later, in fog, you clip a fishing boat's stern in the same area. Your first job is the people: stand by and help, if you can without endangering your own passengers. Your second is to give the other master your boat's name and the name and address of the authorized representative.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking a distress signal must come from the casualty itself. Section 131 says "from any source".
- Writing nothing down when deciding not to go. The reason must go in the official log book.
- Believing the first answering vessel is released once it arrives. Release comes under s. 131(4) or (5) on the two stated triggers.
- Treating the collision duty as only about injuries. Naming your vessel and authorized representative is a separate offence if skipped.
- Forgetting the safety limit. The collision duty applies only so far as the master can act without endangering their own vessel, crew or passengers.
