# Bridge teamwork, handover and communication

Even a two-person crew on a fishing boat needs teamwork. Mistakes happen when people assume rather than check.

## Why teamwork

Navigation errors usually arise from poor communication, assumptions, and failure to challenge. Teamwork means everyone knows ==who is in charge==, ==what is happening== and ==what to do if something looks wrong==.

## Handover and relief

Before taking over a watch, the relieving person should:

- Know the ==vessel's position, course, speed and intended track==.
- Know ==standing orders== and special instructions.
- Know ==traffic, hazards, tides, currents and weather==.
- Check the ==compass error==, instruments, steering and lights.
- Be ==fit and alert==, with eyes adjusted to the dark.

If any manoeuvre or action is in progress, the handover is ==delayed== until it is complete. The outgoing OOW stays if the relieving person is not fit.

## Responsibility clearly defined

The ==responsibility for the safety of navigation== must be clear at all times. When the master is on the bridge, say clearly who has the conn. If a pilot is aboard, the master still has responsibility, and the OOW supports the pilot and master. If you are uncertain, ask.

## Coordination of tasks

Share tasks so no one is overloaded. Look-out, steering, radar, radio and plotting should have clear owners. Don't let radio calls or paperwork replace the look-out.

## Communication and acknowledgement

Use ==clear, simple language==. ==Repeat back== orders and information to confirm they are understood (closed-loop communication). Make sure messages are acknowledged. Use agreed terms for helm and engine orders.

## Verification

Decisions should be ==verified== using more than one source: GPS and radar, chart and depth, compass and visual marks. Question information that doesn't fit. Encourage crew to speak up.

## Reporting mistakes

==Mistakes and unclear situations must be reported promptly==, accounted for and corrected. A culture where people can report errors without blame prevents repeat mistakes.

## On fishing vessels

On a small crew, the master may be busy with the gear. Agree before hauling who watches the traffic. Plan how to hand over when the master rests.

## A BC example

At 0300 a deckhand takes over the watch from the skipper in Hecate Strait. The skipper points out the track on the chart, the vessels on radar, the compass error, and the course to steer. The deckhand repeats it back, and the skipper goes below only when satisfied.

## Common mistakes

- Handing over in a hurry with no briefing.
- Assuming the other person has seen a target.
- Not repeating back orders.
- Hiding a mistake.

## Check yourself

1. What must the relieving officer know before taking over?
2. Why repeat back orders?
3. What should you do if you make a mistake?
