# Charts, weather and water

Before you leave the dock, ask three questions. Where is the deep water? What will the tide and current do? What will the weather do? This lesson gives you the basics for all three.

## Reading a chart

A ==nautical chart== is a map of the water. It shows depths, rocks, buoys, lights, shorelines and hazards such as cables and bridges. In Canada, charts come from the ==Canadian Hydrographic Service==. A booklet called ==Chart 1== explains every symbol and abbreviation.

You must carry the charts and publications for the area you boat in. Small boats may not need them if the operator knows the waters very well. A chart is still the best way to plan a trip.

{{figure:svop-chart-datum}}

Most new Canadian charts show depths in ==metres==. Older charts may use fathoms or feet, so check the title. Depths are measured down from ==chart datum==. In BC tidal waters, chart datum is a ==very low tide== level. Most of the time there is ==more water than the chart shows==. But on a very low tide there can be less.

To find the real depth, add the tide: ==depth of water = charted depth + height of tide==.

A number that is ==underlined== is a ==drying height==. It marks a rock or bank that ==uncovers at low tide==. The number is its height above chart datum.

## Compass and GPS

A pleasure craft must carry a ==magnetic compass==. A boat ==8 m or under== that stays ==within sight of seamarks==, such as buoys and lights, does not need one. A compass needs no battery. Keep phones, speakers and metal tools away from it. The boat's own magnetism can pull the needle off, so learn how your compass behaves.

==GPS== and chartplotters are very useful. A marine GPS can find your position to about 10 m. But batteries die, units fail and charts on a screen can be out of date. ==Keep paper charts on board== in case your GPS fails. Use a marine unit, not a car GPS. Check your position against buoys and landmarks as you go.

## Tides and currents in BC

The tide rises and falls because of the pull of the ==moon== and the ==sun==. Most of the BC coast has ==two high and two low tides a day==, and they are ==not the same height==. In some places, such as Victoria, one tide a day can be much bigger than the other.

As the tide rises and falls, water flows in and out of inlets and passes. This is the ==tidal current==. The ==flood== flows in from the sea. The ==ebb== flows back out. ==Slack water== is the short time when the current stops and turns. In narrow passes, such as Active Pass or Seymour Narrows, the current can be faster than your boat. ==Slack water does not always happen at high or low tide==.

The Canadian Hydrographic Service publishes ==tide and current tables== for BC in regional volumes. Predictions are also on the CHS tides website. Use the station closest to where you will be. The tables use ==standard time==. When ==daylight saving time== is in effect, ==add one hour==.

## Marine forecasts and wind warnings

Environment and Climate Change Canada (==ECCC==) issues marine forecasts for coastal BC several times a day. You can read them online or hear them on the ==VHF weather channels== (WX1, WX2, WX3 and 21B on the Pacific coast). On inland lakes, check the local weather forecast too.

In a marine forecast, ==wind speed is in knots==. The wind ==direction is where the wind blows from==. A "west wind" blows from the west. Wind speeds are averages, so gusts can be stronger.

{{figure:svop-wind-warnings}}

| Warning | Wind speed |
|---|---|
| ==Strong wind warning== | ==20 to 33 knots== |
| ==Gale warning== | ==34 to 47 knots== |
| ==Storm warning== | ==48 to 63 knots== |
| ==Hurricane force wind warning== | ==64 knots or more== |

A strong wind warning is serious for a small boat. If one is out for your area, stay in port.

## Signs of changing weather

Check the forecast before you leave, and keep listening while you are out. Watch for signs of trouble:

- A ==falling barometer== means a ==low or a front is coming==.
- Dark, building clouds, or a sudden change in wind direction.
- Wind rising above the forecast, or fog rolling in.

If you see these signs, head for shelter early.

## Wind against current

{{figure:svop-wind-against-tide}}

When the ==wind blows against the current==, the waves become ==short, steep and breaking==. A calm pass can turn rough in minutes when the tide turns, with no change in the wind. Plan to cross passes and river mouths at ==slack water==, or when wind and current go ==the same way==.

## A BC example

It is July. You plan to take a 5 m runabout from Galiano Island out through Porlier Pass to fish for the afternoon. The forecast is northwest 10 to 15 knots, with no warnings. The current table shows the current in the pass turns at 1:10 p.m. standard time. You add an hour for daylight saving time: 2:10 p.m. Until then the current runs against the wind and the pass will be rough. You wait at the dock, go through near slack, and plan your trip home for the next slack.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting to add an hour for daylight saving time.
- Assuming high tide and slack water happen at the same time.
- Trusting the chartplotter alone near rocks.
- Going out with a strong wind warning in effect.
- Crossing a pass with the wind against the current.
