# Cold water and falling overboard

Canadian water stays cold all year, and BC is no different. Transport Canada says about 94 per cent of Canadian boaters who drowned were in water colder than 20°C. This lesson is about getting through those first minutes, and getting people back aboard.

## Cold water shock and 1-10-1

When you fall into cold water, your body reacts by itself. The Canadian Safe Boating Council teaches it as ==1-10-1==.

- ==1 minute: cold shock.== You gasp, then breathe very fast. If your face is under water when you gasp, you can drown at once. ==Control your breathing.== Keep your face out of the water. A lifejacket holds your head up while you calm down.
- ==10 minutes: useful movement.== Your hands, arms and legs get weaker. Use these minutes to get out, climb onto something, or get into a safe position. After this, you may not be able to swim, hold on, or work a flare.
- ==1 hour: hypothermia.== It can take about an hour before you pass out from cold, even in ice water. Without a lifejacket, you will likely drown long before that.

Strong swimmers are affected too. Swimming is not a plan in cold water. Swim only to reach a boat, another person or safety.

## Hypothermia

==Hypothermia== means your body core has cooled below ==35°C==.

Signs to watch for:

- ==shivering== (it may stop as the person gets worse);
- ==slurred speech==;
- ==clumsiness== and odd behaviour;
- ==confusion== or sleepiness;
- slow, shallow breathing and a weak pulse;
- in the worst case, unconsciousness.

Basic first aid:

- Call for help (VHF channel 16 or 911).
- Get the person out of the wind and into a warm, dry place.
- Remove wet clothing. Wrap them in dry blankets or clothes.
- Warm them ==slowly==. Do not rub their skin.

## HELP and huddle

{{figure:pcoc-cold-water}}

If you are in the water with a lifejacket and cannot get out, keep still to keep your heat.

- ==HELP== (==Heat Escape Lessening Position==): ==cross your arms tight against your chest== and ==draw your knees up==.
- ==Huddle==: with two or more people, hold each other close, ==chests together==, legs woven together. A group is also easier for rescuers to see.

Climb onto anything that floats, such as an overturned boat. That gets more of your body out of the water.

## Wear your lifejacket

Most people who drown while boating were ==not wearing a lifejacket or PFD==. Many had one on board. In cold water, rough water, or with an injury, you may not be able to put one on after you fall in. ==Wear it, do not just carry it.==

If your PFD is the inflatable kind, the law says you must wear it on an open boat. In water under 15°C, a PFD with thermal protection is a good choice.

## Person overboard

If someone falls overboard:

1. ==Shout "Person overboard!"== so everyone knows.
2. ==Slow down or stop.==
3. ==Throw something that floats== to them, such as a lifebuoy or a buoyant heaving line. It also marks the spot.
4. Give one person one job: ==keep pointing at the person in the water== and never look away.
5. Turn back and ==come in slowly==.
6. ==Keep the propeller away from the person.== When you are close, put the engine in ==neutral==, or stop it.
7. Get a line or lifebuoy to them and bring them alongside.

Call for help early if you cannot reach them or they are hurt.

## Getting back aboard

It is hard to climb into a boat from the water, even when warm. That is why a ==reboarding device==, such as a ladder, is required if the climb is ==more than 0.5 m==. A rope can serve as the device only if it is kept for that purpose. ==Never use the motor or its bracket to climb aboard.==

## Swamped or capsized

A boat that fills with water is ==swamped==. A boat that turns over has ==capsized==.

- Check that everyone is there and ==wearing a lifejacket==.
- If the boat is swamped but floating, ==bail== or pump.
- ==Stay with the boat.== A boat is much easier for rescuers to see than a head in the water.
- ==Climb onto it== if you can, to get out of the cold water.
- Call or signal for help as soon as you can.
- Leave the boat only as a last resort.

## A BC example

Liam and his daughter are fishing on Kootenay Lake in May. The water is about 8°C. Liam stands up to net a fish and falls in. He gasps, but his PFD holds his face up. He counts his breaths for a minute until they slow. His daughter shouts, throws the buoyant heaving line, and keeps her eyes on him. She comes back slowly and puts the engine in neutral. The climb into their boat is more than 0.5 m, so it carries a ladder. Liam climbs back in within five minutes. They head home to warm up.

## Common mistakes

- Carrying lifejackets under a seat instead of wearing them.
- Trying to swim for a far shore in cold water.
- Driving straight at the person in the water with the engine in gear.
- Leaving a capsized boat to swim for help.
- Rubbing a cold person's skin hard to warm them.
