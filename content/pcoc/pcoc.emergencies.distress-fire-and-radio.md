# Calling for help, fire and grounding

Things can go wrong fast on the water. Help may be far away. This lesson covers how to call for help, how to fight a small fire, what to do if you hit bottom, and what you owe other boaters.

## Distress signals

These signals all mean ==I am in distress and need help==. You can use them alone or together:

- a ==red flare==, hand-held or fired into the air;
- ==orange smoke==;
- ==slowly raising and lowering your arms==, stretched out to each side;
- a ==continuous sound== on a horn or whistle;
- ==SOS== in Morse code (· · · – – – · · ·), by light or sound;
- the spoken word ==Mayday== on a radio;
- a ==DSC distress alert== from a marine radio;
- signals from an EPIRB or personal locator beacon;
- in Canadian waters, a ==white light flashing 50 to 70 times a minute==.

It is against the law to use these signals ==except in distress==.

## Flares

Canada approves four kinds of flare:

- ==Rocket parachute== (Type A): one bright red flare that hangs under a small parachute.
- ==Multi-star== (Type B): fires two or more red stars.
- ==Hand flare== (Type C): a red flame you hold. Hold it over the side, ==downwind==, and do not look at it.
- ==Smoke signal== (Type D): orange smoke. Use it ==in daylight==.

Use flares only when someone is likely to see them, such as a boat or plane in sight.

Flares ==expire 4 years after the date of manufacture==. The date is stamped on each one. Store them in a ==cool, dry place== you can reach fast.

Old flares are a hazard. ==Never fire them except in an emergency.== Never soak them, sink them or put them in the garbage. Ask the ==distributor== how to dispose of them. Do not leave them at a Coast Guard base, police station or fire hall.

## VHF radio

{{figure:svop-mayday-card}}

A marine ==VHF radio== is the best way to call for help on the water. The Coast Guard and nearby boats can all hear you.

- ==Channel 16== is for ==distress, urgency, safety and calling==. Keep your radio on it. Once you reach another boat, move to a working channel to talk.
- ==Channel 70== is for ==DSC== (Digital Selective Calling). It sends a digital alert. You ==cannot talk== on channel 70.
- A DSC radio has a red ==distress button==. It sends your position and your ==MMSI==, a ==9-digit== number that identifies your boat. Get an MMSI and program it into the radio before you need it.

The three spoken calls:

| Call | Use it when |
|---|---|
| ==Mayday== (said 3 times) | ==Grave and imminent danger== to a person or the boat: sinking, fire, a life-threatening injury |
| ==Pan-Pan== (said 3 times) | ==Urgent, but not immediately life-threatening==: engine failure drifting toward a shipping lane |
| ==Sécurité== (said 3 times) | ==Safety information==, such as a log adrift or a weather warning |

To use a marine VHF radio in Canada, you must hold a ==Restricted Operator Certificate (Maritime)==, called the ==ROC(M)==. Your PCOC does not cover it. You get it by passing a separate exam, usually after a short course.

If your DSC radio sends a false alert, cancel it at once on channel 16.

## Cellphones

A cellphone is ==not a substitute for a marine radio==. It may lose signal or get wet. Only the person you call can hear you. Nearby boats cannot.

Dialling ==\*16== connects to the nearest Coast Guard radio centre ==on some networks only==. Check with your provider before you rely on it.

## Fire on board

{{figure:svmo-fire-triangle}}

A fire needs three things: ==heat, fuel and oxygen==. Take away any one and the fire goes out.

Fire classes on a boat:

- ==Class A==: wood, paper, cloth, rubber and plastic.
- ==Class B==: flammable liquids such as ==fuel, oil and grease==.
- ==Class C==: ==live electrical equipment==.

Pleasure craft extinguishers are rated like ==5B:C== or ==10B:C==. The letters are the fire classes it can fight. A ==bigger number== means it can put out a bigger fire.

If a fire starts:

1. Make sure everyone is ==wearing a lifejacket==.
2. Turn the boat so the ==fire is downwind==. Then stop the engine if you can.
3. Use the extinguisher: pull the pin, ==aim at the base of the flames==, squeeze, and ==sweep side to side==.
4. Keep sweeping for a few seconds after the flames go out. A fire can flare up again.
5. If the fire is too big, call Mayday and get ready to leave the boat.

Check each extinguisher ==monthly==. Take it off its bracket and shake it hard. If the powder inside does not seem to move, replace it.

## Running aground

If you hit bottom:

- Stop the engine.
- Check that ==everyone is all right== and wearing a lifejacket.
- ==Check for leaks and damage.==
- Do not just put it in reverse and rev hard. Check the bottom first. Prop wash can pile sand up around you, and the engine can overheat.
- If the hull is holed, you may sink once you float free. Make sure the boat will stay afloat first.
- On the coast, a rising tide may lift you off. Set an anchor out to stop you being pushed further on.
- If you are holed or in danger, ==call for help==.

## Helping others

If you see a distress signal or hear a Mayday, you must ==go to help== if you can do so ==without putting your own boat and people in danger==. Tell the Coast Guard what you see. Do not talk over a distress call on channel 16 unless you are helping.

## A BC example

Sam is crossing the Strait of Georgia toward Gabriola. Black smoke pours from the engine hatch. Sam has everyone put on lifejackets. He turns the bow into the wind so the fire and smoke blow away from everyone, then stops the engine. He keys the radio on channel 16: "Mayday, Mayday, Mayday." He gives the boat's name, position, the fire, and that four people are aboard. His friend puts out the fire with the 10B:C extinguisher, aiming at the base and sweeping. The Coast Guard asks a nearby sailboat to stand by until Sam is towed in.

## Common mistakes

- Carrying flares that are past their 4-year date.
- Testing flares on the long weekend "for fun".
- Owning a VHF radio but no ROC(M).
- Relying only on a cellphone in the Strait.
- Aiming the extinguisher at the top of the flames.
