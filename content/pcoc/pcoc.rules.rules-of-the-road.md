# Rules of the road for pleasure boaters

There are no painted lanes or traffic lights on the water. Instead, every boat follows the ==Collision Regulations==, from a kayak to a cruise ship. The rules only work if you ==see the other boat in time==.

## Keep a lookout

Keep a ==proper lookout== at all times. Use your eyes and your ears, and any other help you have, such as radar. Look all around, not just ahead. The driver should not be busy with a phone or a fishing line.

## Safe speed

Always go at a ==safe speed==: a speed at which you can stop or turn in time. There is no single number. It depends on how far you can see, how much traffic there is, how fast your boat stops and turns, wind, waves and current, and nearby dangers. At night, bright shore lights can hide other boats. In fog, rain, smoke or darkness, ==slow is the only safe speed==. You are ==responsible for your wake== and any harm it causes.

## Is there a risk of collision?

Watch the other boat against a fixed point on your boat, or take a compass bearing. If the ==bearing does not change== while the boat gets closer, you are on a collision course. If you are not sure, assume there is a risk. Act ==early== and make your change ==big enough to be seen==. Slowing down or stopping is also good action.

## Who gives way?

The ==give-way== boat keeps out of the way. It acts early and keeps well clear. The ==stand-on== boat keeps her ==course and speed==. But if a collision is close and the give-way boat does nothing, the stand-on boat must act too.

{{figure:head-on-situation}}

**Head-on.** Two power-driven boats meet nearly bow to bow. ==Both turn to starboard== and pass ==port to port==.

{{figure:crossing-situation}}

**Crossing.** Two power-driven boats cross. The boat that has the other on her ==starboard side== gives way. Turn to starboard or slow down, and pass astern of her.

{{figure:overtaking-situation}}

**Overtaking.** You are overtaking if you come up from ==more than 22.5 degrees abaft the beam== of the other boat. At night you would see only her white stern light. ==The overtaking boat keeps clear== until she is well past.

## Power, sail and paddle

A ==power-driven== boat keeps out of the way of:

- a vessel ==not under command== (she cannot steer, for example after a breakdown)
- a vessel ==restricted in her ability to manoeuvre== (for example, dredging)
- a vessel ==engaged in fishing== with nets or gear that limit her turning (trolling lines do not count)
- a ==sailing vessel== under sail

A sailboat keeps out of the way of the first three. A sailboat using her engine counts as a power-driven boat.

Between two sailboats, if the wind is on different sides, the boat with the ==wind on her port side gives way==. If the wind is on the same side, the boat to ==windward== keeps clear.

The rules give canoes, kayaks and paddleboards no special place in this list. They are low and hard to see. A powerboat should slow down and give them room. Paddlers should wear bright colours and never assume they are seen.

## Narrow channels and large vessels

In a ==narrow channel==, keep to the ==starboard side==. A boat ==under 20 m==, or any sailboat, ==must not impede== a vessel that can only navigate safely inside the channel. Ferries, ships and tugs need a long way to stop. Never cross ahead of them, and ==never pass between a tug and its tow==. In Canada, a boat crossing a river keeps out of the way of a power-driven vessel going up or down it.

## Sound signals

{{figure:svop-sound-signals}}

A ==short blast== lasts ==about 1 second==. A ==prolonged blast== lasts ==4 to 6 seconds==. When boats are in sight of each other, a power-driven vessel signals:

- ==1 short==: I am turning to starboard.
- ==2 short==: I am turning to port.
- ==3 short==: I am going astern.
- ==5 or more short and rapid==: I doubt your intentions. For a small boat, it means get out of the way now.

In fog, a power-driven vessel ==making way== sounds ==1 prolonged== blast at least every ==2 minutes==. Stopped, she sounds 2 prolonged. A sailboat sounds 1 prolonged and 2 short. A boat under 12 m may use another effective sound instead, at least every 2 minutes.

A pleasure craft up to 12 m must carry an approved whistle or horn, or a ==sound-signalling device==: a ==pealess whistle==, or a ==compressed-gas or electric horn==. Canoes and kayaks need one too.

## A BC example

You are driving a 6 m runabout up the Fraser River on a hazy afternoon. A tug pushing a gravel barge comes downriver in the channel. You slow down and keep to your own starboard side. Then you hear five short, rapid blasts: the tug is not sure what you are doing. You turn clearly to starboard so she can see your plan and wait for her to pass. Then you cross well behind her, clear of her wake.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking the stand-on boat may hold course no matter what.
- Cutting across the bow of a ferry or tug because "I have the right of way."
- Making a small, slow turn that the other boat cannot see.
- Driving fast in fog or at night.
- Carrying a whistle with a pea, which can jam when wet.

## Check yourself

1. A powerboat is crossing from your starboard side. Who gives way?
2. What does one short blast mean?
3. Why must a small boat not impede a large vessel in a narrow channel?
