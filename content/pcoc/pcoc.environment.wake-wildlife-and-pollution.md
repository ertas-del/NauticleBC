# Wake, wildlife and clean water

BC has some of the busiest and most beautiful water in Canada. Your boat can harm it. This lesson covers your wake, the shoreline speed limit, what may and may not go over the side, and how to share the water with whales.

## Your wake

A wake is the wave your boat makes. It can rock a kayak over, throw people off a dock, hurt a swimmer, and wash away a shoreline.

You are ==responsible for any damage or injury your wake causes==. The law also says you must not operate a boat ==without due care and attention== or ==without reasonable consideration for other persons==.

Good habits:

- Slow down near docks, marinas, swimmers, paddlers and divers.
- Look behind you. See what your wake is doing.
- Think about your wake when you choose your speed.

## Speed near shore in BC

The ==Vessel Operation Restriction Regulations== (VORR) set boating limits for Canadian waters. Some limits cover a whole province. Others cover one lake or river.

In BC, the province-wide rule is this: on ==rivers and lakes==, a power-driven or electric boat must not go faster than ==10 km/h within 30 m of shore==. It applies ==whether or not there is a sign==.

This rule does ==not== cover BC's coastal waters. On the coast, watch for local speed limits and always use a safe speed.

The 10 km/h rule does not apply:

- when towing a skier straight out from shore (at a right angle to it);
- on rivers under 100 m wide, in canals and in buoyed channels;
- where a different posted speed limit is set for that water.

Many BC lakes also have their own limits, such as no motors, a power limit, or set hours for towing. These are listed by name in the VORR schedules. Only authorized signs can set these limits.

## Sewage and grey water

If your boat has a toilet, it must have a ==holding tank== or an approved ==marine sanitation device==. Empty a holding tank at a ==pump-out station==.

- In a ==lake or river==, untreated sewage must ==never== go into the water.
- In a ==designated sewage area==, use a pump-out. In BC these include Okanagan, Shuswap, Kalamalka and Christina lakes, and coastal spots such as Montague Harbour, Smuggler Cove, Prideaux Haven and Squirrel Cove.
- On the rest of the BC coast, a small boat may discharge untreated sewage only ==3 nautical miles or more from shore==, while under way. Narrow waters, less than 6 nautical miles from shore to shore, have extra rules. The simple, legal choice anywhere is a pump-out.

Grey water is drain water from sinks and showers. It must not leave solids or a sheen on the water. Use shore washrooms, and use little soap.

## Fuel and oil

Fuel must ==not leak== from your boat. Oil and fuel must not go overboard.

- Do not overfill the tank. Wipe up spills with an absorbent pad.
- Run the bilge pump only when the bilge holds ==only water==. Never pump oily bilge water overboard.
- Bilge cleaners do not remove oil. They only break it into small drops.
- If you cause or see a spill, ==report it== to the Canadian Coast Guard.

## Garbage

Garbage is a pollutant under the law. ==Never throw garbage overboard.== That includes cigarette butts, cans and fishing line. ==Bring it all back to shore.==

## Invasive species: Clean, Drain, Dry

Zebra and quagga mussels and other invasive species ride from lake to lake on boats, trailers and gear. BC works hard to keep them out. Before you move to new water:

- ==Clean==: remove all plants, animals and mud, on dry land.
- ==Drain==: ==pull the drain plug== and empty the bilge, live well and buckets before you leave the launch.
- ==Dry==: let every part dry completely before it goes in other water.

If you are moving any boat in BC, even a ==canoe, kayak or paddleboard==, you ==must stop at every open inspection station== on your route. Not stopping can bring a fine.

## Whales and other marine mammals

It is against the law to ==disturb== a marine mammal. That includes feeding it, swimming with it, chasing it, cutting between a mother and calf, or trapping it against the shore.

Legal approach distances in BC:

| Animal | Stay at least |
|---|---|
| Whales, dolphins, porpoises | ==100 m== |
| Any of them resting or with a calf | ==200 m== |
| ==Killer whales== (all BC waters) | ==200 m== |
| ==Southern Resident killer whales== (southern BC, June 1, 2026 to May 31, 2027) | ==1,000 m== |

You often cannot tell which killer whales you are seeing. Fisheries and Oceans Canada asks boaters in southern BC to stay 1,000 m from any killer whale in that case. Some areas near Pender and Saturna islands are ==closed to boats== from June 1 to November 30. These rules change each year, so ==check the current rules before you go==.

If a whale surfaces close to you, put the engine in ==neutral== and let it pass. Move away slowly.

## A BC example

Priya is crossing Howe Sound in her 6 m runabout. She sees black fins and a tall dorsal fin 600 m ahead. She cannot tell which group of killer whales it is. She slows down, turns away and stays more than 1,000 m off. Later she comes into Snug Cove at slow speed so her wake does not rock the boats on the dock.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking the 10 km/h rule applies only where there is a sign.
- Pumping the bilge without checking for oil.
- Trailering from one lake to another with the drain plug in and weeds on the trailer.
- Driving past an inspection station with a kayak on the roof.
- Following whales to get a better photo.
