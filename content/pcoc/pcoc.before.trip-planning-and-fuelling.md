# Trip planning and fuelling

Running out of fuel, getting caught by wind, and fuel fires can all be prevented before you leave the dock.

## Check the weather

{{figure:svop-wind-warnings}}

Get the ==marine forecast== before you go. You can hear it on ==VHF radio== or read it ==online== from Environment and Climate Change Canada. Marine forecasts give wind in ==knots==. Keep checking while you are out, because weather can change fast.

Know the ==wind warnings==:

- ==Strong wind warning==: ==20 to 33 knots==. Dangerous for small boats.
- ==Gale warning==: ==34 to 47 knots==.
- ==Storm warning==: ==48 to 63 knots==.
- ==Hurricane force wind warning==: ==64 knots== or more.

If a warning is out and you are in a small boat, stay in. If the sky darkens fast, head for shelter.

## Tides and currents in BC

{{figure:svop-wind-against-tide}}

The BC coast has large ==tides== and strong ==tidal currents==. In most places on the coast, the water rises and falls about twice a day. A rock that is deep at high tide may be just under the surface at low tide.

In narrow passes and rapids the current can be very strong. Small boats should go through near ==slack water==, when the current is weakest. Slack water does not always happen at the same time as high or low tide, so use the ==current tables==, not just the tide tables.

==Wind against the current== makes ==short, steep, breaking waves==. Plan to cross when the wind and the current go the same way.

The ==Canadian Hydrographic Service== publishes tide and current predictions online and in the Canadian Tide and Current Tables. Check them for your area before you go.

## Leave a trip plan

A ==trip plan==, also called a ==sail plan==, tells someone where you are going and when you will be back. File one for ==every trip==, even a short one.

Write down:

- your name, phone number and an emergency contact,
- the boat's name, licence number, type, length and colour,
- the safety gear on board and any radio channels you listen to,
- how many people are on board,
- where you leave from, your route, stops and when you expect to arrive.

Leave it with ==someone you trust==. If you are ==overdue==, they call a ==Joint Rescue Coordination Centre (JRCC)==. When you get back, ==close the plan==. Call your contact. If you forget, a search may start for nothing.

## The pre-departure check

Before you leave, check:

- one approved lifejacket or PFD of the right size for each person,
- all safety gear on board, working and easy to reach,
- the forecast, the tides and your charts,
- the hull, steering, oil, hoses, clamps, battery and ==drain plug==,
- the fuel, using the one-third rule,
- the load, spread evenly.

Then give everyone a ==safety briefing== on where the gear is and how to use it.

## Plan your fuel

Engine trouble, such as running out of fuel, is a common reason boaters call for help. Use the ==one-third rule==:

- ==one third== of your fuel to go ==out==,
- ==one third== to come ==back==,
- ==one third== in ==reserve==.

The reserve covers wind, a detour or a long wait.

## Refuel safely

{{figure:svop-fuelling-steps}}

Gasoline fumes can collect inside the boat, and one spark can set them off. Follow these steps:

1. ==Moor the boat== securely. ==Shut off the engine== and turn off electrical switches.
2. Get your ==passengers off== the boat.
3. ==No smoking==, no flames.
4. ==Close== windows, hatches, ports and cabin doors.
5. Take ==portable tanks off the boat== and fill them ==on the dock or shore==.
6. Keep the nozzle ==touching the filler pipe== while you fill.
7. ==Do not overfill==. Wipe up any spill.
8. Open up the boat to air it out.
9. For an ==inboard gasoline engine==, run the ==blower for at least 4 minutes== before you start the engine.
10. ==Sniff== the engine space. If you smell fuel, do not start the engine. Find the leak.

## Carbon monoxide

==Carbon monoxide== (CO) is a gas in engine and generator exhaust. It has ==no colour, smell or taste==, and it can kill within minutes.

Early signs are ==headache==, ==nausea==, ==dizziness== and ==itchy eyes==. People often mistake them for seasickness.

- Exhaust can be pulled back into the boat at low speed or by a tailwind. This is called the ==station wagon effect==.
- CO builds up under swim platforms and between pontoons.
- ==Never sit, hang on or teak surf== on the ==swim platform or back deck== while an engine or generator is running.
- Fit a ==marine-grade CO alarm==, test it, and check its battery before each trip.

## A BC example

Lee plans a day trip from Horseshoe Bay up Howe Sound in a 6 m runabout. The forecast has a strong wind warning for the afternoon, so he plans to be home by 1 pm. The run up the Sound will take about 15 litres and the run back about 15 more. With the one-third rule he leaves with at least 45 litres: 15 out, 15 back and 15 in reserve. He gives his sister a trip plan with his route and return time. At the fuel dock his passengers step off and he closes the hatches before filling. He runs the blower, sniffs, and only then starts up. Back on the trailer, he calls his sister.

## Common mistakes

- Leaving with "enough" fuel for the trip out but not the trip back.
- Filling a portable tank while it sits in the boat.
- Starting an inboard gasoline engine without running the blower.
- Forgetting to close the trip plan.
