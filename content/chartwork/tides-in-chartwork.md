# Using tide heights in pilotage

**Study aid only. Not for navigation.** All ports, heights and times are invented. Real tide data comes from the current CHS publications.

## What you need to know

Two lessons already give you the tools. Lesson 2 (chart datums) shows how charted depth and the height of tide add up to depth of water, and how a bridge clearance is measured from a high-water level. The tides lesson on tables (tides 5) shows reference ports, secondary-port corrections and the rule of twelfths. This lesson puts the tools to work on a clock. The question is never "what is the tide?" It is ==what will the tide be when I am there, and how long does the water last?==

One note for the exam. A study guide for the CP1 exam says tide calculations have not so far appeared on the paper. Treat that as a hint, not a promise. The oral examiner and a real passage will ask for them.

## The checklist

1. Choose the place and the clearance wanted.
2. Work out the depth (or height) the tide must provide: ==tide needed = draught + wanted clearance − charted depth==.
3. Find the tide table for the right port and year, correct for a secondary port, and convert the table's time to your clock: the CHS tables use standard time, so ==add one hour in daylight saving time==.
4. Work out your ETA at the place from distance ÷ speed made good (lesson 20).
5. Find the height of tide at that time by the rule of twelfths or the method in the tables.
6. Compare, add a margin, decide, and write the decision on the plan.

## Worked example 1 (invented): earliest time to cross

A shoal is charted at 1.8 m. Draught is 2.1 m and you want 0.6 m under the keel. The tide needed is 2.1 + 0.6 − 1.8 = ==0.9 m==.

At the invented port, low water is at 0630 (0.5 m) and high water at 1230 (3.7 m). The range is 3.2 m and one twelfth is 0.267 m. The rise needed is 0.9 − 0.5 = 0.4 m, which is 1.5 twelfths. By the rule of twelfths the first hour gives 1 twelfth and the second 2 twelfths, so 1.5 twelfths is reached 15 minutes into the second hour: 1 h 15 min after low water, ==0745==.

Check: at 0745 the tide is about 0.9 m, so depth = 1.8 + 0.9 = 2.7 m and the keel clearance is 0.6 m. That is the minimum, not a target. A prudent skipper would cross an hour later.

## Worked example 2: the other end of the window

The next low water is at 1850 (0.6 m). The fall from 1230 is 3.1 m, 0.258 m a twelfth. The tide must not fall below 0.9 m, a fall of 2.8 m, which is 10.8 twelfths. The rule of twelfths reaches 9 twelfths after 4 periods and 11 after 5, so the time is 4.9 periods of 63 minutes after 1230: about 5 h 12 min, ==1742==.

The window is about ==0745 to 1742==. The lesson of a window is that the end is at least as important as the start. If you cross at 1600 and then something delays you, the tide is leaving. It is the falling tide that takes the water from under you.

## Why falling is the dangerous direction

A vessel that touches on a rising tide has water coming to her. In an hour there will be more. A vessel that touches on a falling tide has less each hour and may sit aground until the next flood. Even on a rising tide a hard grounding can damage the hull or the propeller. So the rule is: ==pass shoals on the rising tide, or well before the end of the window on a falling one==. If a bay dries out, a vessel left on the bottom needs a plan for how she will lie, and that is an oral topic, not a chart one.

## Worked example 3: ETA matters

Your anchorage is 14 nm from the shoal. At SMG 7.0 knots the run takes 2 h 00 min. If you want to cross at 1100, you leave at ==0900==. If a foul current drops the SMG to 6.0 knots, the run takes 2 h 20 min, and you reach the shoal at 1120 instead. That is fine here, but if the window closed at 1130, the extra 20 minutes would be most of your margin. Recompute your ETA at each fix and ask whether the window is still open.

## Worked example 4 (invented): a low bridge

The chart gives a clearance of 12.0 m above HHWLT. The tables give HHWLT as 4.8 m above chart datum. Your mast is 14.0 m above the waterline, and you want 1.0 m of margin, so you need 15.0 m of clearance.

Clearance now = 12.0 + (4.8 − tide). To have 15.0 m you need the tide at or below 4.8 − 3.0 = ==1.8 m==.

On a day when high water is 0940 (4.2 m) and the next low is 1550 (0.6 m), the fall reaches 1.8 m after 8 twelfths of the 3.6 m range, which the rule of twelfths puts at ==1326==. The tide climbs back to 1.8 m at about ==1814==. So the invented window under the bridge is ==1326 to about 1814==, widest at low water. At the highest tide of 4.2 m the clearance is 12.0 + 0.6 = 12.6 m and you would not fit.

Notice that the shoal and the bridge want opposite ends of the tide. A boat with a deep keel and a tall mast may find the window is narrow or does not exist on some days.

## At anchor

Anchoring in a tidal bay needs two heights. For the ==swinging circle==, check the depth at ==low water== (will she touch the bottom or a charted rock?). For the ==scope==, which you pay out against the ==high water== depth, check that the length of chain you have is enough for the highest tide you expect. A boat that anchors at low water with 5:1 scope on the depth at the time may find at high water that the scope is only 3:1. This is the bridge between this lesson and the anchoring lesson in seamanship.

## BC example

A skipper wants to leave a drying bay in the Gulf Islands for the next anchorage. She works backward from the time the water reaches the charted channel depth at the bay mouth, and adds the run to the first shoal at her SMG. The tide tables are for a reference port 15 nm away, with a secondary-port correction for her bay, and she makes it in standard time then adds the hour for daylight saving. She sees that, with a morning low water, the window opens late. She departs later and takes the shoal at mid-rise rather than at the first moment. She has also noted the wind: an onshore wind can raise the water above the table and an offshore wind can lower it, and she looks at the staff gauge as she goes.

## Common mistakes

- Reading the tide at the time of planning rather than the time of arrival.
- Forgetting the daylight saving hour, or applying it twice.
- Using the height of the tide as the depth. Depth = charted depth + tide.
- Adding a drying height instead of subtracting it.
- Forgetting that the best clearance under a bridge is at low water, and that the charted figure is the clearance at the high-water datum.
- Reading the rule of twelfths as exact.
- Crossing at the first moment the water is deep enough, with no margin.
