# Chart datum, depths, drying heights and heights

**Study aid only. Not for navigation.** Every number here is invented. Tide heights in real life come from the current CHS tables.

## What you need to know

A chart number means nothing until you know what it is measured from. There are two reference levels to learn:

- ==Chart datum== is a ==low-water level==. ==Depths (soundings) and drying heights== are measured from it.
- A ==high-water datum== is used for ==heights of lights, islands and vertical clearances== (bridges, overhead cables) in tidal waters. In Canadian tidal waters this is ==higher high water, large tide (HHWLT)==.

In Canada, chart datum in tidal waters is ==lower low water, large tide (LLWLT)==, an older name for which is ==lowest normal tide (LNT)==. It is a low level chosen so that the water ==seldom falls below it==. Notice **seldom**: it can. A strong wind, high pressure or a river in low flow can pull the water below datum, so real depth may be less than charted. The chart title always states the datums, so read it.

## The three heights you add to the chart

Tide tables give a ==height of tide above chart datum==. That is why they combine so easily with chart numbers:

==Depth of water = charted depth + height of tide==
==Water under the keel = depth of water − draught==

For a drying height (the seabed that dries out at low tide, shown as an underlined figure on the chart):

==Depth over a drying area = height of tide − drying height==

If that comes out negative, the ground is still dry.

For overhead clearance (a bridge or power cable), the chart gives the clearance above the high-water datum. When the tide is lower than that level, the gap is bigger:

==Clearance now = charted clearance + (height of HHWLT above chart datum − present height of tide)==

The tide tables give the height of the high-water level you need for the place (invented in the examples below).

## Worked examples (invented numbers)

### Example 1: depth and water under the keel

Charted depth 3.2 m. Height of tide at the time 2.1 m. Draught 1.8 m.

1. Depth of water = 3.2 + 2.1 = ==5.3 m==.
2. Water under the keel = 5.3 − 1.8 = ==3.5 m==.

### Example 2: depth over a drying rock

Underlined figure 1.2 on the chart (the rock dries 1.2 m above chart datum). Height of tide 2.6 m.

1. Depth over the rock = 2.6 − 1.2 = ==1.4 m==.
2. Draught 1.8 m, so the keel would hit. Even 1.4 m of water covers it, so ==do not pass over it== at this tide.

### Example 3: when is there enough water?

Charted depth 1.5 m. Draught 1.6 m. You want at least 0.5 m under the keel.

1. Water needed = 1.6 + 0.5 = 2.1 m.
2. Tide needed = 2.1 − 1.5 = ==0.6 m== above chart datum, at the very least.
3. You would also want a margin because the table is a prediction.

### Example 4: a charted sounding that is in brackets or underlined

If a number is **underlined** it is a drying height, not a depth. It means ground standing above datum. Treat an underlined 0.9 and a plain 0.9 as opposites: one is 0.9 m of ground above datum, the other is 0.9 m of water below datum.

### Example 5: bridge clearance

Charted vertical clearance 12.0 m above HHWLT. The height of HHWLT in the tables at this place is 4.8 m above chart datum. The present height of tide is 1.5 m. The top of your mast, with aerial, is 14.0 m above the waterline.

1. How much lower is the water than the HHWLT level? 4.8 − 1.5 = 3.3 m.
2. Clearance now = 12.0 + 3.3 = ==15.3 m==.
3. Gap above the mast = 15.3 − 14.0 = ==1.3 m==.

Looks fine, but a safe skipper also checks the tide is not about to be higher than predicted and allows a margin.

## Charted depths are the least you would expect

Charted depths use a low datum on purpose, so you normally find ==at least that much water==. The words "normally" and "seldom" matter in an exam. Depth can still be less than charted when the water level is pushed below datum, or if the seabed has changed since the survey. Also check the **source diagram** on a chart: old or sparse surveys mean unseen dangers.

## BC practical example

You want to anchor in a bay with a charted 2.0 m patch. Draught is 1.4 m. Low water that evening is predicted at 0.5 m above chart datum. At low water the depth is 2.0 + 0.5 = 2.5 m, so you float with 1.1 m under the keel. You would still allow for swinging into shallower water and for an error in the tide. The tide rises about 3 m by morning, so the water would be 5.5 m deep, more than ample.

## Exam traps

- Adding a drying height to the tide instead of subtracting it.
- Treating a bridge clearance as measured from chart datum rather than from the high-water datum.
- Using height of tide from the wrong reference, or forgetting daylight saving on the table time.
- Taking charted depth as a guarantee. Winds and air pressure shift the real level.
- Mixing metres and fathoms because the chart is old.
