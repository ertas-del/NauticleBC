# Voyage planning and the log book

**Study aid only. Not for navigation.** The passage, depths and tide below are invented. A real plan uses current charts, tide tables and weather.

## What you need to know

Most groundings on a coast are not caused by a failure of seamanship in the moment. They are caused by a plan that was not made, or that was made and not followed. Canadian regulations make the plan a duty of the master, and the content of a good plan follows the IMO guidelines.

## The legal duties

Under the ==Navigation Safety Regulations, 2020==:

- ==s. 144(1)==: ==before the vessel embarks on a voyage the master must plan it==, taking into account the Annex to ==IMO Resolution A.893(21), Guidelines for Voyage Planning==, and using the charts, documents and publications required by s. 142 to the extent they relate to planning.
- ==s. 144(2)==: the route must take into account:
  1. any relevant ==routing systems==;
  2. ==sufficient sea room== for safe passage;
  3. all known ==navigational hazards and adverse weather conditions==;
  4. any ==marine environmental protection measures== that apply; and
  5. actions and activities that could ==damage the environment==, and the measures to avoid them.
- ==s. 144(3)==: ==display the route on a chart== (the required largest-scale chart), and ==plot and monitor the vessel's position throughout the voyage== on it.
- ==s. 145==: the vessel must be fitted with the accessories for using the chart, for checking compass accuracy, and binoculars.
- ==s. 146==: the charts and publications must be ==corrected from Notices to Mariners or a navigational warning== before they are used to plan and execute a voyage.

Under the ==Marine Personnel Regulations, s. 213==, the master ==shall ensure that the intended voyage is planned and that a deck watch is maintained== in accordance with Parts 2, 3 and 3-1 of section A-VIII/2 of the STCW Code (subject to s. 252).

## The four stages (A.893(21))

1. ==Appraisal==: gather and weigh the information. The vessel (condition, stability, equipment, manoeuvring data), cargo, crew fitness, certificates, charts and notices, sailing directions, lists of lights and radio aids, tides and tidal streams, weather, traffic. The outcome is a picture of the danger areas, the safe areas, and the areas with special rules.
2. ==Planning==: lay the track on suitable charts, plot true courses and mark the danger areas. The plan covers the ==whole voyage berth to berth==, including pilotage waters. Set out ==safe speed, minimum under-keel clearance, course alteration points, the position-fixing method and frequency== (a primary and a secondary method, with attention to areas where accuracy matters most) and ==contingency plans== (where to go for deep water, a port of refuge or an anchorage). The ==master approves== the plan and its details before sailing.
3. ==Execution==: carry out the plan when departure and arrival times can be estimated well, taking account of equipment reliability, tide heights and streams at critical points, weather, visibility, and the effect of day or night on the accuracy of fixing.
4. ==Monitoring==: follow the plan against the actual progress, continuously. The plan is kept ==available at all times on the bridge==. Changes are made in line with the guidelines and are clearly marked and recorded.

In practice, many mariners add common devices to the plan: clearing bearings (lesson 15) for each danger, parallel indexing where radar is fitted, and abort points where a decision to turn back must be taken. These are good practice rather than quotations from the Resolution.

## Worked example (invented): a three-leg passage with a tide window

Draught 1.6 m. The master wants at least 0.5 m under the keel. The speed is 8.0 knots.

| Leg | From to | Distance | True course | Time at 8 knots |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | A to B | 11.5 nm | 312° | 86 min |
| 2 | B to C | 8.0 nm | 275° | 60 min |
| 3 | C to D | 6.2 nm | 330° | 47 min |

Total: 25.7 nm in 193 min (3 h 13 min).

**The constraint.** Midway along leg 2 there is a shoal patch charted at ==1.2 m==. Tide needed = draught + clearance − charted depth = 1.6 + 0.5 − 1.2 = ==0.9 m== above chart datum.

**The tide.** Low water at 0700 is 0.4 m; high water at 1300 is 3.9 m. By the rule of twelfths (range 3.5 m, one twelfth 0.29 m):

| Time | 0700 | 0800 | 0900 | 1000 | 1100 | 1200 | 1300 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Height (m) | 0.40 | 0.69 | 1.28 | 2.15 | 3.03 | 3.61 | 3.90 |

The tide passes 0.9 m about 21 minutes after 0800, at about 0821. That is the earliest, with no margin. The rule of twelfths is rough and the prediction itself can be wrong, so plan to cross after 0900, when the tide is at least 1.28 m, or about 0.4 m above the minimum.

**The timing.** To cross the shoal at about 0926, working back: the shoal is 30 minutes after B, B is 86 minutes after A, so leave A at ==0730==. Check: B at 0856, shoal at 0926, C at 0956, D at about 1042. At 0926 the tide is about 1.65 m, so the clearance under the keel is 1.2 + 1.65 − 1.6 = ==1.25 m==, much more than the 0.5 m minimum.

Leaving at 0645 would put the shoal at 0841, when the tide is about 1.09 m and the clearance is 1.2 + 1.09 − 1.6 = 0.69 m: acceptable by arithmetic but with no margin for tide or weather. The window in the plan is an interval, not a point.

**The rest of the plan.** Write the compass courses (true to compass for each heading, lesson 8), the fixing plan (a fix every 15 minutes on leg 2 and every 30 minutes elsewhere, bearings of the tower and light as primary, radar ranges as secondary, depth contours as a check), a clearing bearing for a reef off the end of leg 2, a contingency (a bay at B in which to anchor if the weather worsens), and the sources checked (charts corrected to the latest Notices to Mariners, weather forecast, tide table for the standard port).

## The log book

The competence in the draft TP 2293 includes keeping a ==log book and a record of compass errors==. A log book is how the master and the next watch know what happened, and how they prove it. A good log entry names the time, the position (fix, DR or EP and how it was obtained), the course and speed, any change, weather and visibility, and anything unusual. Compass-error checks (lesson 9) are entered with date, time, heading, true and compass bearings and the resulting error. The deviation card is rebuilt from these observations.

## BC example

A skipper plans a run from a marina to a sheltered bay up the coast. Her chart is the largest scale available, corrected to the last Notices to Mariners. She marks the track in pencil with the courses and distances, finds a drying reef near the second leg, calculates the tide needed to cross a shoal, checks the forecast for winds against the strong current in a pass, and notes the nearest anchorages. She tells a friend ashore the route and ETA. During the trip she fixes her position every 20 minutes and writes the fix, the time and the bearing on the plan. This is a voyage plan in the sense of s. 144, scaled to a small vessel.

## Exam traps

- Saying the plan is optional on small vessels. Section 144 applies to the master of a vessel.
- Planning with an uncorrected chart or an old edition.
- Taking the tide needed as the height of the draught, forgetting that the charted depth and the clearance are part of the sum.
- Planning to cross at the earliest moment the tide allows, with no margin.
- Not displaying the route or not plotting the position: both are required (s. 144(3)).
- Treating A.893(21) as a form to fill in instead of a process of four stages.
