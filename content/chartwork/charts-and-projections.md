# Charts, projections and scale

**Study aid only. Not for navigation.** All numbers here are invented. Real navigation uses current official charts, corrected.

## What you need to know

The earth is round and a chart is flat, so every chart distorts something. The skill is knowing what your chart keeps true. For coastal work in BC you will mostly use the ==Mercator== projection, and you will meet the ==gnomonic== projection only as a planning tool for long ocean tracks.

## Mercator: the working chart

Picture the meridians (lines of longitude) drawn as straight parallel lines, as if the globe were unrolled. To stop the chart looking squashed sideways, the spacing between the parallels of latitude is stretched by the same ratio as the meridians were stretched. The result:

- A ==rhumb line== (a line cutting every meridian at the same angle, in other words a steady course) is a ==straight line==. You can lay a course with parallel rules and read it off the compass rose.
- Angles are true. The projection is ==conformal==, so shapes of small areas look right.
- The ==latitude scale grows toward the poles==. A degree of latitude is drawn longer at 55°N than at 48°N. For that reason the poles cannot be shown at all.

Because the distance scale changes with latitude, you measure distance on the ==latitude scale at the side of the chart, level with the part you are measuring==. One minute of latitude is one nautical mile. Never use the longitude scale along the bottom for distance (lesson 3 covers this).

## Gnomonic: straight great circles

On a gnomonic chart a ==great circle== (the shortest path between two places on a sphere) is a ==straight line==. It is neither conformal nor equal-area, and the distance scale changes quickly, so you cannot lay courses on it. Ocean navigators draw the great-circle line on a gnomonic chart, pick points along it, and ==transfer those points to a Mercator chart==, joining them with short rhumb lines. In BC coastal work you will rarely need this; know what it is for.

## Scale

Scale compares a length on the chart with the same length on the ground. It is shown as a ==representative fraction== such as 1:50 000, in words, or as a graphic bar.

- ==Large scale==: a small area shown in detail (harbour plan, 1:10 000 or 1:20 000).
- ==Small scale==: a large area with less detail (offshore chart, 1:350 000 or smaller).

The trap: **1:20 000 is larger scale than 1:80 000**, even though 20 000 is the smaller number.

### Worked example 1: chart length to distance

Chart scale 1:40 000. You measure 7.5 cm between two points.

1. Ground distance = 7.5 cm × 40 000 = 300 000 cm.
2. 300 000 cm = 3000 m.
3. A nautical mile is ==1852 m==, so 3000 ÷ 1852 = ==1.62 nm==.

Check on the chart: you would not normally do this. You would lift the distance on dividers and read it on the latitude scale. The sum is for understanding scale, and for charts where no latitude scale is handy.

### Worked example 2: how big is 1 cm?

Scale 1:50 000. 1 cm × 50 000 = 50 000 cm = 500 m. So ==1 cm is 500 m==. A nautical mile (1852 m) is 1852 ÷ 500 = 3.7 cm long on this chart.

### Worked example 3: which chart shows more detail?

Chart A is 1:25 000. Chart B is 1:150 000. The same 2 nm channel is 2 × 1852 = 3704 m, which is 14.8 cm on chart A and only 2.5 cm on chart B. Chart A is the larger scale and shows far more detail of the channel.

## Title block, edition and corrections

Read the chart's title block before you trust it. It names the area and scale, the projection, the units and the ==datums== for depths and heights (lesson 2), and the source of the survey. Look for the **edition date** and the record of corrections. The printed information is only up to date to the date shown. ==You are responsible for applying later corrections== from Notices to Mariners.

## What Canada requires you to carry

Under the Navigation Safety Regulations, 2020 (s. 142) the master and authorized representative must keep on board the ==most recent versions== of the ==reference catalogue and the applicable charts in their largest scale== for each area where the vessel is scheduled to be navigated, together with the ==annual edition of Notices to Mariners==. For vessels in Canadian waters the list also includes Canadian Sailing Directions, Canadian Tide and Current Tables and the List of Lights, Buoys and Fog Signals (lesson 23 covers publications). Charts ==may be electronic== only when displayed on an ECDIS meeting the conditions in s. 143.

A vessel of ==less than 100 gross tonnage== need not comply with s. 142(1) if the person in charge of navigation has sufficient knowledge of the charted routes, lights, buoys and marks and hazards, and of the usual tides, currents, ice and weather, so that safety is not compromised (s. 142(3)). That is a knowledge test, not permission to go without a chart.

Section 146: the charts, documents and publications ==must be correct and up to date, from Notices to Mariners or a navigational warning, before being used to plan and execute a voyage==.

## A BC example

A skipper plans a run across the Strait of Georgia and picks up a small-scale chart that shows the whole strait on one sheet. She uses it to see the overall route, then switches to the large-scale chart of the destination approaches to find the buoys and shoals. Rule: ==use the largest scale available for the area you are in==.

## Exam traps

- Calling 1:20 000 "smaller scale" than 1:80 000. It is larger scale.
- Using the longitude scale to measure distance.
- Thinking a Mercator great circle is straight. On Mercator the straight line is the rhumb line.
- Forgetting that an edition date is not a guarantee: you must apply later corrections.
