# Current tables, current stations and BC passes

**Study aid only. Not for navigation.** Every rate, time and distance here is invented. Real passes need the current CHS tables and Sailing Directions.

## What you need to know

The tides module explains how the stream behaves and how a current table is built (the lesson on current stations and slack water). This lesson is the chart side: how a predicted stream gets into the plot, and how the same numbers decide when you go through a pass. Two ideas carry it.

1. The stream is a ==vector that changes with time==. A set-and-drift answer from a single fix is an average. A table gives you what the stream will be at the time you will be there.
2. In a narrow pass the stream can be ==faster than the boat==. There the question is not how much it helps or hinders, but whether you can go at all.

## What the table gives and what it leaves out

The current table gives, for a station, the ==times of slack water and the times and speeds in knots of maximum flood and ebb==, with a plus for flood and a minus for ebb. Times are standard time. It does not give heights, and it does not give a compass direction for the stream. For the direction you use the chart, the Sailing Directions for the area and any notes with the station. A flood stream in one channel flows toward the head of the water, which may be a different compass direction from a flood in the next channel.

The Canadian Tide and Current Tables and the Canadian Sailing Directions are both on the NSR list of publications to be kept on board (s. 142; see lesson 23), unless a vessel under 100 GT is exempt by local knowledge.

## A stream along the track

When the stream runs along your track, the sum is easy. ==Speed made good = speed through the water + rate== if the stream is fair, and ==minus rate== if foul. The difficulty is that the rate changes. Between slack and maximum it climbs; after the maximum it falls. For a run of several hours, break it into steps. For each hour estimate the ==mean rate== from the table (the average of the rates at the start and end of the hour is a fair approximation), work the distance for that hour, and add them up.

### Worked example 1 (invented): a foul run

A vessel makes 7.0 knots through the water along a channel 21.0 nm long, against a flood. Mean rates for four hours from departure: 1.0, 2.0, 2.5 and 1.5 knots.

| Hour | Rate (foul) | Speed made good | Distance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 1.0 | 6.0 | 6.0 |
| 2 | 2.0 | 5.0 | 5.0 |
| 3 | 2.5 | 4.5 | 4.5 |
| 4 | 1.5 | 5.5 | 5.5 |

The total is 21.0 nm in exactly ==4 hours==, an average of 5.25 knots over the ground. In still water the same 21.0 nm would take 3 hours.

### Worked example 2: leave later, ride a fair stream

Delay the departure until the stream turns fair. Mean rates now: 0.5, 1.5 and 2.0 knots (fair).

- Hour 1: 7.0 + 0.5 = 7.5 nm.
- Hour 2: 7.0 + 1.5 = 8.5 nm. Total after two hours: 16.0 nm.
- The last 5.0 nm in hour 3 at 7.0 + 2.0 = 9.0 knots: 5.0 ÷ 9.0 = 0.56 h = ==33 minutes==.

The passage takes ==2 h 33 min==. Waiting for the turn saved nearly an hour and a half of running time and the fuel for it (lesson 20), even after allowing for the wait itself.

The arithmetic for a stream at an angle to the track is the triangle of lesson 18. Take the mean set and rate for the leg, work the course to steer and the speed made good, and repeat for the next leg.

## The BC passes

The coast has narrow channels in which the whole tide must pass through a small opening. The strongest named in DFO sources are ==Seymour Narrows, Okisollo, Surge, Hole-in-the-Wall, Yuculta and Arran Rapids==, and one source reports Seymour Narrows streams above ==13 knots== (7 m/s). University notes give Sechelt (Skookumchuk) Rapids at up to 17.6 knots. These are the figures for the strongest streams, not for every day, but the lesson is that they can be several times the speed of a small vessel.

A pass like this is ==navigable at slack, and at some times not at peak flow==. The planning method is therefore:

1. Read the current table (and the notes in the Sailing Directions) for the pass and the day.
2. Decide the stream you will accept and the window around slack it gives (the next lesson in the tides module works this through).
3. Work backward from the arrival time at the pass entrance to the departure time, using speed made good (lesson 20).
4. Arrive ==at or a little before== the predicted slack; wait in a safe spot outside if you are early.
5. Plan for a missed slack: the next one is hours away.

## Wind against the stream

Moving water shortens and steepens wind-driven waves. When the wind blows against the stream the sea becomes shorter, steeper and confused, and it feels worse than the wind alone suggests. The hazard is worst at headlands, bars, harbour entrances and narrow channels. A turning tide can also build seas with no change in the wind at all. So when you plan a pass, look at the forecast wind ==and== at the stream at your time of arrival, and consider delaying if the two oppose.

## The limits of the numbers

- ==Wind== can advance or delay slack considerably.
- ==Secondary stations== are less precise than reference stations.
- ==Position==: the prediction is for a stated point or the middle of the channel.
- ==Time standard==: standard time; add the daylight saving hour.
- A prediction is a starting point; the fix is the check. If you reach a mark earlier or later than expected, the stream is not what the table said: revise.

## BC example

A skipper heading north from the Gulf Islands for Desolation Sound has to pass Seymour Narrows. She reads the current table for the day, adds the daylight saving hour and finds that slack falls in mid-afternoon. She plans the run from her anchorage by legs, with a fair stream in the first two hours (about 8 knots over the ground) and a slower final leg. She works out an arrival 30 minutes before slack and a departure time from that, and lists a waiting bay on each side of the narrows. The wind forecast is light, so she sees no problem with a sea against the stream. If she is more than an hour late at her fix halfway, she will turn her plan into a wait for the next slack.

## Common mistakes

- Timing a pass from the tide table.
- Taking a plus as "north" or "east". It means flood.
- Using one mean rate for a run of many hours when the stream turns.
- Adding the stream rate when it is foul, or subtracting it when fair.
- Forgetting the daylight saving hour.
- Assuming the prediction will hold in strong wind.
- Planning to arrive exactly at slack with no margin.
