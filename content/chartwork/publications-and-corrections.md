# Canadian publications and keeping them current

**Study aid only. Not for navigation.** This lesson paraphrases the Navigation Safety Regulations, 2020 (SOR/2020-216) from the Justice Canada text read for this app. It is not the official version; check the live regulations.

## What you need to know

A chart is only as good as its last correction. A shoal found last month, a buoy moved last week, a light out of service today: none of these is on the printed chart you bought two years ago. The law reflects that in two steps. It tells you what you must carry (section 142), and it tells you that what you carry must be up to date before you use it (section 146). Examiners ask about both, and also about the exception for small vessels.

## What must be on board: section 142(1)

The master and the authorized representative of the vessel must keep on board the ==most recent versions==, for each area where the vessel is scheduled to be navigated, of:

1. The ==reference catalogue== and the applicable ==charts in their largest scale==. For a Canadian vessel in Canadian waters these are published by the Canadian Hydrographic Service (CHS).
2. The ==annual edition of Notices to Mariners==.
3. For a Canadian vessel in waters under Canadian jurisdiction:
   - ==Canadian Sailing Directions== (CHS);
   - ==Canadian Tide and Current Tables== (CHS);
   - ==List of Lights, Buoys and Fog Signals== (Canadian Coast Guard);
   - ==Radio Aids to Marine Navigation== (CCG), but only if the vessel is required by the regulations (or by any foreign law) to carry radio equipment.
4. An ==illustrated table of life-saving signals==, for a vessel other than a pleasure craft under 150 GT that is on a voyage other than a sheltered waters voyage. It must be readily available to the person in charge of navigation.
5. ==Ice Navigation in Canadian Waters== (CCG) for a voyage on which ice may be met.

Other items on the list apply to larger vessels or to near-coastal and unlimited voyages (the search and rescue manual at 150 GT, the International Code of Signals and the IMO standard phrases). They are not part of the Master Limited under 60 GT picture, but know that the list grows with the vessel and the voyage.

Foreign publications may stand in for the Sailing Directions, tables and lists (the (c) and (d) items) when they come from an official foreign source and are as complete, accurate and up to date.

If, after reasonable efforts, you cannot obtain a required item at a place where you call, and safety and efficiency of navigation are not compromised, the regulation does not apply, but you must comply as soon as circumstances permit.

## The small-vessel exception: under 100 GT

Section 142(3) says the list in 142(1) ==does not apply to a vessel under 100 gross tonnage== if the person in charge of navigation has ==sufficient knowledge== of the area where the vessel is to be navigated, so that the safety and efficiency of navigation will not be compromised. The knowledge must cover:

- the location and character of the charted ==shipping routes, lights, buoys and marks, and navigational hazards==; and
- the ==prevailing navigational conditions==, such as ==tides, currents, ice and weather patterns==.

A Master Limited under 60 GT vessel is well under 100 GT, so this exception is likely to be an oral question. Notice what it is not. It is not a licence to go without charts. It says that the carriage list is not mandatory if you truly know the water. The better answer to an examiner is: the exception exists, I would still carry the charts and tables for any area I did not know thoroughly, and I could not claim it for a place I had never been.

## Electronic charts: section 143

A chart in electronic form counts as the required chart only if it is displayed on an ==ECDIS== that:

- uses the ==ENC== where one is available, or a raster chart (RNC) that is a facsimile of a CHS or foreign hydrographic office chart where it is not, and in the raster mode is used with other printed charts that meet 142(1)(a);
- meets the ==IHO S-52== content and display specification; and
- has a ==back-up arrangement== to the IMO standard, with main and emergency power and 30 minutes of transitional power, or has printed charts on which the position is plotted often enough to take over at once if the ECDIS fails.

As read from the text, a chart plotter, tablet or phone app that is not an ECDIS meeting these conditions does not stand in for the chart under s. 142. Plotters and apps are valuable aids, but ask your instructor how the rule is applied to your vessel. The wording of 143 is about charts "referred to in" 142(1)(a); what a small vessel needs and how a plotter is treated in practice are points for an expert to confirm.

## Navigation accessories: section 145

A vessel that must keep charts and publications under s. 142 must also be fitted with the ==navigation accessories== needed to ==plot the position precisely==, to ==determine the accuracy of compass readings==, and ==binoculars==. Parallel rules, dividers, a pencil and a way to take bearings fit the first two.

## Keeping it current: section 146

Before the charts, documents and publications are used to plan and execute a voyage, the master must make sure they are ==correct and up to date==, based on information in ==Notices to Mariners or a navigational warning==. The only exception is where it is impossible, after reasonable efforts, to receive them.

This applies to the whole set, not just the chart: the lights list, the Sailing Directions and the radio aids list all have corrections.

## Notices to Mariners in practice

The Canadian Coast Guard publishes Notices to Mariners (NOTMAR) in monthly editions, and an annual edition. For BC, the Western edition applies. An archived monthly edition (January 2019) was read for this lesson, and the layout may have changed since. It had five numbered sections:

1. General and safety information, with notices on buoys, ranges and aids.
2. ==Chart corrections==, ordered by ==chart number==. Each line gives an action (add, delete, amend, reposition), the item, its position and the last notice number and date.
3. Radio Aids to Marine Navigation corrections.
4. Sailing Directions and Small Craft Guide corrections, by paragraph.
5. List of Lights, Buoys and Fog Signals corrections.

Three practical points from that edition:

- ==Temporary (T) and preliminary (P) notices are not charted== by CHS. The edition advises marking them on the chart in ==pencil== (a soft pencil, light pressure) so that they can be rubbed out.
- Only critical changes appear in the chart corrections section, so small discrepancies may remain, and the edition said the ==List of Lights is the most current source== if it conflicts with a chart.
- ==Navigational warnings== (broadcast, for urgent matters) are kept until superseded by Notices to Mariners or a revised chart. For the western region they are issued from a desk at Prince Rupert.

On the chart itself, the margin gives the edition date and a correction record. A chart printed before a correction is still the same chart; the responsibility for bringing it up to date lies with the user.

## BC example

A skipper preparing to take a 40-foot boat from Vancouver to Prince Rupert pulls out her charts, tables and the Sailing Directions for each area. She reads the latest monthly Notices to Mariners and the current navigational warnings, finds three corrections for her charts of the Strait of Georgia, and pencils them in. A temporary notice about a light being off station she marks in pencil. She also checks the List of Lights for the lights she will rely on at night, because a correction there may not yet be on the chart. She logs the date of the last correction applied to each chart in the passage plan.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking the exception for under 100 GT means no charts are needed.
- Treating a plain chart plotter or phone app as the required chart. Under s. 143 an electronic chart counts only on an ECDIS that meets the conditions.
- Forgetting that the Radio Aids publication depends on radio carriage.
- Using an old edition of the Tide and Current Tables, or the wrong region's volume.
- Correcting the chart but not the lights list or the Sailing Directions.
- Inking a temporary notice into the chart instead of marking it in pencil.
- Ignoring navigational warnings between monthly editions.
