# Reading chart symbols (Chart 1)

**Study aid only. Not for navigation.** This lesson describes symbols in words. Use the current **Canadian Chart 1** (printed or online) and the real chart for navigation.

## What you need to know

A chart is a code. ==Chart 1== is the key. It is the Canadian Hydrographic Service publication that ==explains the symbols, abbreviations and terms== found on nautical charts. It follows the lettered format agreed by member nations of the International Hydrographic Organization, so a symbol in a Canadian chart looks like a symbol in most others. Each section has a letter and each entry has a number.

You do not need to memorise every entry. You must know ==which section to open==, and you must recognise the dangers by sight.

## The sections

| Letter | Contents |
|---|---|
| ==A== | Chart number, title, marginal notes (datums, scale, source, warnings) |
| ==B== | Positions, distances, directions, compass (variation, compass rose) |
| C, D, E, F | Natural features, cultural features, landmarks, ports |
| ==H== | Tides and currents |
| ==I== | Depths (soundings, contours, drying heights) |
| ==J== | Nature of the seabed |
| ==K== | Rocks, wrecks and obstructions |
| L | Offshore installations (including cables and pipelines) |
| M | Tracks and routes |
| ==N== | Areas and limits (anchorages, prohibited areas) |
| ==P== | Lights |
| ==Q== | Buoys and beacons |
| R, S, T, U | Fog signals, radar and radio, services, small craft |
| V, W, X | Indexes of abbreviations and descriptions |

If you remember only four: ==I for depths, K for dangers, P for lights, Q for buoys==.

## Depths and heights

- Soundings are in ==metres and decimetres (tenths) down to 21 m, then whole metres==. A figure like 7₂ means 7.2 m. The spot where the depth was found is the centre of the figure.
- An ==underlined figure== on a drying area is a ==drying height==: the seabed stands that many metres above chart datum. At the moment of chart datum you could walk there. A plain figure is a depth below datum.
- Figures in brackets against a feature (a rock, say) give a height above datum for that feature.
- Areas marked ==unsurveyed or inadequately surveyed== have a special symbol. Treat the water there as unknown, not as deep.
- Depth contours (lines of equal depth) and the chart's colour tints help you see shallow water at a glance; the chart's own key explains them.

Heights of lights and clearances use the high-water datum (lesson 2).

## Dangers: rocks, wrecks, obstructions

Section K is the section a skipper reads most carefully.

- A ==rock that covers and uncovers== (it dries at low water) has its own symbol, with its ==height above chart datum in brackets== when known. A ==rock awash== is awash at the level of chart datum itself. An ==underwater rock of known depth== is shown with the letter ==R== and the depth. The first two are the traps at a low or middle tide, because they are at or above the water near low water.
- ==Wk== is a wreck. Symbols differ for a wreck whose hull is always dry, a wreck with a known least depth, a wreck with an unknown least depth that may be dangerous to surface navigation, and non-dangerous wreck remains. Treat any wreck with unknown depth as dangerous.
- ==Br== is breakers. ==Co== is a submerged coral reef.
- Obstructions, foul ground and fish havens have their own symbols. A foul area is dangerous to anchor in or to fish in.

## Bottom type

Section J gives the nature of the seabed, important for choosing an anchorage. Common letters are ==S for sand== and ==M for mud==. Rock and stones are marked with their own letters; look up the rest in Chart 1 rather than guessing. The anchoring lessons say which bottoms hold best.

## Areas and limits

Section N includes anchorage areas (with numbers or names), anchor berths and a swinging circle, and prohibited areas: entry prohibited, anchoring prohibited, fishing prohibited and diving prohibited. Treat them as restrictions, not as advice.

## The variation note (section B)

Variation is written in degrees with E or W, followed by the year and the annual change in brackets in minutes, for example:

==4° 30′ W 2007 (8′ E)==

This means variation was 4° 30′ west in 2007, and it changes by 8′ east each year, so it is decreasing. Lesson 6 shows you how to correct it to the current year.

## Lights, buoys, beacons

Light characteristics are written as short codes such as **Fl(2) W 10s 25m 15M** (two flashes, white, repeated every 10 seconds, 25 m high, 15 nautical miles range). The seamanship lessons on light characteristics and buoyage explain the codes. Section P covers lights and section Q buoys and beacons.

## Worked example: reading a chart extract in words

Imagine an area of a chart with these markings (invented):

- A cluster of figures: 12, 9₄ and 6₁ near the middle of a channel; along the south shore an underlined figure 1₂ inside a dotted line.
- A symbol for rock labelled with a small circle and "(0.3)" near it.
- A wreck marked "Wk" with a depth of 5.8 m (shown 5₈).
- A boxed area with a small anchor and a diagonal line through it.

What do they tell you?

1. The channel soundings are 12 m, 9.4 m and 6.1 m. The shoalest is 6.1 m.
2. The underlined 1₂ is a **drying height** of 1.2 m: dry ground 1.2 m above datum.
3. The rock with (0.3) is a ==rock that covers and uncovers==: its top stands 0.3 m above chart datum, so it is under water at high tide and shows near low water. It is a hazard that you cannot see at high tide.
4. The wreck has 5.8 m of water over it. At a low tide it could be a hazard to a deep-draught vessel.
5. The anchor symbol with a line is probably an anchoring-prohibited area. Check Chart 1 section N for the exact symbol.

## BC practical example

You plan to anchor for lunch in a bay. On the chart you see an area of small dots, an "M" and soundings of 5 to 8 m, and no hazard symbols. The dots and M show a mud bottom, good for holding. You pick a spot at least 4 m deep at low tide for your 1.5 m draught. You check the area is not a cable area (section L) and not a prohibited area (section N) before you drop the hook.

## Exam traps

- Reading an underlined number as a depth.
- Treating the absence of soundings as deep water. Unsurveyed means unknown.
- Mixing Chart 1 sections (confusing section I depths with section K dangers).
- Forgetting that a wreck with unknown depth is treated as dangerous.
- Reading the variation annual change as the total variation.

## Check yourself

1. Which Chart 1 section would you open for a symbol you do not recognise on a rock?
2. A plain 3.5 and an underlined 3.5: what is the difference?
3. What does "Wk" mean?
4. 3° 15′ E 2010 (6′ W): what is happening to the variation?
5. Does a gap in the soundings mean deep water?
