# Transits, leading lines and clearing bearings

**Study aid only. Not for navigation.** The geometry and numbers are invented.

## What you need to know

Three of the best tools in pilotage use no calculation at all. They need only the chart, your eyes and, for clearing bearings, a compass.

- A ==transit== is two charted fixed objects seen ==in line==. You are on the line through them somewhere.
- A ==leading line== is a transit selected, or built, to keep you ==on a safe track==.
- A ==clearing bearing== is a bearing line from a charted object drawn ==clear of a danger==. As long as the actual bearing stays on the safe side, you are clear.

## Transits

When two objects line up, you are on the straight line drawn through them on the chart. That is a ==line of position that needs no compass==, so it is independent of compass error and as accurate as the charted positions of the objects.

Uses:

1. **A line of position.** Cross it with a second line (a bearing, a range, a depth, another transit) to get a fix. Two transits crossing give a very good fix.
2. **A check on the track.** Watch the objects as you go. If the front object moves to the left of the rear one, you have moved off the line to one side; if it moves right, to the other. You see the error before it gets big. The nearer you are to the objects, the faster they open, so a transit is most sensitive close to the front mark.
3. **A compass check.** Compare the charted true bearing with the compass bearing (lesson 9).
4. **A mark to turn on.** A second transit crossing the track tells you exactly when to alter.

A transit whose front and rear objects are well separated, with the front one close, is more sensitive than two objects that are close together, because a small shift off the line opens them visibly. Objects close together, or both far away, open slowly.

## Leading lines

Many channels have **leading lines** (also called ranges): two lights, or two daymarks, built to be kept in line. They are marked on the chart with the bearing of the line. When the lights or daymarks are kept in line, the vessel is on the charted track. The Canadian Aids to Navigation System includes range lights and range daymarks for this purpose.

- Keep them in line, not slightly open. A "pair open to the right" means you are off the line.
- The charted bearing is the direction toward the marks, not the course you are steering. Your heading may differ because of a current.
- Do not rely on one pair for the whole passage. Know the next mark before you leave the first.

## Clearing bearings

A clearing bearing keeps you away from a danger without a fix. It is drawn from a ==fixed charted object== (a lighthouse, a beacon, a prominent tower) as follows.

1. Find the danger and the side you must stay on.
2. From the object, draw a line past the danger, leaving a ==margin== between the line and the danger. Do not draw the line touching the danger.
3. ==Hatch the side of the line that is the danger side.== Label it ==NMT== (not more than) or ==NLT== (not less than) with the true bearing of the object from the ship.
4. At sea, check the bearing of the object from the ship. As long as it stays on the safe side of the stated value, you are clear. If it nears the limit, alter away from the danger at once.

To choose NMT or NLT, ask which way the bearing changes as the ship moves toward the danger. If it would decrease, the safe condition is "not less than" (NLT); if it would increase, "not more than" (NMT). ==Check the direction the bearing moves== each time you draw one, because the label depends on the geometry.

### Worked example (invented)

A lighthouse L stands on a point. The ship is approaching it on a track of 045° T. A reef lies 3.1 nm from the light on a bearing of 212° T from the light, which puts it 0.7 nm to starboard of the approach track, about 3 nm out. The skipper wants to keep at least 0.3 nm clear of the reef.

1. From the lighthouse, draw a line past the reef with 0.3 nm of margin on the track side. It runs on a bearing of ==217.4° T from the light== (the bearing from L to the margin point).
2. As seen from the ship, the light is on the reciprocal: 217.4 − 180 = ==037.4° T==. Round to the safe side: ==038° T==.
3. If the ship drifts toward the reef (to starboard), the light moves toward the port bow and its bearing from the ship ==decreases==. So the limit is ==light bearing NLT 038° T==. Hatch the reef side of the line.
4. Check by taking positions. On the track the light bears 045°. Half a mile to starboard of the track, 3 nm out, the bearing is 35.5° (less than 038°) and the reef is only 0.2 nm away: the NLT limit has been crossed and the warning has worked. Three-tenths of a mile to starboard, the bearing is 39.3° and the reef is 0.4 nm off: still clear.

### Converting to compass

The ship is heading 045° T. The compass error is 14° E (variation 16° E, deviation 2° W), so the compass heading is 045 − 14 = ==031° C==. A true bearing of 038° is 038 − 14 = ==024° C== on this heading. At the wheel, the clearing bearing is "light NLT 024° C".

If the ship alters to 070° T, the deviation will change, and so will the compass error; the compass limit must be worked out again. Do not carry a compass limit from one heading to another.

## Choose objects well

- Use ==fixed, charted objects== for transits and clearing bearings. A floating buoy may be off station or dragging and cannot guarantee clearance of a danger.
- Identify the object. Check against the chart and the DR, or you may clear the wrong reef.
- Short bearings from a nearby object change quickly and are sensitive; distant objects change slowly and give less warning.

## BC example

Entering a narrow harbour behind a drying reef, a skipper keeps the two white leading beacons in line. Halfway in, a rock lies just outside the line on the left. She has also pre-drawn a clearing bearing from a conspicuous light on the far shore, converted to a compass bearing for her heading. When the beacons start to open she knows at once which way to correct, and the clearing bearing warns of the rock if she corrects too far.

## Exam traps

- Putting NMT where it should be NLT. Think about the direction the bearing changes toward the danger.
- Drawing the clearing line touching the danger.
- Quoting the true limit when the question asks for the compass limit (or the reverse).
- Using the same compass limit after a change of heading.
- Reversing the direction: the bearing in a clearing bearing is the bearing of the mark from the ship.
- Trusting a buoy as the clearing mark.
