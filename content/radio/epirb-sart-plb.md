# EPIRB, PLB and SART in the radio system

**Study aid only.** This lesson adds the radio-system view to the Bridge Watch lesson on flares, EPIRBs and SARTs. Read that first for the rules on flares and the post-activation steps. Follow the maker's instructions for your own beacon.

## What you need to know

Three devices help in a rescue, and they do different jobs:

- An ==EPIRB== (emergency position-indicating radio beacon) and a ==PLB== (personal locator beacon) are ==406 MHz== beacons. They alert the rescue system by satellite. An EPIRB belongs to the vessel; a PLB is worn or carried by a person.
- A ==SART== (search and rescue transponder) does not call for help. It helps rescuers who are already close to ==pinpoint== the survivors.

In the GMDSS language, the EPIRB is the **primary alert** device, and the SART is a **secondary** device for locating. The vessel's VHF with DSC is the third way to alert help, within VHF range.

## EPIRB, PLB: alert

An EPIRB sends a ==406 MHz== distress signal to the Cospas-Sarsat satellites, and a separate ==121.5 MHz== signal that aircraft and rescue boats can home on. It can start by hand or automatically when it floats free. Because the beacon carries a code for the vessel, it must be ==registered==: NSR 2020 s. 228 requires registration with the ==Canadian Beacon Registry==, kept by the Department of National Defence, and an update ==within 30 days== of any change. An unregistered beacon makes rescue slower and a false alert harder to clear.

### Who must carry which

| Vessel and voyage | Beacon |
|---|---|
| Near coastal voyage Class 1 or unlimited voyage | ==Float-free EPIRB== |
| Over 12 m, inland voyage or near coastal Class 2 | ==Float-free EPIRB== |
| 12 m or less, inland (Great Lakes and St. Lawrence) or near coastal Class 2 | Float-free EPIRB, manual EPIRB, PLB, or (under 8 m, in A1 only) a waterproof DSC handheld VHF |

A **float-free** EPIRB must: float free if the vessel sinks; be readily accessible near the navigating position unless it can be activated by remote control from there; and be able to be released by hand and carried into a survival craft (s. 209(2)). A manual EPIRB, a PLB or a handheld must be ==worn by the person in charge of the navigational watch== or, if that is not practical, stowed where it is ready for abandonment (s. 209(4)).

## SART: locate

A radar SART answers a rescuer's ==X-band (9 GHz)== radar. Within about ==8 nautical miles== of a ship's radar it replies with a sweep that appears as a line of ==12 dots== about ==0.64 nm== apart. Closer than about 1 nm the replies fill in as arcs, then full circles. An AIS-SART does the same job by sending a GPS-based message. Radar SART range is line-of-sight, roughly 8 to 10 nm. NSR 2020 s. 208 says one of the SARTs a vessel must carry is stowed so it can be got at ==immediately on board==.

## Tests and maintenance

- An EPIRB, PLB or SART, except one stowed in an inflatable life raft, is inspected and tested by a radio operator ==on installation and at least every six months== (s. 239). The test follows the maker's instructions.
- An EPIRB's ==automatic release== carries a replacement date and is replaced on or before it. An EPIRB or PLB ==battery== is replaced after use in an emergency, after inadvertent activation, when a test requires it, and by its expiry date. A SART battery is replaced by expiry (ss. 230 and 231).
- A survival craft VHF with a user-replaceable power source is tested at each boat and fire drill (s. 239(3)).

## A BC example

A 10 m crab boat works inside the BC coast on a near coastal Class 2 voyage. Because she is 12 m or less, the rules let her carry a manual EPIRB instead of a float-free one. The skipper chooses a PLB clipped to the lifejacket. It is registered in the Canadian Beacon Registry in the boat's name and the skipper's phone number. The crew check it at the six-month test and write the date in the log. If a new owner buys the boat, the registration is updated within 30 days.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking a SART calls for help. It only helps already-close rescuers find you.
- Expecting a PLB to float free. It does not; it is carried by a person.
- Skipping the six-month test or ignoring the maker's test instructions.
- Forgetting registration after a sale or an address change.
- Stowing a SART in a locked locker, not "readily accessible for immediate use".
- Confusing 406 MHz (satellite alert) with 121.5 MHz (homing).
