# Listening watch and channels

**Study aid only.** The rules below come from the Navigation Safety Regulations, 2020 (NSR 2020) and the Marine Personnel Regulations (MPR). Traffic-zone channels change, so always take the current channel from the Notices to Mariners, the RAMN and the VTS publications.

## What you need to know

Owning a radio is not the same as hearing it. The regulations require a vessel with a compulsory VHF to keep a **continuous listening watch**, tuned to the right channel and loud enough to hear. The point is simple: another vessel or the coast guard must be able to reach you, and you must hear a distress, urgency or safety call when one comes.

## When the watch starts and ends

NSR 2020 s. 252(1): a vessel that must carry a VHF under s. 204 keeps a ==continuous listening watch== starting ==15 minutes before the vessel is underway==. It ends only when the vessel is both of these:

1. ==securely anchored, moored, made fast to the shore or secured to the bottom==; and
2. in a place where its presence does ==not constitute a hazard to passing vessels==.

If an anchored vessel might be a hazard, say in a narrow channel or close to a ferry track, the watch carries on. The MPR (s. 254) makes it the master's job to ensure it, in the master's opinion. This is a judgement the master must be ready to explain.

## Which channel

The channel is in Schedule 4 of the NSR 2020. For a vessel in waters under Canadian jurisdiction, other than the Great Lakes:

- ==outside a traffic zone==: Channel 16 for both the listening watch and navigation safety calls;
- ==inside a traffic zone==: the ==appropriate VHF channel==, which is the channel named in Notices to Mariners or by law for vessel traffic management in that zone.

On the BC coast the traffic zones are the Vessel Traffic Services (VTS) zones run by the two MCTS centres. When you enter a zone you change the watch channel to the one for that sector. When you leave you go back to Channel 16.

(The Great Lakes entry, with Channel 13, is different. Do not carry it over to the Pacific.)

The set must be run with ==sufficient gain== so that you can hear a weak or distant call. A radio with the volume or squelch turned down does not meet the rule, even if it is on the right channel.

## Interrupting the watch

The watch can be interrupted only in a limited case. If the vessel has no ==additional VHF installation==, the watch may be broken ==for short periods== while the one set is used to send or receive on another channel (s. 252(3)). Fit a second set and the watch should not be broken. Brief breaks while you work a channel are permitted; leaving the set on the wrong channel is not.

A related duty, s. 248, says a vessel with VHF, MF, MF/HF, NAVTEX or similar equipment must also keep a continuous watch on the frequencies assigned for ==maritime safety information== (weather and navigation warnings), appropriate to the time of day, position and equipment.

## Who is responsible

- The ==master and the person in charge of the deck watch== must ensure the radiotelephone procedures in Division 3 are met (s. 242).
- Only a ==radio operator== (a certificate holder) may keep the listening watch or make a navigation safety call, if authorised by the master (s. 250(1)).
- If the person keeping the watch is not the person in charge of the deck watch, they must ==tell the person in charge without delay== about anything they hear that may affect safe navigation (s. 250(2)).

For a rating, the last point is the one that matters on watch. If you hear a call that affects the vessel, you pass it on at once.

## The BC coast guard centres

Radio calls on the BC coast are handled by two Marine Communications and Traffic Services centres:

| Centre | Call sign | Open | Language |
|---|---|---|---|
| Victoria MCTS | ==VAK== | 24 hours | English only |
| Prince Rupert MCTS | ==VAJ== | 24 hours | English only |

Each centre works through remote VHF sites. The RAMN shows them carrying Channel 16, Channel 70 and working channels such as 11, 12 and 83A, depending on the site, and the continuous marine broadcast on Channel 21B. Calls are recorded.

## A BC example

The tug Sound Pilot (invented) leaves a Burrard Inlet berth for Howe Sound. At 0545 the master tells the mate, "Underway at 0600." At 0545 the radio is on the traffic channel for her VTS sector, volume up, and a log note is made. She was inside a traffic zone, so the watch channel was the appropriate VHF channel, not 16. Two hours later, when she leaves the VTS zone for open water, the watch moves to Channel 16. After anchoring in a quiet bay out of the track, she can end the watch, because she is securely anchored and not a hazard.

## Common mistakes

- Starting the watch at departure instead of 15 minutes before.
- Leaving the radio on Channel 16 inside a traffic zone.
- Ending the watch when the lines are on but the vessel is lying in a channel where she could be a hazard.
- Assuming the Great Lakes rule (Channel 13 plus 16) applies on the Pacific.
- Treating a turned-down speaker as "on watch".
- Keeping a watch without being a certificate holder.
