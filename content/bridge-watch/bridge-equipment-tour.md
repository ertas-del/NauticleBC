# Bridge equipment a rating uses

**Study aid only.** Every ship's bridge is laid out differently. Your officer will show you your own ship's equipment and its
limits. This lesson gives you the vocabulary and the regulation facts for the exam.

## What you need to know

The Bridge Watch Rating course lists ten pieces of bridge equipment you should know: ==gyro repeater, rate-of-turn
indicator, radar, electronic chart, AIS, echo sounder, steering position, autopilot, GPS and VHF radio==. A rating is
not in charge of the navigation, but you will stand next to this equipment for hours, you will be asked to read it, and
you will be the one who notices when it looks wrong. The Navigation Safety Regulations, 2020 (NSR) set out what ships
must carry. Smaller ships carry less, so a small BC boat may have only some of these.

## The ten, one at a time

**Gyro repeater.** A gyro-compass finds north by itself and does not need magnetism. A gyro repeater is a dial elsewhere
that copies its heading. The NSR require a ==gyro-compass heading repeater== at the emergency steering position on a vessel
of ==500 GT or more== on a voyage other than sheltered waters (s. 111(c)(ii)), and a bearing repeater for taking bearings
(s. 111(c)(iii)). A repeater can only be as right as the gyro behind it, so you compare it with the magnetic compass
regularly. The compass lessons explain how the readings differ.

**Rate-of-turn indicator.** It shows how fast the ship is swinging, such as 15° per minute to starboard. It tells you the
turn has started, is steady and has stopped, before the compass can show it. Fitted by the NSR on ==50 000 GT or more==
(s. 114(a)). Worked example (invented): at 15° per minute, a turn of 90° takes 90 ÷ 15 = **6 minutes**.

**Radar.** It shows range and bearing of other craft, obstructions, buoys and shorelines. The NSR require a ==9-GHz
radar== at ==300 GT or more== (s. 110(b)). The radar lesson for look-outs covers what a rating needs to recognise on the
screen.

**Electronic chart.** A chart plotter or ECDIS shows the chart and the ship's position. An ECDIS is the type the NSR allow
to take the place of paper charts (s. 143). It is only as good as its inputs and its settings, and the officer owns the
navigation, but a rating who sees the boat symbol apparently on land should say so.

**AIS.** It shows other ships' identity, position, course and speed. The NSR say that if an AIS Class A and a gyro-compass
or transmitting heading device are both fitted, they must be ==connected so that AIS transmits heading== (s. 118(4)). It
shows only ships that carry it and have it on.

**Echo sounder.** It measures and displays the depth of water. The NSR require one on ships of ==300 GT or more== (s. 110(a)).
Ask which depth your sounder shows, because a setting can mean depth below the transducer, below the keel or from the
waterline, and the officer will want to know a rating has not guessed.

**Steering position.** This is where you will steer: wheel or joystick or follow-up lever, the rudder angle indicator,
the compass reading and the view ahead. The NSR require the ==standard magnetic compass reading to be displayed at the main
steering position== (s. 106(1)). On Canadian vessels 55 m or more in length and built on or after 1 July 2002, s. 140 also sets the field of vision from
the main steering position: from right ahead to at least ==60° each side==. Report a blind arc caused by cargo, a crane or a dirty window.

**Autopilot.** A heading or track control system steers the ship on its own. Under NSR s. 133, when it is used in heavy
traffic, restricted visibility or any hazardous situation, the ship must be able to change over ==immediately== to manual
steering, a ==qualified helmsman== must be available, and every changeover is made by or under the supervision of the person in
charge of the deck watch. The course says you must also check that the automatic steering is actually holding its course.
The autopilot lesson shows the sequence.

**GPS.** GPS is one GNSS. The NSR require a GNSS receiver on ==150 GT or more== (s. 109(1)(a)), and an automatic distress
alert takes its position from it (s. 221). It gives position, course over ground and speed over ground, none of which is your
heading.

**VHF radio.** The NSR require a VHF to be ==accessible from the conning position==, with channel selection possible from that
position (s. 220). A rating may be asked to monitor a channel. The radio lessons cover procedure and channels.

## Putting it together

All of these are aids. The look-out rule is not softened by any of them. Rule 5 requires a look-out by sight and hearing
==and by all available means==. Radar, AIS and the chart add to your eyes and ears, never replace them. If an instrument
shows something you cannot see, or fails to show something you can, say so out loud. The officer wants to know.

Some NSR points that connect the tour together: the mode an instrument is operating in must be indicated where it has
alternatives (s. 129), and a fault in one part of an integrated system must not disable the others (ss. 130 and 131).

## BC example (invented)

A new rating is on the 0000–0400 watch on a 45 m vessel crossing Queen Charlotte Sound. At the start, the officer points
out the gyro repeater, the radar, the plotter, the AIS and the VHF, and says the gyro repeater should read about 15° more
than the steering compass, which is mostly the local variation. Twenty minutes in, the rating sees the two are now 24°
apart. She says: "Gyro repeater and steering compass are 24 degrees apart. You said about 15." The officer checks, finds the
repeater has lost step with the gyro-compass, and has it reset. The rating's job was to notice and say so.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking that a rating does not need to know the equipment. You are expected to know what it is for and to report it when
  it looks wrong.
- Mixing up the repeater (a copy) with the gyro-compass (the source).
- Believing the gyro repeater can't be wrong.
- Reading COG from the GPS as the ship's heading.
- Treating the autopilot as a helmsman.
- Forgetting that the electronic aids do not replace the look-out.
