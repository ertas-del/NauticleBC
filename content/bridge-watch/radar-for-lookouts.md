# Radar for look-outs: echoes and dangerous echoes

**Study aid only.** The officer of the watch operates and interprets the radar. A look-out reads it, and tells the officer what
is seen. Do not adjust the radar controls unless the officer or your training says to.

## What you need to know

The Bridge Watch Rating course says you must ==understand the display information of a marine radar== and know how to
==recognise an echo== of an object, including a ==dangerous echo==. That is a reading skill, not a plotting skill. You need
to know what you are looking at, what is likely to be missing, and how to describe a danger quickly.

## Reading the screen

A standard radar screen is a plan view with ==own ship at the centre==. You will see:

- the ==heading line== (heading marker): a line from the centre showing which way the bow points;
- ==range rings==: circles at equal steps, labelled in nautical miles, so you can estimate distance from the centre;
- a ==bearing scale== around the edge;
- bright patches called ==echoes==, each a return from an object.

Distance from the centre is range. The angle from the heading line is the **relative bearing** on a head-up display,
and the same bearing scale gives true bearings on a north-up display, where north is at the top. The officer will tell you
which type your screen is. Two tools are used to measure an echo: the ==electronic bearing line (EBL)==, which is swung onto an echo,
and the ==variable range marker (VRM)==, a ring expanded to touch it.

## What an echo tells you

A bright, steady, well-defined echo at medium range is often a steel ship. Land gives large areas of steady return. Heavy
rain gives large, scattered, changing patches. A small, fading echo may be a boat, a buoy or a wave. You often cannot tell
what an echo is from the screen alone, so report it by range, bearing and behaviour, not by guessing what it is.

## The dangerous echo

A ==dangerous echo== is one that is ==closing on a steady bearing==. The range falls and the bearing does not change. This is
radar's version of the look-out's steady bearing. Rule 7 says that if the compass bearing of an approaching vessel does not
appreciably change, ==risk of collision is deemed to exist==, and also that risk may exist even when the bearing changes,
particularly with a very large vessel, a tow or a vessel at close range.

A practical way to test it: have the officer place the EBL on the echo, then watch whether the echo stays on the line as the
range falls. If it does, it is a dangerous echo.

Remember the compass-bearing point from the look-out report lesson. On a head-up screen you read relative bearings, and they
change when the ship alters course, even if nothing else has changed.

Worked example (invented). You are on a steady course. An echo is 30° on the starboard bow at 3.0 nm. Five minutes later it is
still 30° on the starboard bow, at 2.5 nm.

1. The range has fallen 0.5 nm in 5 minutes, so the closing speed is 0.5 ÷ (5 ÷ 60) = **6 knots**.
2. If nothing changes, the time to meet is 2.5 ÷ 6 = 0.417 h = **25 minutes**.
3. The report: "Echo, starboard bow, 030 relative, two and a half miles, bearing steady, closing."

If the ship then alters 20° to starboard, the echo's relative bearing moves to about 010°, even though the compass bearing
has not changed. A well-trained look-out says so: "Relative bearing now 010, I think the compass bearing is steady."

## What radar can miss

Radar is not an all-seeing eye. The collision rules list what it may miss: ==small vessels, ice and floating objects may
not be detected at an adequate range== (Rule 6(b)). The reasons are:

- **Small or low targets**, such as a kayak, a log or a small open boat.
- **Wood and fibreglass** return weaker echoes than steel, and a vessel seen end-on returns less than one seen from the side.
- **Clutter.** Waves near the ship (sea clutter) and rain (rain clutter) can hide small targets, and the controls that
  remove clutter can remove real targets as well.
- **Shadow sectors** behind masts, funnels and cranes.
- **Distance and height.** Low targets far away are over the horizon of the antenna.

So a clear screen does not mean clear water. Keep looking and listening. A look-out who hears a fog signal that the radar
does not show must report it.

## False echoes

Some echoes are not real. A strong nearby ship can produce a row of weaker echoes at two, three or more times its true
range on the same bearing. A reflection from your own ship's structure can put a ghost in a shadow sector. If you see an
echo that behaves oddly, tell the officer what you see and let them check. Do not just ignore it.

## Racons, reflectors and SARTs

- A ==racon== is a transponder fitted to a mark. When a ship's radar triggers it, the screen shows a ==Morse-coded
  signal== or a short radial line just beyond the mark, which helps identify it.
- A ==radar reflector== is made of metal surfaces that return the signal, so it makes a small vessel or a buoy easier to see.
  The Collision Regulations (Rule 40) ask a vessel under 20 m or built mostly of non-metallic material to carry one if
  practicable.
- A ==radar SART== is carried in a liferaft or lifeboat. It answers 9 GHz (X-band) radar only and shows as a ==line of 12
  dots==. As a ship approaches, the dots become arcs and then circles. If you ever see it, report it at once as a possible
  distress.

## BC example (invented)

At 0300 in thick fog in the Strait of Georgia, a rating on look-out on a 30 m vessel hears a fog horn on the starboard bow and
reports: "Fog signal, starboard bow, one prolonged blast." The radar shows nothing at that bearing. The officer reduces
speed. Two minutes later a small wooden fishing boat appears at 300 m, barely showing on the screen. The radar had
nothing because the boat was small, low and in sea clutter. The look-out's ears found it.

## Common mistakes

- Believing that "nothing on the radar" means nothing there.
- Reporting what you think an echo is instead of its range, bearing and whether it is closing.
- Judging a relative bearing as steady after the ship has altered course.
- Turning the clutter controls up to tidy the screen without being told.
- Ignoring a sound or sight that the radar does not confirm.
