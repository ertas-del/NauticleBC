# Survival craft and rescue boats

**Study aid only.** This is a short refresher. The main teaching for survival craft and rescue boats is the Marine
Emergency Duties (MED) course that the Bridge Watch Rating certificate requires, with a certified instructor and real
equipment. Nothing here replaces that hands-on training.

## What you need to know

If a ship has to be abandoned, the lives of everyone aboard depend on the **survival craft** (lifeboats, life rafts and
the like) and on the people who launch and use them. A Bridge Watch Rating is often one of those people. Learn the
vocabulary, the certificate you need, the way drills are run and the basic Canadian equipment rule for small vessels.

## Definitions (Fire and Boat Drills Regulations, s. 1)

- A ==survival craft== is "a lifeboat, rescue boat, emergency boat, suitable boat, buoyant apparatus, life raft,
  inflatable rescue platform or any other vessel capable of sustaining the lives of persons in distress from the time
  of abandoning the vessel on which those persons were carried".
- A ==rescue boat== is a boat designed to be used for **rescuing persons in distress and marshalling survival craft**,
  and includes an emergency boat that does the same job.

So a rescue boat is for **fetching people out of the water** and gathering the survival craft together; a life raft or
lifeboat is where people **survive until rescued**.

## Training and certificates

- The Bridge Watch Rating certificate requires MED training in **proficiency in survival craft and rescue boats other
  than fast rescue boats** (Marine Personnel Regulations, s. 171 table). Master Limited and other certificates have
  similar requirements in their own tables.
- Under MPR s. 205(6), the master and authorized representative must make sure that ==every person assigned on the muster
  list to the preparation or launching of a survival craft== (other than a manually launched life raft or a manually
  launched inflatable rescue platform) holds a ==training certificate in proficiency in survival craft and rescue boats
  other than fast rescue boats==.
- Under MPR s. 205(2), every member of the complement needed to meet safe manning must also hold the **MED basic safety**
  certificate (STCW basic safety) unless a special case applies.
- The detailed technical rules for lifeboats, rafts and launching appliances come through SOLAS Chapter III and the LSA
  Code, which the Vessel Construction and Equipment Regulations (s. 103) incorporate, modified by TP 14475. Those documents were
  not read for this lesson.

## Survival craft drills (FBDR, ss. 25 to 31)

| Rule | What it says |
|---|---|
| Crew lists (s. 25(1)) | Before a survival craft drill, the ==person in charge of each survival craft and the second-in-command each have a list of the crew==, and the person in charge makes sure the crew know their duties. |
| Duties in the drill (s. 25(2)) | Include mustering passengers, searching for the missing, preparing the craft and its equipment (including blankets), testing radio life saving equipment, operating the davits, starting lifeboat engines and checking fuel, and checking the emergency lighting. |
| Immersion suits (s. 26) | The master makes sure crew can ==don the immersion suits or anti-exposure suits== carried on board. |
| Lifeboats (s. 27(1)) | A vessel with more than one lifeboat launches a **different lifeboat** during each survival craft drill, and **each lifeboat** (including a lifeboat that is a rescue boat) is launched and manoeuvred in the water by the assigned crew ==at least once every three months==. |
| Vessel at sea (s. 27(2) and (3)) | At sea, a launching may be replaced by clearing and swinging out the lifeboats (each at least monthly), as long as each is still launched and manoeuvred at least every three months. When under way, launching and manoeuvring is done in **sheltered waters under the supervision of an experienced officer**. |
| Rescue boats that are not lifeboats (s. 31) | A **rescue boat drill** is held ==separately from any other drill, at least once every month==; the crew launch and manoeuvre the boat in the water, in sheltered waters under supervision if the vessel is under way. |
| Free-fall lifeboats (ss. 28 to 29) | Special rules (boarding, strapping in and the launch procedure every three months; a free-fall launch or simulated launch every six months). |

The master records each survival craft drill and rescue boat drill (see the previous lesson, s. 37).

## Small passenger vessels (Small Vessel Regulations)

Under SVR Part 4 (passenger-carrying, not more than 15 gross tonnage, 12 passengers or fewer):

- **Life rafts (s. 411).** The vessel must carry ==life rafts with a total capacity for all persons on board==, unless it is
  ==not more than 8.5 m in length==, on a ==sheltered waters voyage==, or at a distance of ==not more than two nautical miles from
  the shore of a river or lake== (measured from the mainland or from an island that can be used as a safe refuge from the
  weather).
- **Cold water (s. 403).** If the **water temperature is less than 15 degrees Celsius** and the vessel does not carry a life
  raft, the operator must make sure **equipment is carried or procedures are established** to protect everyone from
  hypothermia or cold shock caused by swamping, capsizing or falling overboard.
- **Procedures (s. 420).** The owner and operator must establish procedures for using life-saving appliances and fire
  extinguishing equipment in an emergency and make sure the crew **practise** them until proficient.

## Why speed and care matter: cold water

The Canadian Safe Boating Council describes the **1-10-1 principle**: about **1 minute** of cold shock (control your
breathing), about **10 minutes** of useful movement (cold incapacitation), and about **1 hour** before hypothermia leads to
unconsciousness. BC water is cold year-round. That is why a survival craft must be launched quickly and **correctly**, why
crew practise donning immersion suits, and why a lifejacket worn from the start of an emergency can save a life.

## Survival at sea techniques

TP 10936 lists survival at sea techniques for ratings, and the MED course teaches them. The sources read for this lesson
do not cover specific techniques such as huddling to conserve heat or staying with the craft, so they are **not** listed as exam
facts here. Learn them from your MED instructor and note the ones your course highlights.

## A BC example

A small BC whale-watching vessel of 9 m carries 10 passengers and operates in the open Strait of Georgia, more than two
nautical miles from the shore of any river or lake and not on a sheltered waters voyage. She is more than 8.5 m, so the
Small Vessel Regulations require her to carry life rafts with total capacity for everyone on board. On a cold March day
the water is 9 degrees Celsius, so the operator also makes sure procedures are in place to protect people from cold shock
(the life raft already helps meet the rule). The skipper practises the emergency procedures with the crew each month and
checks that the life raft is stowed ready for use.

## Exam traps

- **Survival craft is a wide term**: lifeboat, rescue boat, emergency boat, suitable boat, buoyant apparatus, life raft,
  inflatable rescue platform, or any vessel that sustains lives after abandoning.
- **Rescue boat drill: monthly, separate. Lifeboat launch: every three months.**
- **Under way, drills use sheltered waters and an experienced officer.**
- **The survival craft certificate is for people assigned on the muster list** to prepare or launch the craft.
- **8.5 m or less, sheltered waters, or within two nautical miles of a river or lake shore**: the three exceptions to the
  life raft rule for small passenger vessels.
- **Cold water means under 15 degrees Celsius** in the SVR cold water rule.
