# Principles of the navigational watch

**Study aid only.** These principles come from the STCW Code, which is a copyrighted IMO text. This lesson is based on
a government reproduction and on the Canadian regulation that applies it. Check the current Code with your instructor.

## What you need to know

The work of the bridge watch rests on a few rules that every officer and every rating should know. The Marine
Personnel Regulations (s. 213) make the ==master responsible== for two things before and during a voyage:

1. the intended ==voyage is planned==, and
2. a ==deck watch is kept== in line with ==Parts 2, 3 and 3-1 of STCW Code section A-VIII/2==.

Those Parts set out the **principles to be observed**. The rest of this lesson turns them into plain language.

## The bridge is never left unattended

The first principle is simple: the ==bridge must never be left unattended==. Someone qualified is always there, at
sea, at anchor and in port, and the watch carries on without a gap until a properly briefed relief takes over.

## How big should the watch be?

The master decides how many people the watch needs. The minimum by size is in the previous lesson. The principles list
what the master must weigh when deciding whether to put more people on:

- ==weather==,
- ==visibility== and whether it is ==daylight or darkness==,
- how close ==navigational hazards== are, and whether the officer must devote extra attention to navigation,
- the state of ==navigation aids== such as radar, GPS and electronic charts,
- whether ==automatic steering== is in use,
- ==radio duties== that take the officer's attention,
- ==unusual operational demands== (for example, a sudden manoeuvre or a pilot transfer).

A calm sunny morning in open water with the autopilot on needs fewer people than a dark, rainy night in a busy
channel with the radar acting up.

## What the officer of the watch must do

The officer of the watch (OOW) is the person in charge of the deck watch. The principles say the OOW must:

- ==keep the watch on the bridge== and, in no circumstances, ==leave the bridge until properly relieved==;
- stay responsible for the vessel's safe navigation ==until the master explicitly takes over== (even if the master
  comes onto the bridge) and the two of them clearly understand who has the con;
- at regular intervals check the ==course steered, the position and the speed== so the vessel keeps to the planned route;
- have ==full knowledge of the location and operation of all safety and navigational equipment== on the bridge;
- follow the ==master's standing orders== and any special orders written for the voyage.

## What the officer must not do

The OOW ==must not undertake any other duty== that would interfere with the safe navigation of the vessel. A watch
officer who is also writing a long email, doing cargo paperwork or repairing something does not have the full
attention that navigation demands.

## Where the rating fits in

For you as a rating, these principles explain your orders:

- Do **not** leave the bridge or wheel without being relieved by a fit person.
- Report what you see and hear promptly: the OOW can only meet the duty to "appraise the situation" if the information reaches them.
- If the master comes up during your watch, **keep reporting to the OOW** unless told clearly that the master has the con.
- Know where the equipment is: alarms, radios, steering controls, the way to the emergency steering position.

## Worked example: who has the con?

You are the additional person on a 300 GT vessel. At 0230 in the Strait of Georgia the master comes onto the bridge to
look at the chart and says only "How's the traffic?" The officer replies "Two ferries on the starboard bow, no change."
The master nods and stays beside the chart table.

- The master has not said "I have the con". So the ==officer remains responsible== for navigation.
- A third vessel appears fine on the port bow. You report it to the officer, as you always do, not to the master.
- Only after the master says clearly, for example, "I have the con," and the officer acknowledges, does the master hold
  responsibility.

## Exam traps

- "The master is on the bridge, so the master is in charge." Not until the master says so explicitly and it is understood.
- Leaving the bridge to fetch something "for a minute". The bridge may not be left unattended, and the officer may not
  leave until relieved.
- Mixing the minimum watch (from the regulation, by GT) with the **factors** (from the principles). The factors raise
  the number above the minimum when they call for it.
- Thinking automatic steering removes the need for a helmsman or a look-out. It is one factor in the decision, not a replacement.
