# Autopilot and hand steering changeover

**Study aid only.** Follow your vessel's own autopilot procedures and the master's standing orders. The legal
requirements below are from the Navigation Safety Regulations, 2020 (NSR 2020), SOR/2020-216.

## What you need to know

Most vessels now spend long periods on **autopilot**. The regulations call it the ==heading or track control system==:
heading control holds a compass course; track control follows a route. Autopilot is a useful tool, but it can only do
what it is told. It cannot see a boat ahead or notice that the tide has set you toward a shoal. The law therefore
insists on three safeguards:

1. You must be able to take over **immediately**.
2. A **qualified helmsman** must be ready.
3. The person in charge of the deck watch makes, or **supervises**, **every** changeover.

## The four rules in section 133

**(1) Immediate changeover.** When the autopilot is used in an area of ==high traffic density==, in ==restricted
visibility== or in ==any other hazardous navigational situation==, **means must be provided to enable the immediate
changeover to manual steering**. In practice this means the changeover control or switch is within reach, working, and
understood, and nobody has to hunt for the instructions.

**(2) A qualified helmsman.** In those same situations the person in charge of the deck watch must ensure that a
==qualified helmsman is available at all times== to take over. "Available" means able to take the wheel at once, not
asleep in the mess or at the other end of the ship. The section does not define "qualified"; ask your instructor how your employer
and Transport Canada read it for your vessel.

**(3) Who makes the changeover.** ==Every changeover== from autopilot to manual steering, **and back again**, must be made
by, or under the supervision of, ==the person in charge of the deck watch==. A helmsman does not switch modes on his
own; the officer decides and watches.

**(4) Testing manual steering.** While the autopilot is not in use, the manual steering must be tested before the vessel
enters any area where navigation demands special caution, and ==at least once a day==, to make sure the manual steering
is in effective working order. A system that has been unused for days may fail the first time someone needs it.

## Which vessels?

As in the steering systems lesson, ss. 133 to 136 are in **Division 5 of Part 1** ("Additional Requirements — Vessels
Not Subject to Chapter V of SOLAS"), and s. 126 says Division 5 does not apply to vessels subject to Division 2. So
these sections apply to Canadian vessels outside the SOLAS Chapter V group (many domestic vessels under 500 gross
tonnage and fishing vessels). Large SOLAS ships have equivalent duties through SOLAS Chapter V, which was not read for
this lesson.

## Checks when the autopilot is engaged

TP 10936 lists "check the autopilot is holding the course" as part of a rating's competence. Practical checks that
fit that duty (confirm against your vessel's procedure):

- **Is the heading what the officer ordered?** Compare the autopilot heading, the compass and the gyro repeater.
- **Is the vessel on the track?** A heading can be perfect while the tide sets the ship off the intended line.
- **Mode.** Is the autopilot in the mode the officer expects (heading, track, or an alternate mode)? The ==mode of
  operation being used must be indicated== on equipment fitted on or after July 1, 2002 that offers alternative modes (NSR 2020, s. 129).
- **Rudder behaviour.** Is the rudder angle indicator moving smoothly? Large, repeated rudder movements may mean a
  poor setting.

## Why changeover at close quarters is dangerous

The standard names "dangers of changeover at close quarters" but the sources read for this lesson do not list the
dangers, so ask your instructor for your vessel's practice. The usual ideas are straightforward: at the moment of
changeover the rudder may be at an angle you did not expect, the heading may be drifting, and a helmsman who has not
been steering needs a moment to get the feel of the ship. Close to another vessel or a hazard there is little room to
absorb that moment. This is why the changeover is made **by or under the supervision of the officer** and why a
helmsman should be ready **before** the traffic gets thick, not after.

## A BC example

A coastal vessel on autopilot in open water runs into fog approaching a busy BC harbour entrance. The OOW does not wait
for traffic to appear on radar. He calls the helmsman to the wheel, checks the rudder angle indicator, and **supervises
the changeover** to hand steering while the vessel still has plenty of room. The helmsman repeats "Hand steering, sir,
steering one-three-zero", and the OOW logs the time. Because the vessel will be in restricted visibility, the helmsman
remains at the wheel until the fog lifts. A once-a-day manual steering test had been done at 0600 that morning.

## Exam traps

- **"By or under the supervision of the person in charge of the deck watch"**: the helmsman may physically operate the
  switch, but the officer is responsible.
- **Both directions count**: changeover from autopilot to hand **and** back.
- **Daily** manual test, **and** before special-caution areas, **while the autopilot is not in use**.
- **Qualified helmsman available "at all times"** in heavy traffic, restricted visibility or other hazardous
  situations. It is a duty of the person in charge of the watch, not of the helmsman.
- **Heading control is not track control.** The vessel may be on heading and off track.
