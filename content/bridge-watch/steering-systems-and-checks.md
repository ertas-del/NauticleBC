# Steering systems, checks and emergency steering

**Study aid only.** The numbers below are from the Navigation Safety Regulations, 2020 (NSR 2020), SOR/2020-216.
Rules change; your ship's procedures and your instructor come first.

## What you need to know

A ship's steering gear is the one system that must work every time. The law therefore requires two things: **checks
before departure** (so a fault is found alongside, not in a channel) and **drills** (so the crew can steer from the
emergency position when it is needed). A rating at the wheel needs to know what those checks are, because
the helmsman is often the first to feel that something is wrong.

## Which vessels do the NSR steering sections cover?

Sections 133 to 137 of NSR 2020 sit in Division 5 of Part 1, which is headed **"Additional Requirements — Vessels Not
Subject to Chapter V of SOLAS"**. Section 126 says Division 5 does not apply to vessels covered by Division 2 (the
vessels subject to SOLAS Chapter V: Canadian vessels of 500 gross tonnage or more, smaller vessels on international
voyages of 150 gross tonnage or more, and foreign SOLAS vessels, with some exceptions such as fishing vessels). So:

- The rules in this lesson apply most directly to Canadian vessels **outside** Division 2, which includes many
  domestic vessels under 500 gross tonnage and fishing vessels.
- Large SOLAS ships have equivalent duties through SOLAS Chapter V itself. Those chapters were not read for this
  lesson; the ideas are the same.

## Types of steering control

==Full follow-up (FFU)== is what most people know as "the wheel". The helmsman turns the wheel to an angle and the
steering gear moves the rudder to that angle and **holds it there**. The rudder angle indicator shows the actual
angle. Moving the wheel again moves the rudder again.

==Non-follow-up (NFU)== is a separate control, often a spring-loaded lever or push-buttons. The rudder ==moves while the
control is held== and ==stops when it is released==; it does not return to an angle. The helmsman must watch the rudder
angle indicator to know where the rudder is. The helm wheel is normally deactivated when NFU is selected.

If the follow-up system fails, the Nautical Institute reporting scheme says the first reaction should be to ==switch to
NFU==. Other options are to change to the backup steering system or to stop the ship if that is practicable. In
confined water you may not be able to stop, so practise the changeover.

## Pre-departure checks (s. 136(1))

**==Within 12 hours before departure==** the steering gear must be checked and tested. The checks and tests include:

| Item | What is verified |
|---|---|
| Main steering gear | It operates. |
| Auxiliary steering gear | It operates (unless the auxiliary gear is tackle). |
| Remote steering gear control systems | They operate. |
| Steering controls at different positions on the bridge | Each operates. |
| Emergency power supply | It operates. |
| Rudder angle indicators | They agree with the actual rudder position. |
| Remote control power failure alarms | They work. |
| Power unit failure alarms | They work. |
| Automatic isolating arrangements and other automatic equipment | They operate. |
| Full rudder movement | The rudder moves through the full range the gear is designed for. |
| Visual inspection | Steering gear and connecting linkage. |
| Bridge to steering gear compartment communication | It works. |

**Regular short voyages (s. 136(2)).** A vessel that regularly engages on voyages of ==less than one week== does not
need the tests within 12 hours of every departure if they are done at least ==once every week==.

**Drills (s. 136(3)).** In addition, ==emergency steering drills must be held at least once every three months==. A drill
must include ==direct control from within the steering gear compartment==, **communications procedures with the
navigating bridge** and, where applicable, the operation of **alternative power supplies**.

**Records (s. 136(4)).** The person in charge of the deck watch records the dates of the checks and tests, and the dates
and details of the drills, in the official log book (or any log book if the official log book requirement does not
apply).

## Other steering gear rules

- **Two power units (s. 134).** If a vessel has two or more steering gear power units that **can run at the same time**,
  at least ==two must be running== in areas where navigation demands special caution.
- **Changeover instructions (s. 135(1)).** A vessel with remote steering control or a power unit must have ==simple,
  brief operating instructions and a block diagram== showing the changeover procedures, ==permanently displayed on the
  bridge and in the steering gear compartment==.
- **Familiarity (s. 135(2)).** The master and any person responsible for operating or maintaining the steering gear
  must know the vessel's steering systems and how to change from one to another.
- **Emergency steering position (s. 108).** Every vessel with an emergency steering position must have a two-way voice
  system that can pass heading information to it. On vessels of 500 gross tonnage or more, a gyro heading repeater at
  that position is also required in most cases (s. 111(c)(ii)).

## Small vessels and the Small Vessel Regulations

Under the Small Vessel Regulations (SVR) s. 737, a vessel must have a safe, reliable main steering gear operable from the
operating position. A means of **emergency steering** is also required if the vessel works in **remote areas or areas
where help is not readily available**, or if the main steering has a **remote control**. It is **not** required if the
main steering is a ==rudder and hand tiller==, or an **outboard engine or stern-drive**.

## What to do if steering fails

The regulations do not give a watchkeeper procedure, so follow your ship's. The common first steps, from the sources
read here and general seamanship, are:

1. **Tell the OOW at once** ("She does not answer the wheel").
2. The OOW will usually try **non-follow-up**, or change to the backup system, using the changeover instructions on the
   bridge.
3. The OOW will warn other vessels and may stop the ship if there is room.
4. If the bridge cannot recover steering, the OOW may order emergency steering from the **steering gear compartment**,
   which is why the drill and the bridge-to-compartment communication exist.

## A BC example

A small BC passenger vessel is about to leave the dock at 0800. The mate last tested the steering at 2015 the evening
before. That is more than 12 hours earlier, so the test is repeated before departure: wheel to full port and full
starboard with the rudder angle indicator checked at each end, the second bridge control tried, the alarms checked and
the intercom to the steering flat tested. The mate writes the time in the log book. If the vessel were on a regular
service of under a week she could, if the rules apply to her, instead test once a week, but the master may well still
choose to test every day.

## Exam traps

- **12 hours** before departure; **every three months** for emergency steering drills; **weekly** for regular voyages of
  under one week. Do not mix these three numbers.
- **Follow-up** holds a set angle; **non-follow-up** moves only while the control is held.
- **If follow-up fails, switch to NFU first** is the reported lesson.
- **Two power units** running is for areas needing special caution, not all of the time.
- **Block diagram and instructions** are displayed in **two places**: the bridge and the steering gear compartment.
- **The log book is written by the person in charge of the deck watch.**
- **The rules cover vessels outside SOLAS Chapter V**; do not state them as covering every ship.
