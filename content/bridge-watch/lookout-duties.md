# Look-out duties

**Study aid only.** Rule 5 of the Collision Regulations, which makes the look-out a legal duty, is taught in the
COLREGs lesson on Rule 5. This lesson is about how you actually do the job.

## What you need to know

A look-out is not just "someone looking out of the window". It is a job with a purpose, a method and some limits. The
STCW principles Canada applies through the Marine Personnel Regulations (s. 213) give the purpose in three parts:

1. ==continuous vigilance== by sight, hearing and all other available means about any significant change in the
   surroundings;
2. a ==full appraisal of the situation and of the risk of collision, stranding and other dangers== to navigation; and
3. ==detecting ships or aircraft in distress, shipwrecked persons, wrecks and debris==.

The look-out is kept ==at all times==, by sight, by hearing and by all available means (Rule 5). Radar, AIS and the
electronic chart help the look-out, but they ==do not replace eyes and ears==.

## The look-out and the helmsperson are separate jobs

Steering takes concentration on the compass, the helm order and the rudder. The principles say the duties of
look-out and helmsperson are ==separate==, and the helmsperson is ==not considered to be the look-out while steering==.
The exception is a small ship that gives an ==unobstructed all-round view at the steering position==.

So on a ship with a rating and an officer, the usual split is: one steers, one keeps the look-out, and the officer
supervises and navigates. When one person must do both on a small vessel, the all-round view is the condition.

## The sole look-out

The officer may be the ==sole look-out== only if all of these are true:

- it is ==daylight==;
- the officer has ==carefully assessed== the situation, taking account of weather, visibility, traffic density, how
  close dangers are, and any traffic separation scheme; and
- ==assistance is immediately available== on the bridge if circumstances change.

Because the allowance is for daylight only, darkness is not covered. UK guidance (MGN 137) also advises against the
officer being the sole look-out in the dark, and calls for ==additional look-outs in restricted visibility==. That
guidance is not Canadian law, but it shows the common view.

## Seeing at night

Your eyes need time to adapt to the dark.

- **Time.** Full dark adaptation can take ==as long as 30 minutes==, though most of the improvement comes in the first
  few minutes. ==A brief exposure to bright light can undo it==. Research on bridge watches also notes that bright
  screens on modern bridges make full adaptation hard.
- **Where to look.** At night the centre of your vision sees poorly. Look ==5 to 10 degrees to one side== of the thing
  you want to see, and it often appears.
- **Dim lights.** Keep bridge lights dim. Dim red light is used when preparing for a night watch (research on bridge watches describes it that way).
- **Do not hold a bright screen in your view.** If you must read a display, look away from it afterwards and let your eyes recover.

Treat the numbers as study-aid detail from research. Your instructor may give slightly different ones; the ideas are
what matter.

## A scanning routine

Scan in steps, not in one fast sweep. A method from bridge simulator research is the "window wiper": divide the horizon
into ==10-degree sectors== and spend ==no more than about 4 seconds== in each, moving across and back. Look astern and
on the quarters as well as ahead.

**Worked example: how long is one scan?** Suppose the forward window covers 210 degrees and you scan in 10-degree
sectors with 4 seconds each.

1. Number of sectors = 210 ÷ 10 = 21.
2. One pass across = 21 × 4 s = 84 s.
3. Across and back = 84 × 2 = 168 s, or 2 minutes 48 seconds.

So a full careful scan takes nearly three minutes. That is why a look-out needs a rhythm, and why the watch should not
be asked to do other tasks at the same time.

## What to look and listen for

Besides ships, look for and listen for:

- lights, shapes and sound signals, and any change in them;
- aids to navigation and land;
- floating objects, ice, logs and fishing gear;
- persons in the water, wrecks and ships or aircraft that look as if they need help;
- changes in visibility such as fog banks or rain squalls.

Report **every** light, shape, sound signal and object, with its ==approximate bearing in degrees or points==. The next
lessons cover bearings and how to word a report.

## On the bridge: a short example

You are a rating on the afternoon watch crossing the Strait of Georgia on a 40 m vessel. You are not steering; the
other rating has the wheel. The officer is plotting a position.

1. You scan the horizon in sectors, spending a few seconds on each, and glance astern at the end of each sweep.
2. You hear a low engine noise that you cannot yet see. You report "Engine noise, port bow" at once.
3. The officer comes to the window, sees a log tug with a tow, and takes action.

You were **not** the one steering, so you had your full attention for looking and listening. That is the point of keeping the jobs separate.

## Exam traps

- "The helmsman is the look-out." Not while steering, except where the steering position has an unobstructed
  all-round view and the ship is small.
- "Radar is the look-out." It supports the look-out and does not replace sight and hearing.
- The sole look-out exception is **daylight only** and needs **assistance immediately available**.
- Stating the Rule 5 words as if they were different from the STCW purposes. They fit together: Rule 5 is the duty,
  the STCW principles give the purposes.
