# Bridge communications and teamwork

**Study aid only.** The helm order wording is taught in the helm orders lesson. Follow your ship's own phrases and
your master's standing orders.

## What you need to know

Most near-misses are not caused by a lack of equipment but by a message that was not heard, not understood or not
passed on. TP 10936 asks you to **understand orders**, **communicate with the officer of the watch (OOW)** and **use
shipboard terms** clearly. It also asks you to know the **internal communication and alarm systems** on board.

## Closed-loop communication

Closed loop is a simple habit with three steps:

1. **Order or report given** by the sender.
2. **Repeated back** by the receiver, in the same words.
3. **Confirmed** by the sender ("That is correct") or **corrected**.

Only when all three have happened is the loop closed. The same idea works the other way: when you, as a look-out,
report a vessel, the officer acknowledges ("Vessel, port bow, I have it"). If nothing comes back, repeat your report.

**Worked example.**

| Who | Says |
|---|---|
| OOW | "Slow ahead." (to engine room by telephone) |
| Engineer | "Slow ahead." |
| OOW | "Correct." |

If the engineer had said "Slow astern", the OOW would hear the error and correct it at once. Without the repeat the
error would not be noticed until the ship moved.

## If you do not understand, ask

If an order is unclear, you cannot hear it, or it does not seem to make sense, ==ask the OOW to clarify before you act==.
Guessing is the worst option. A short "Say again" or "Repeat the course, please" costs a second. A wrong action might
cost the ship.

## Use shipboard terms

Use the right words: **port**, **starboard**, **bow**, **stern**, **abeam**, **ahead**, **astern**, **bridge wing**.
Avoid "left" and "right", which depend on which way a person is facing, and vague words like "over there". Terms for
bearings are in the relative bearings lesson. The IMO has a set of Standard Marine Communication Phrases for the same
purpose; your training provider will show you which ones it uses.

## Working language: Navigation Safety Regulations, s. 137

- The master or authorized representative must ==determine the appropriate working language== for navigational safety
  matters and ==record it in the official log book==. Each crew member must be able to ==understand it, give orders and
  instructions in it, and report back in it==.
- If the working language is not an official language of the vessel's flag state, posted plans and written procedures need a
  translation into the working language as well as a copy in an official language.
- ==English must be used== for ==bridge-to-bridge== and ==bridge-to-shore== safety communications, and for communication on
  board between the ==pilot and the bridge watchkeepers==, unless everyone directly involved speaks a common language other
  than English.

On a BC coastal vessel the working language is usually English. A crew with mixed languages makes this rule important.

## Equipment you use to talk

| System | Use | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Engine room telephone or talk-back | Engine orders and reports | Learn how to use it before you need it |
| ==Two-way voice link to the emergency steering position== | Pass heading information if steering must be done locally | Required on a vessel with an emergency steering position (NSR s. 108) |
| General alarm and alarm panels | Raise the alarm | Know where each is and what each means |
| ==VHF radio== | Distress, urgency, safety and calling on ==channel 16==; bridge-to-bridge on ==channel 13== | Operator certificate rules apply to the person in charge of the watch |
| Public address, handheld radios | Move people and orders around the ship | Check batteries and channels at the start of the watch |

## Bridge resource management in plain words

"Bridge resource management" (BRM) is the idea of using everything on the bridge well: people, equipment and information.
The watchkeeping principles say the master and officer should arrange the watch sensibly, make sure everyone understands
their roles and responsibilities, and use available resources effectively.

For a rating, that means:

- know **your job** on this watch (look-out, wheel, reports);
- know **who to report to**, and report early;
- **ask** when unsure and **speak up** when something looks wrong, even to a senior person, politely and clearly;
- **repeat back** orders so the loop is closed;
- do not let a **distraction** (a phone, a conversation) take you off the job.

These habits are widely taught in BRM courses. Your training provider may use the terms "situational awareness" and "challenge and
response". They fit with the principles above, but those terms are not defined in the sources used for this lesson.

## On the bridge: a short example

A tug and barge are leaving the Fraser River. You are the additional person. The officer says "Bridge wing, look-out." You
repeat "Bridge wing, look-out" and go. Three minutes later you report: "Log, fine on the starboard bow, drifting." The
officer answers "Log, fine on the starboard bow." The loop is closed.

Ten minutes later the officer says something you cannot hear over the engine noise. You say: "Say again, please." The officer
repeats it. You repeat it back. That is all it takes.

## Exam traps

- **Repeat back**: the receiver repeats the order, then the sender confirms.
- **Ask first**: unclear order, ask before acting.
- **English** is mandatory for bridge-to-bridge, bridge-to-shore and pilot-to-bridge safety talk unless everyone shares another language.
- The **working language is recorded in the log book**.
- **Channel 16** is distress, urgency, safety and calling; **channel 13** is bridge-to-bridge.
- The two-way voice link goes **to the emergency steering position**.
