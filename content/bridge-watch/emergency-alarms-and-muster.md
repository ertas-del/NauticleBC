# Alarms, muster lists and emergency duties

**Study aid only.** Your own vessel's muster list is the authority for **your** station and duties. This lesson explains
what the Canadian regulation says and how the system works. The regulation read here is the Fire and Boat Drills
Regulations, SOR/2010-83 (FBDR).

## What you need to know

An emergency on a ship (fire, flooding, collision, abandoning the ship) is handled by people who each know their job
before it starts. Three things make that work:

1. A ==signal== that everyone recognises.
2. A ==muster list== that tells each person where to go and what to do.
3. ==Drills== that practise it until it is automatic.

## The alarm signals

Both signals are defined in s. 1 of the FBDR:

- The ==general emergency alarm signal== is "a succession of ==seven or more short blasts== followed by ==one long blast=="
  on the whistle or siren of a vessel (on many vessels the alarm bells ring the same pattern).
- The ==fire alarm signal== is the ==continuous sounding== of the vessel's fire alarm.

The detailed technical rules for alarm systems on the larger vessels come through SOLAS Chapter III and the LSA Code,
which the Vessel Construction and Equipment Regulations (s. 103) incorporate. Those documents were not read here; the
signal itself is defined in the FBDR.

**The abandon-ship order.** The muster list must describe **how the order to abandon ship is given** (s. 7(1)(b)). The
alarm calls everyone to their stations; it does not by itself order abandonment. On many vessels the master gives the
order by voice or public address, but the regulation only says the muster list must describe the method, so check yours.

## What you do when the alarm sounds

Section 19 of the FBDR is plain: when the general emergency alarm signal or the fire alarm signal sounds (unless a
drill notice said otherwise), crew members ==report to their designated muster stations== and prepare to perform their
assigned duties as described in the muster list. Passengers go to their designated muster stations. A practical routine:

1. Stop what you are doing, make safe anything dangerous (but do not delay).
2. Collect your lifejacket and any other gear the muster list says to collect.
3. Go to your station and report to the person in charge.
4. Do your assigned duty.

## The muster list

The master must make sure there is a muster list at all times (s. 3(1)), and a **rescue boat muster list** on a vessel
that has rescue boats. It must be written in either or both official languages according to the needs of the crew and
==conspicuously posted throughout the vessel, including the navigation bridge, the engine room and the crew
accommodations== (s. 3(2)).

A muster list must contain (s. 7(1)):

- a **description of the general emergency alarm signal**, the **fire alarm signal** and, if there is one, the **public
  address system**;
- a description of **how the order to abandon ship is given**;
- the **station** where each crew member reports;
- the **specific duties** of each crew member, including closing watertight doors, fire doors, valves and openings;
  equipping and launching survival craft; mustering passengers; using radio equipment; and fire party duties;
- the **substitutes for key persons** who may be disabled;
- the crew member responsible for **each survival craft** and a second-in-command;
- the crew member assigned as **principal communicator**;
- the crew members who keep the life saving and fire fighting equipment maintained and ready.

A vessel that carries passengers adds the actions passengers take and the crew duties toward passengers (s. 7(2)).

The master keeps the lists **up to date and legible**, and ==revises or replaces the muster list when the crew changes==
(s. 6). Small crews (fewer than three members and carrying passengers, or fewer than five and carrying none) need not
have a muster list if the master has another way of telling crew the essential actions in an emergency (s. 4).

Under the Marine Personnel Regulations, s. 205(5) and (6), people assigned on the muster list to a **fire team** or to
**preparing and launching survival craft** (other than manually launched life rafts) must hold the right marine
emergency duties certificates.

## Drills

- **As if real.** The master makes sure drills are, as far as feasible, carried out ==as if there were an actual
  emergency== (s. 17). **Passengers are told first** that a drill will be held and that there is no actual emergency
  (s. 16).
- **Every crew member, every month.** Each crew member must take part in at least ==one fire drill and one survival craft
  drill every month== (s. 21).
- **Intervals** depend on the vessel (s. 20 and the Schedule):

| Vessel | Period for a fire drill and a survival craft drill |
|---|---|
| Safety Convention passenger vessel | One week |
| Other vessel certified for 50 or more passengers, carrying passengers, not on a sheltered waters voyage | One week |
| Other vessel certified for fewer than 50 passengers, carrying passengers, not sheltered | Two weeks |
| Passenger vessel on a sheltered waters voyage | Two weeks |
| Cargo vessel | One month |
| Fishing vessel over 150 gross tonnage | One month |

- **Extra drill on departure.** If more than 25 percent of the crew did not take part in a fire drill (or survival craft
  drill) in the month before the vessel sails, one is held within 24 hours after departure (s. 20(2) and (3)).
- **Equipment ready again.** Anything used in a drill must be returned to full working condition at once (s. 18).
- **Records (s. 37).** The master records the date of each muster, a detailed report of each fire drill, survival craft
  drill and rescue boat drill, any drill not held (and why), and an evaluation of the crew's readiness.
- **Rest periods.** Under the Marine Personnel Regulations, s. 322(3), the hours-of-rest rules do not apply during
  practice musters, fire drills or survival craft drills **if** the master minimises the disturbance of rest and does not
  induce fatigue.

## Which vessels?

The FBDR apply to **self-propelled Canadian vessels** that are Safety Convention vessels or must hold a safety
certificate. They do **not** apply to **fishing vessels of 150 gross tonnage or less**, **cable ferries**, or **vessels of 15
gross tonnage or less that carry 12 or fewer passengers** (s. 2). Smaller passenger vessels have their own duty in the Small
Vessel Regulations (s. 420): the owner and operator must establish procedures for using life-saving appliances and
firefighting equipment, and the crew must practise them.

## A BC example

A small BC passenger vessel certified for 40 passengers runs a daily tour from a dock in the Strait of Georgia (not a
sheltered waters voyage). The muster list is posted on the bridge, in the engine room and in the crew mess. A new deckhand
joins on Monday morning; the master revises the list before sailing, and the deckhand learns his station. The vessel holds
a fire drill and a survival craft drill at least once every two weeks, and the new deckhand takes part in his first within
the month. Before the drill the master announces to passengers on the public address: "This is a drill; there is no
emergency."

## Exam traps

- **Seven OR MORE short blasts, then one long**, not "seven short then two long".
- **Fire alarm = continuous sounding**, which is **not** the same pattern as the general alarm.
- **The alarm is not the abandon-ship order.** The muster list describes how that order is given.
- **The muster list is posted on the bridge, engine room and crew accommodations.**
- **Monthly** crew participation, but the **vessel's drill interval** depends on type (one week, two weeks, one month).
- **Passengers must be told it is a drill** before the signal is sounded.
- **Know which vessels the regulations cover**; do not claim they cover every vessel.
