# Magnetic and gyro compasses

**Study aid only.** Compass facts here support steering and watchkeeping. They do not replace the instruction manual of
the compass on your vessel or a compass adjuster's work.

## What you need to know

A **compass** tells you which way the ship's head is pointing. There are two kinds on most vessels:

- The ==magnetic compass==: a magnetised card that lines up with the Earth's magnetic field. It needs no power.
- The ==gyro-compass==: a spinning wheel that finds true north. It needs power. It feeds **repeaters** around the ship.

The magnetic compass is the one you can always trust to exist, because it needs nothing but the Earth. The gyro is
more accurate and has no iron-related error, but it needs electricity.

## The magnetic compass: parts

Think of the compass from the outside in (a United States Navy seaman text describes the same layout):

| Part | What it is and does |
|---|---|
| ==Bowl== | Brass or bronze case holding the card in a liquid, which damps the card's swing. |
| ==Card== | Round card with a scale and magnets underneath. It tries to point at magnetic north. |
| ==Lubber line== | A fixed mark on the bowl, lined up with the ship's centreline. The number on the card at the lubber line is the ship's compass heading. |
| ==Gimbals== | Rings that keep the bowl level when the ship rolls and pitches. |
| ==Binnacle== | The stand that holds the compass. It carries the **correctors**: small magnets and soft-iron spheres placed to cancel some of the ship's own magnetism. |

The ship **turns under the card** (see the steering technique lesson). The card keeps its direction and the lubber line
moves around it.

## Why the magnetic compass is wrong, and by how much

A magnetic compass points toward **magnetic north**, not true north. The difference between them is **variation**, which
depends on where you are. The ship's own iron and electrical gear also pull on the card; that pull is **deviation**, and
it changes with the ship's heading. Both are explained, with sums, in the compass error lesson. The binnacle correctors
reduce deviation but never remove all of it. What is left is called **residual deviation** and is recorded in a table or
curve.

## What the Navigation Safety Regulations require (NSR 2020, ss. 106, 109, 132)

These sections are in Division 3 of Part 1 (vessels not subject to SOLAS Chapter V, per s. 105); larger SOLAS vessels
meet equivalent rules through SOLAS Chapter V, which was not read for this lesson.

| Item | Rule |
|---|---|
| **Standard magnetic compass** (s. 106(1)) | Every vessel must have one, ==independent of any power supply==, able to show the heading **at the main steering position**. |
| **Exceptions** (s. 106(1)) | Vessels of **8 m or less** that stay **within sight of navigation marks**, and **cable ferries**. |
| **Under 150 GT** (s. 106(2)) | May instead have a **steering magnetic compass** (plus a gyro-compass if on an international voyage). |
| **150 to under 500 GT** (s. 106(3)) | A steering magnetic compass may do on sheltered-waters, inland or near coastal Class 2 voyages that are not international; on near coastal Class 1, unlimited or international voyages, a steering magnetic compass **and a gyro-compass**. |
| **Compensation** (s. 106(4)) | The compass must be properly **compensated** and its **table or curve of residual deviations** must be kept on board **near the compass**. |
| **Correction to true** (s. 106(5)) | A vessel with a magnetic compass (except small pleasure craft) must have a way of **correcting headings and bearings to true at all times**. |
| **Communication** (s. 106(6)) | There must be a means of communication between the standard compass position and the position the vessel is normally navigated from. |
| **Spare compass** (s. 109(1)(c)) | A vessel of **150 GT or more** must carry a **spare magnetic compass interchangeable** with the first. |
| **Inspection** (s. 132) | The master must show the inspector the latest **deviation card** for each compass, **signed and dated by the master and a deck officer**, or a signed statement that the compasses have been found satisfactory. |

## The gyro-compass

A gyro-compass has a fast-spinning wheel mounted so that it settles with its axis along the meridian, pointing at
==true north== (the axis of the Earth's rotation). Consequences:

- **No variation.** It points to true north, so there is no variation to apply. A small **gyro error** can remain and is
  named east or west (see the compass error lesson).
- **No deviation.** It is not affected by the ship's iron.
- **Needs power.** The wheel must be running. If power fails, the gyro stops being reliable.
- **Settling time.** After starting, a gyro-compass takes time to settle on north. Do not trust it before it has
  settled; follow the instructions for your equipment.
- **Steaming error.** Rapid changes of course, speed or latitude disturb it for a while before it recovers.
- **Repeaters.** The master gyro drives **repeaters** by electrical signal: on the bridge, on the wings and at the
  emergency steering position. On vessels of 500 gross tonnage or more on voyages other than sheltered waters, NSR
  2020 s. 111(c) requires a gyro heading repeater at the emergency steering position (if there is one) and a gyro
  bearing repeater for taking bearings.

Report any gyro alarm or a disagreement between the gyro and the magnetic compass to the OOW **at once**.

## High latitudes

TP 10936 lists the limitations of both compasses in high latitudes. A magnetic compass works on the **horizontal part** of
the Earth's field, and that part ==weakens as you approach the magnetic pole==, so the card becomes sluggish and unreliable.
Gyro-compasses also lose reliability at high latitudes. The sources read for this lesson give no latitude limits, so
do not learn numbers from here; ask your instructor if your course expects them.

## A BC example

A small BC coastal vessel has a standard magnetic compass in a binnacle over the wheelhouse and a gyro repeater at the
steering position. On the way out the helmsman notices that the gyro repeater and the magnetic compass disagree by
five degrees more than the posted error. He tells the OOW at once. The OOW checks the gyro alarm panel and compares
both compasses with a visual bearing. The deviation card, signed by the master and a deck officer, hangs next to the
compass, as the regulations require (an inspector can ask to see it).

## Exam traps

- **Magnetic compass: independent of power; gyro: needs power.** If a question mentions a power failure, the magnetic
  compass is the answer.
- **Magnetic compass points to magnetic north; gyro points to true north.**
- **Deviation depends on the ship's heading**; variation depends on the place.
- **A deviation table is kept near the compass**; a deviation card is shown to the inspector.
- **Spare compass at 150 GT or more**; the steering compass may be enough for smaller vessels.
- **Parts**: the lubber line is a mark on the bowl, not on the card.
- **High latitudes** are a problem for both compasses.
