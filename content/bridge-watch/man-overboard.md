# Man overboard: the look-out's job

**Study aid only.** Your vessel's own man overboard procedure and the master's orders come first. The turns below are
standard seamanship patterns; the exact action depends on the ship, the weather and how the person went into the
water. Practise them in drills.

## What you need to know

When someone goes into the water, **seconds count** and **sight is everything**. A person's head in a seaway is
visible for only a short distance, and once lost it is very hard to find again. The first person to see the accident
therefore has two jobs: **raise the alarm** and **keep the person in view**.

## The first seconds (the person who sees it)

1. ==Shout "Man overboard"== and call the **side**: "Man overboard, starboard side!" (or port). The side tells the
   helm which way to turn.
2. ==Throw or release a lifebuoy== at once, preferably one with a light and a smoke signal, so there is something
   floating near the person and a mark on the water.
3. ==Point at the person== and keep pointing. ==Never take your eyes or your arm away== until recovery is complete.
   If you have to move, hand over the pointing to someone else. The look-out is the best "tracking device" the ship has.
4. Make sure the bridge has been told. If you can reach the intercom or radio without taking your eyes off the person,
   do so. Otherwise shout again until someone answers.

## The bridge (the officer of the watch)

Typical actions (follow your own vessel's procedure):

- Put the helm ==hard over toward the side the person fell==, which swings the stern, and the propeller, away from
  the person. (The sources read say to put the helm over toward the casualty's side; the reason for it is common
  seamanship, so confirm with your instructor.)
- Change to **hand steering** if on autopilot (see the autopilot changeover lesson).
- Sound the **alarm** and call all hands; post extra look-outs.
- **Mark the position** (GPS or ECDIS man overboard button), and note the time, wind and heading.
- Display flag ==O== and call on VHF as the vessel's procedure requires.
- Choose the recovery manoeuvre and order the rudder and engine orders. (Use the helm orders from the earlier lesson.)

A trade website (Marine Insight) and a training site list **three prolonged whistle blasts** as a man overboard signal;
the sources read do not tie this to a regulation, so check your vessel's procedure and do not rely on it as a legal fact.

## Flag O

In the International Code of Signals, the single-letter flag ==O means "Man overboard"==. It tells nearby vessels that
someone is in the water.

## The three standard turns

All three bring the ship back to the position where the person went in. They differ in how quickly and in what
conditions they work. Examples use invented headings: the ship is steering **090** and the person falls from the
**starboard** side.

### Williamson turn

1. Put the rudder **hard over toward the casualty** (starboard).
2. When the heading has changed by ==60 degrees== (to **150**), put the rudder **hard over the other way** (port).
3. When the heading is about ==20 degrees short of the reciprocal course== (the reciprocal of 090 is **270**, so the
   rudder is centred at about **290**), put the rudder **midships**. The ship settles on about **270**.

Arithmetic: 090 + 60 = **150**; reciprocal = 090 + 180 = **270**; 20 degrees short, coming from the north-west side as
the ship swings to port, is 270 + 20 = **290**.

**Use:** ==any visibility and weather==; **delayed action** (the person was missed for some time) and **person
missing**. It puts the ship on the reciprocal course of the original track, so she runs back along her own wake toward
the place where the person fell. **Drawback:** it takes the ship further from the person at first, so it is not the
quickest when the person is still in sight.

### Anderson turn

1. Put the rudder **hard over toward the casualty** (starboard) and keep it there.
2. Turn about ==250 degrees== (some texts say 240). From 090 to starboard this is 090 + 250 = **340**.
3. Centre the rudder and reduce speed or stop so that the person is near the bow (about 15 degrees off the bow is a
   common target) and not near the propeller.

**Use:** ==immediate action== when the person is **still in sight**; it is the **quickest** because the ship makes one
turn and comes back. **Drawback:** it is not suited to poor visibility or to a person who has drifted out of sight,
because it relies on seeing the person throughout.

### Scharnow turn

1. Put the rudder **hard over** toward the casualty's side.
2. After about ==240 degrees== of turn (090 + 240 = **330**), put the rudder **hard the other way**.
3. When the heading is about **20 degrees short of the reciprocal** (about **290** here), centre the rudder.

**Use:** when the person is **well astern**, farther than the ship's turning circle, or the report was delayed. The ship
ends on the reciprocal course **in her own wake**. **Drawback:** it takes longer than the others in many cases.

### Summary table

| Turn | First turn | Second rudder | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|
| Williamson | 60 degrees toward the casualty | hard over the other way, centre about 20 degrees short of reciprocal | Any visibility; delayed report or missing person |
| Anderson | about 250 degrees, single turn | none; centre and stop | Immediate action, person in sight |
| Scharnow | about 240 degrees | hard over the other way, centre about 20 degrees short of reciprocal | Person well astern or delayed |

Sources disagree in small ways (for example 240 or 250 degrees for the Anderson), and the web pages read for this lesson
are secondary. Ask your instructor which numbers your examiner expects.

## Equipment: the Small Vessel Regulations

For a **passenger-carrying vessel of not more than 15 gross tonnage that carries 12 or fewer passengers** (Part 4), the Small
Vessel Regulations require, as personal life-saving appliances in addition to lifejackets (s. 409):

| Vessel length | Additional appliance |
|---|---|
| Not more than 6 m | A ==buoyant heaving line of not less than 15 m== |
| More than 6 m, up to 9 m | A buoyant heaving line of not less than 15 m, **or** a lifebuoy attached to a buoyant line of not less than 15 m |
| More than 9 m, up to 12 m | A buoyant heaving line of not less than 15 m, **and** a lifebuoy attached to a buoyant line of not less than 15 m |
| More than 12 m | A buoyant heaving line of not less than 15 m, **and** a lifebuoy equipped with a self-igniting light **or** attached to a buoyant line of not less than 15 m |

The vessel must also carry a **reboarding device**, ==unless the vertical height that must be climbed to reboard is not
more than 0.5 m==. The heaving line has a buoyant mass at one end to help it carry when thrown.

## Cold water

A person in cold water faces ==cold shock (about 1 minute)==, ==cold incapacitation (about 10 minutes of useful
movement)== and ==hypothermia (about 1 hour before unconsciousness)==, according to the Canadian Safe Boating Council. In
BC waters, which are cold all year, a person who falls overboard is on a clock the moment they hit the water. A
lifejacket keeps them afloat through the first two stages.

## A BC example

A deckhand on a small BC tug towing along the coast slips on the afterdeck and falls over the port quarter. The other
deckhand on the afterdeck shouts "Man overboard, port side!", throws the lifebuoy, and points at the deckhand in the
water. The mate on the bridge hears the shout and puts the wheel hard over to port, then presses the MOB button on
the plotter. The person is still clearly in sight in daylight, so the mate chooses an **Anderson** turn (a single turn
to port) and stops the engine with the person near the bow. The pointing deckhand never takes his arm down.

## Exam traps

- **Say the side**: "port side" or "starboard side".
- **Never stop pointing.** If you must leave, hand the job to someone else.
- **Hard over toward the side of the casualty**: not away.
- **Williamson = 60 degrees, then opposite rudder, centre 20 degrees short of reciprocal.** The Williamson is the turn for
  any visibility.
- **Anderson = single turn of about 250 degrees**, best when the person is still in sight.
- **Scharnow = about 240 degrees, then opposite**, for a person well astern.
- **Flag O is Man overboard.**
- **The 15 m heaving line** goes with the length table; do not mix the table rows.
