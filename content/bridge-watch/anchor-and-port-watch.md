# Watch at anchor and in port

**Study aid only.** Anchoring technique is taught in the seamanship module. Follow your ship's procedures and the
master's orders. Several oil-transfer rules below apply only to vessels above stated sizes, so check which apply to you.

## What you need to know

A ship that is not moving still needs a watch. TP 10936 treats the watch ==at sea, at anchor and in port== as
three situations, each with its own dangers: dragging, moorings, pollution and visitors. The look-out and bridge watch
continue in all three.

## The anchor watch

When the ship anchors, the officer must:

- ==determine and plot the ship's position== promptly;
- check at ==frequent intervals== that the ship remains ==securely anchored==, using ==bearings== of fixed marks;
- keep a proper look-out and make ==periodic rounds== of the ship;
- observe the ==weather, tide and sea state==;
- ==notify the master== if the anchor ==drags==, and also if visibility deteriorates;
- keep the main engine ready as the master orders;
- show the right ==lights, shapes and sound signals==;
- take steps to ==prevent pollution==.

### Is the anchor dragging?

A dragging anchor moves the ship over the bottom. Signs to watch for:

- **Bearings change.** A bearing to a fixed mark that was steady now moves.
- **The chain.** Watch the strain on the chain and the way it is leading (tending). A change in the strain or the lead is a warning.
- **Drift lead.** A line weighted and lowered to the bottom shows if the ship is moving: if there is no slack in the line
  and it tends noticeably forward, the anchor is probably dragging, and ==the bridge needs to know at once==.
- **Weather change.** Wind or tide changing, or a swell building, can pull an anchor loose.

Report any of these at once. The officer decides whether to veer more cable, start the engine or heave up.

### Anchor lights

An anchored vessel shows (Rule 30):

- ==an all-round white light in the fore part== and ==a second all-round white light at or near the stern, lower than the forward one==;
- under 50 m, ==one all-round white light== where it can best be seen may be shown instead;
- by day ==one ball== in the fore part.

If you are on the watch, check the lights come on at dusk and are still lit.

## In port, alongside

Alongside, the watch looks after the ship, the people and the mooring lines.

- **Moorings.** The tide rises and falls and the load changes, so lines go slack or tight. A watch checks them and tells the
  officer when they need adjusting.
- **Gangway.** Keep it safe and lit, and control who comes aboard.
- **Rat guards.** TP 10936 lists them as a topic: they are fitted on lines to stop rats using the lines to board. Your
  instructor will show the practice on your vessel.
- **Visitors and security.** Know who is aboard.

### Snap-back and parted lines

A mooring line under strain stores energy. If it breaks it whips back along its line of pull, an area called the
==snap-back zone==, and can kill or maim. Guidance says:

- stay out of ==snap-back zones== while lines are under strain, and the zone grows where a line goes round a roller or
  pedestal fairlead;
- ==never stand in a bight== (a loop or turn) of rope or wire;
- if a line shows excessive strain, reduce the load at once.

If a line **does** part, the general response is to raise the alarm, tell the officer, keep everyone clear of the
lines and the bight, and put out new lines. The sources read for this lesson did not give a set procedure, so confirm
the steps with your instructor.

## Bulk oil transfers (fuel and oily mixtures)

The Vessel Pollution and Dangerous Chemicals Regulations set rules for loading or unloading oil in bulk. For a rating the
main points are:

- ==Continuous two-way voice communication== between the supervisor on board and the supervisor at the facility or other vessel,
  able to ==direct an immediate shutdown== in an emergency (s. 33).
- Between ==sunset and sunrise==: at least ==54 lx== at each connection point and ==11 lx== at each work area (s. 34),
  measured on a horizontal plane 1 m above the walking or working surface.
- If a conduit or connection ==leaks==, the supervisors must ==slow down or stop== the transfer to take the pressure off it (s. 35(4)).
- The person on duty ==watches the tanks so they do not overflow== and keeps ==continuous communication== with their
  counterpart; the vessel is secured and the mooring lines tended so the ship's movement does not damage the hose (s. 38).
- Transfer conduits must have a bursting pressure of at least four times their design pressure, be marked with it and have
  passed a hydrostatic test within the year before use (s. 35).

Some of these duties apply only to oil tankers of 150 GT or more and other vessels of 400 GT or more, so a small vessel may
be under fewer of them (s. 32(2)). Plugging scuppers and having a spill kit ready are standard practice but were not found
in the regulation text read here, so ask your instructor.

If the tank starts to overflow or a hose bursts: ==stop the transfer==, raise the alarm and contain the spill.

## Worked example: anchor check by bearings

You anchor in a bay. The officer takes two bearings at 2000: Point A at 040 (compass) and Point B at 120.

1. At 2030 you take them again: A is 040 and B is 120. **No change**: the ship is holding.
2. At 2100 A is 046 and B is 112.
3. A moved by 6 degrees and B by 8 degrees. The ship is moving, **so it is probably dragging**. You report: "Bearings
   changing, A 046, B 112, possible dragging." The officer checks, calls the master and decides on action.

The numbers are invented. The point is that steady bearings mean holding, changing bearings mean movement.

## Exam traps

- **Anchor watch includes bearings**, not only looking.
- **Report dragging at once** and tell the master (the officer does).
- A **continuous** two-way link is needed for a transfer, not "radio call when needed".
- **54 lx and 11 lx** are for transfers **between sunset and sunrise**.
- A **leak** means **slow or stop**, not "keep going to finish".
- **Snap-back zones** and **bights**: stay out.
