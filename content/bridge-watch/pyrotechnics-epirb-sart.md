# Flares, EPIRBs and SARTs

**Study aid only.** Real equipment must meet the current regulations and follow the maker's instructions. The facts below
are from the Small Vessel Regulations (SVR), SOR/2010-91, and the Navigation Safety Regulations, 2020 (NSR 2020),
SOR/2020-216.

## What you need to know

When a vessel is in distress, three families of equipment tell rescuers where it is:

- **Pyrotechnic distress signals**: flares and smoke, seen by eye.
- **EPIRBs and PLBs**: radio beacons that send a satellite alert with the vessel's identity and position.
- **SARTs**: transponders that make a ship's or craft's position show on a rescuer's radar.

A rating must recognise each, know where they are stowed, and know the rules on life, testing and false alerts.

## Pyrotechnic distress signals

The SVR (s. 1) defines a ==pyrotechnic distress signal== as one of four things:

1. a ==rocket parachute flare==,
2. a ==multi-star flare==,
3. a ==hand flare==, or
4. a ==buoyant or hand smoke signal==.

(They are distress signals in Rule 37 and Annex IV of the Collision Regulations; see the earlier COLREGs lesson.) A
rocket parachute flare climbs high and burns red while it floats down, so it is seen from a long way. A hand flare
burns where you hold it, for close-up signalling. Smoke is for daylight; the SVR counts smoke signals as part of the
total, with a limit on how many may be smoke.

**Life: four years.** A pyrotechnic distress signal required by the SVR ==expires four years after its date of
manufacture== (s. 20(1)). Look at the date printed on the signal; an expired flare is unreliable and does not count.

**Numbers for small passenger vessels.** Under SVR Part 4 (passenger-carrying vessels of not more than 15 gross tonnage
carrying not more than 12 passengers), s. 410 requires:

| Vessel length | Watertight flashlight | Pyrotechnic distress signals |
|---|---|---|
| Not more than 6 m | Yes | **3**, none of them smoke signals |
| More than 6 m up to 9 m | Yes | **6**, none of them smoke signals |
| More than 9 m | Yes | **12**, of which **not more than 6** are smoke signals |

## EPIRBs and PLBs

The NSR 2020 define an ==EPIRB== (emergency position indicating radio beacon) and a ==PLB== (personal locator beacon) as
operating in the ==406 MHz band==. The Canadian Coast Guard Notice to Mariners explains that only 406 MHz beacons are
detected by the Cospas-Sarsat satellite system, so a 406 MHz beacon is the one that matters. Each EPIRB and PLB must be of a
type approved by Cospas-Sarsat (s. 229).

**Registration.** The authorized representative of the vessel must ==register the beacon with the Canadian Beacon
Registry==, maintained by the Department of National Defence, and ==update the registration within 30 days== after any
change in the information (s. 228). The Coast Guard also advises checking registration yearly. Unregistered beacons
slow down rescue because rescuers cannot immediately identify the vessel.

**Float-free EPIRB (s. 209).** A vessel must have one if she is on a **near coastal voyage, Class 1** or an **unlimited
voyage**, or if she is **more than 12 m** and on an **inland voyage** or a **near coastal voyage, Class 2**. It must be
fitted so it can **float free** if the vessel sinks, be **readily accessible** near the navigating position (unless it can
be activated by remote control from there), and be **manually released and carried into a survival craft**.

**Smaller vessels.** A vessel of **12 m or less** on certain inland waters (the Great Lakes, their connecting and tributary
waters or the St. Lawrence) or a near coastal voyage, Class 2, must be equipped with a float-free EPIRB, **or** a
manually activated EPIRB, **or** a PLB, or, for a vessel **under 8 m in length operating only in sea area A1**, a
waterproof portable VHF handheld radio capable of DSC. The manual EPIRB, PLB or DSC handheld must be ==worn by the
person in charge of the navigational watch== or, if that is not practicable, stowed so it can be reached at once if the
vessel is abandoned (s. 209(4)).

## SARTs

A **SART** is "a transponder designed for search and rescue purposes that operates on a radar frequency or an AIS
frequency" (NSR s. 200). The common kind is the **radar SART**:

- It only answers ==9 GHz (X-band) radar==. It does not respond to 3 GHz radar.
- When it is interrogated, its reply shows on the rescuer's radar as a ==line of 12 dots== running outward from its
  position.
- As the rescuer closes to within about 1 nautical mile the dots become ==arcs, then full circles==, so you can tell you
  are very close.
- Range depends on the height of the rescuer's radar antenna, but the source read gives roughly 8 nautical miles.

One of the SARTs required on board must be stowed so it is **readily accessible for immediate use** (s. 208). A SART
battery is replaced by its expiry date (s. 231).

## Testing and maintenance

- **Inspection and test (s. 239).** An EPIRB or PLB (other than one stowed in an inflatable life raft) and a SART must be
  inspected and tested by a **radio operator** on installation and at least ==once every six months== in accordance with
  the manufacturer's instructions.
- **EPIRB release and battery (s. 230).** The automatic release mechanism is marked with the replacement date and replaced
  on or before it. An EPIRB or PLB battery is replaced **after use in an emergency, after an inadvertent activation,
  if inspection or testing requires it, and on or before its expiry date**.
- **Do not use past expiry.** The Coast Guard advises that a beacon must not be used past its battery expiry date, whatever
  the self-test says.
- **Check dates.** Flare dates (four years), battery and release dates, and the registration.

## False alerts

A false alert wastes rescue resources and may delay help for a real emergency. If a beacon is **accidentally activated**,
the Coast Guard Notice to Mariners says to **switch it from ON to ARMED or SAFE** and **contact the Canadian Mission
Control Centre** (the phone numbers are in the Notice to Mariners and should be checked before you rely on them; the
numbers read were 1-800-211-8107 and 613-965-7265). Keeping the registration up to date lets rescuers resolve the alert
quickly, perhaps with a single call to the owner. NSR 2020 s. 230 also requires the battery to be replaced after an
inadvertent activation.

## A BC example

A small BC whale-watching vessel of 11 m, working near coastal Class 2, carries a manual EPIRB in a bracket by the
helm, and the skipper wears a PLB on his lifejacket. The EPIRB is registered with the Canadian Beacon Registry. When the
vessel changes owner, the new owner updates the registration within 30 days. The flare kit holds twelve signals, not more than
six of them smoke, but the box shows manufacture dates of three and five years ago. The five-year-old flares have
expired and are replaced, even though they look fine. (An 11 m passenger vessel falls under the "more than 9 m" row.)

## Exam traps

- **Four years from manufacture**, not from purchase.
- **Four kinds of pyrotechnic signal**: rocket parachute, multi-star, hand flare, smoke (buoyant or hand).
- **406 MHz** is the beacon band; **9 GHz (X-band)** is the SART's radar band. Do not swap them.
- **12 dots** on the radar for a SART, becoming arcs and then circles very close.
- **30 days** to update the beacon registry; **six months** for beacon and SART testing; **four years** for flares.
- **Float-free** EPIRB by voyage class or size; **manual EPIRB or PLB** for 12 m or less in certain cases, worn by the
  person in charge of the navigational watch.
- **After a false alert**: switch off and call the Mission Control Centre, then replace the battery.
