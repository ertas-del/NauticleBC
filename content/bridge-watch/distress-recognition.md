# Recognising and reporting distress

**Study aid only.** The full list of Annex IV signals is taught in the COLREGs lesson on Annex IV; this lesson is about
what you do when you meet one. A distress call is never a drill to a real vessel: follow the officer's orders.

## What you need to know

TP 10936 expects a Bridge Watch Rating to recognise distress signals given ==by sight, by sound, by radiotelephone
and from an aircraft==, and to ==report them immediately to the master or officer of the watch==. One purpose of
keeping a look-out is also to detect ships or aircraft in distress, shipwrecked persons, wrecks and debris. You are
often the first person to see or hear it.

## The three radio words

On radiotelephone the first word of the message tells the listener how serious it is. It is normally said three
times, then the message follows.

| Word | Meaning | Example use |
|---|---|---|
| ==MAYDAY== | ==Distress==: grave and imminent danger, immediate help needed | Vessel on fire or sinking |
| ==PAN PAN== | ==Urgency==: a very urgent message about the safety of a vessel or person, but not in immediate grave danger | Engine failure in a busy channel; a crew member badly hurt |
| ==SECURITE== | ==Safety==: a navigational or weather warning | Gale warning, floating hazard |

(SECURITE comes from the French "sécurité".)

==VHF channel 16 (156.8 MHz)== is the international channel for distress, urgency, safety and calling. Digital Selective
Calling (DSC) alerts on VHF go out on ==channel 70==. A DSC alert is a digital call that sounds an alarm on receiving
radios; the voice message follows on channel 16.

## Annex IV signals you may see or hear

These are the signals the Collision Regulations list as meaning distress and need of assistance. The Annex IV lesson
has the whole list; here are the ones a look-out is most likely to spot.

| By | Signal |
|---|---|
| **Sound** | ==Gun or explosive signal== at about ==one-minute intervals==; ==continuous sounding== of any fog-signalling apparatus |
| **Sight, pyrotechnic** | Rockets or shells throwing ==red stars==; a ==red== parachute or hand flare; ==orange smoke== |
| **Sight, other** | ==Flames== on the vessel (for example a burning barrel); flags ==N over C==; a square flag with a ball above or below it; ==slowly raising and lowering arms== stretched out at the sides |
| **Radio** | MAYDAY on radiotelephone; DSC distress alert; EPIRB signals; SOS by any means |

A distress signal ==must not be used for any other purpose==. If you are not sure whether something is a distress signal, report what you saw or heard and let the officer decide.

## Distress from the air

Aircraft may be part of a rescue. The look-out may see or hear:

- an aircraft **in distress**: it may call MAYDAY by radio, or give other signals;
- an aircraft **directing a ship to a casualty**. Search and rescue aircraft use three manoeuvres in order:
  1. ==circle the ship at least once==;
  2. ==cross low ahead of the ship's projected course== while ==rocking the wings==;
  3. ==head in the direction== the ship is to follow.

Wing rocking is used instead of changing the engine tone because the engine noise on a ship may mask the sound.
If you see this and the officer has already seen it, you can be useful by watching the aircraft and reporting the
direction it takes.

An aircraft also has a signal that means the ship's help is no longer needed (passing close astern and low while
rocking its wings, in the SOLAS regulation that lists these signals). That signal was **not confirmed** in the Canadian
Coast Guard page used for this course. Check it with your instructor before relying on it.

## How to report distress

Report at once, then keep watching and listening. A good first report has three parts:

1. **What** you saw or heard: "Red flare", "Gun shots, one a minute", "MAYDAY call on 16", "aircraft circling".
2. **Where**: the relative bearing, the side and the approximate distance.
3. **When**: the time, or "just now". The officer will note the time in the log.

**Worked example.** You are on watch heading 270 compass. At 0215 you see a red parachute flare burst on the
starboard bow, about 050 relative.

1. Report: "Red parachute flare, starboard bow, 050 relative, just now." The officer acknowledges.
2. The officer wants a compass bearing. Compass bearing = heading + relative = 270 + 050 = 320, so the flare lies at
   about **320 compass**.
3. You keep your eyes on that direction and report any more flares. Together with the time of the first flare, the
   bearing helps the officer find the position.

## On the bridge

Foggy night on the coast of Vancouver Island. You hear a fog horn sounding without a break, continuously. A buoy or a
vessel might sound at intervals, but this does not stop. You report it at once: "Continuous fog horn, one point on the
port bow." A continuous sounding of any fog-signalling apparatus is an Annex IV distress signal, so the officer informs
the master and acts.

## Exam traps

- **MAYDAY, PAN PAN and SECURITE** ranked: distress, urgency, safety.
- **Channel 16** is voice; **channel 70** is the digital alert.
- A continuous fog signal is a distress signal; a normal fog signal is at intervals.
- An aircraft **directing** a ship **circles**, **crosses low ahead rocking its wings** and **heads off**.
- Always **report immediately**, even if you doubt it.

4. Give two sound and three visual Annex IV distress signals.
