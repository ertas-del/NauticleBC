# Look-out reports and steady bearings

**Study aid only.** The collision rules named here are taught in the COLREGs module. This lesson shows how a rating
uses them when reporting.

## What you need to know

A look-out is only useful if the officer of the watch hears about what the look-out sees and hears. TP 10936 expects
you to detect and report sound signals, lights and objects ==promptly==, giving their ==approximate bearing in
degrees or points==, and to use ==closed-loop communication==. Closed loop means you give the message, the other person
acknowledges it, and you know it was received.

## What a good report contains

Use a pattern the officer can take in at once:

1. **What**: "white light", "fishing boat", "log", "bell buoy", "fog signal".
2. **Where**: the **side** and the **bearing**: "starboard bow, 030 relative" or "two points on the port bow".
3. **If you can tell**: how far (near, far) and whether it is **closing**, **steady** or **drawing** left or right.

Example: "Vessel, red and white lights, two points on the starboard bow, bearing steady."

Report **first**, then keep watching. Waiting until you are certain wastes seconds. The officer asked you to be a
look-out so you can pass on information at once, not so that you can reach a conclusion about it.

If the officer does not answer, ==repeat the report==. Silence might mean the message was not heard. Speak clearly,
and louder if the bridge is noisy.

## Report more than ships

A look-out looks for danger and for people in need, so report:

- ==aids to navigation== such as buoys, beacons and lights;
- ==floating objects==: logs, nets, debris, ice;
- ==persons in the water==, wrecks, ships or aircraft that may be in distress (see the distress recognition lesson);
- ==changes in visibility==: a fog bank, rain squall or haze;
- land and other dangers to navigation.

## Steady bearing means danger

Two vessels collide only if they arrive at the same point at the same time. If another vessel is **closing** and
her **bearing from you stays the same**, you are on a collision course. That is what Rule 7 means by a **steady
bearing**: if the ==compass bearing of an approaching vessel does not appreciably change==, risk of collision is
==deemed to exist==.

The look-out's part is to notice and report. "Bearing steady" is a short, serious report the officer wants to hear.

**Compass bearing, not relative bearing.** The test is the compass bearing. Imagine you are heading 090 and see a vessel at
030 relative: the compass bearing is 090 + 030 = 120. Later you alter course to 110 and the same vessel is now at
010 relative: 110 + 010 = 120. The compass bearing has not moved, so the situation has not changed, even though the
relative bearing went from 030 to 010. If you are steady on course, relative and compass bearings change together, so
you can watch a fixed mark on the ship, but once the ship alters course you must go back to the compass.

**Risk may exist even if the bearing changes.** The rule warns that risk can still exist when the bearing is
changing, particularly when approaching a ==very large vessel==, a ==tow== or ==any vessel at close range==. A tug
and tow can be very long, so the bearing of the tug may drift while the tow still crosses your path.

## Worked example: how long is left?

You report a vessel on a steady bearing. Radar ranges are:

| Time | Range |
|---|---|
| 1000 | 4.0 nautical miles |
| 1003 | 3.0 nautical miles |
| 1006 | 2.0 nautical miles |

1. The range closes 1.0 nautical mile every 3 minutes.
2. Closing speed = 1.0 nm ÷ (3 ÷ 60) h = 1.0 ÷ 0.05 = **20 knots**.
3. Remaining range at 1006 is 2.0 nm, so time left = 2.0 ÷ 20 = 0.1 h = **6 minutes**.
4. If nothing changes, the vessels meet at about 1012.

The numbers are invented. The point is that a steady bearing with closing range leaves little time, and that is why the
report matters.

## What the lights tell you about the other vessel

At night the colour of a vessel's sidelight tells you which side of her you can see (see the night recognition lesson).

| You see | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ==Red sidelight== | Her ==port side== is towards you |
| ==Green sidelight== | Her ==starboard side== is towards you |
| Red and green together | She is heading ==towards you== (you see both sidelights) |
| ==Sternlight only==, no sidelights | You see her from astern; she is heading ==away== from you |

Say what you see: "Green light, three points on the port bow." You do not decide who gives way; you report so the
officer can.

## On the bridge: a night example

Midnight, Georgia Strait, a 25 m vessel. You are on look-out.

1. You see a green light on the port bow, about 3 points. You say: "Green light, three points on the port bow."
2. The officer replies "Green light, port bow." The loop is closed.
3. Three minutes later you check again: still three points, and the light is brighter. You say: "Bearing steady, closing."
4. The officer takes a compass bearing, confirms the risk and alters course in good time.

## Exam traps

- **Waiting to be sure.** Report at once and keep watching.
- **No acknowledgement means no report.** Repeat it.
- **Relative or compass:** the Rule 7 test is the compass bearing. A relative bearing stays steady only if your own course
  does.
- **Changing bearing is not always safe**: large vessels, tows and close range.
- **Red light = her port side**, not "red means stop". Do not mix this up with a buoy's colour.

## Check yourself

1. Write a report for a log in the water, one point on the starboard bow.
2. What should you do if the officer does not reply?
3. A vessel's compass bearing stays the same while the range falls. What does Rule 7 say?
4. Name three situations where bearings may mislead you.
5. You see a red sidelight and a masthead light on a vessel to starboard. Which side of her do you see?
