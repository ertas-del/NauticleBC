# Steering a steady course

**Study aid only.** This lesson teaches steering habits. It is not a substitute for watch time at the wheel under
supervision. Your training record will count your hours.

## What you need to know

Steering is the skill of keeping the ship on a chosen heading with the **least rudder movement** that does the job.
Beginners over-steer: they chase every swing and the ship wanders. Experienced helmsmen make small movements early and
let the ship settle.

Three ideas carry most of the lesson:

1. The ship ==turns under the compass card==.
2. The ship answers the rudder ==with a delay==.
3. The cure for the delay is to ==anticipate the swing== with small, early rudder and to check the swing before it
   overshoots.

## The ship turns under the card

A magnetic compass card tries to stay still, pointing at magnetic north. When the ship's head swings to starboard, the
lubber line (the fixed mark on the bowl that shows the ship's head) moves clockwise over the card, and the number at
the lubber line increases. A helmsman learns to "see" the ship swing by watching the numbers drift past the lubber
line. A gyro repeater works the same way, but its card is driven electrically.

## The delay

When you turn the wheel, the steering gear moves the rudder, the rudder pushes the stern sideways, and only then does
the head begin to swing. The heavier the ship, the longer the lag. The effect is that a helmsman who sees the head
move off the course and puts on a lot of rudder will find that the ship is still swinging after he has taken the rudder
off. The ship then swings back past the course. NAVEDTRA 14067 (a United States Navy seaman text) warns that new
helmsmen often use too much rudder and forget that the steering gear and the ship need time to answer.

## Making a turn

To alter course:

1. Order or take the rudder angle you need (use a small angle for a small alteration).
2. Watch the rate of turn build up on the **rate-of-turn indicator** if one is fitted.
3. **Before the ship reaches the new heading**, "==meet her==": put on some opposite rudder or take off the rudder
   so the swing stops at the heading you want.
4. When the heading is steady, put the rudder amidships and report "Steady on ..." (see the helm orders lesson).

The right moment to start checking depends on the ship. A small, handy boat may need the check only a few degrees
before the new course. A heavy ship may need it a long way ahead. Learn the individual ship.

## The indicators

- The ==rudder angle indicator== shows the **actual** rudder angle. Compare it with the angle you ordered. If they do
  not agree, or if the indicator does not move, tell the OOW at once.
- The **rate-of-turn indicator** shows how fast the head is swinging, so you can stop the swing before the compass
  shows an error.
- The **compass or gyro repeater** shows the heading.

A good helmsman watches all three, not the compass alone.

## Steering by marks and ranges

When a fixed mark is visible ahead (a headland, a beacon, a distant peak), steering on it shows any drift sideways at
once. A **range** is a pair of marks (a front mark and a higher or farther rear mark) that, when they line up, mark a
safe line. The Canadian Coast Guard's aids to navigation system includes range marks for this purpose. The idea:

- When the two marks are in line, the ship is on the range line.
- When they open (one appears to the left or right of the other), the ship has moved off the line.

Which way to steer: if the **rear mark appears on one side of the front mark, you are on that same side of the
line**, so alter the other way, toward the line. For example, if the rear mark is to the **left** of the front mark,
you are to the **left** of the line, and a small alteration to starboard brings you back. This follows from the
geometry (the nearer mark shifts more as you move sideways), but it is not stated in the sources read for this
lesson, so check it against your instructor's explanation and your own watch on a real range.

## Speed, loading and weather

- **Speed and propeller wash.** The rudder works because water flows over it. A ship moving fast through the water,
  or with the propeller driving water past the rudder, answers faster. When the engine is stopped the ==rudder is less
  effective== (a UK guidance note on interaction in channels says this plainly). At slow speed or when the engine is
  stopped, expect a slower answer.
- **Loaded and in ballast.** Common seamanship (taught in TP 10936 but not found in the sources read here, so confirm
  with your instructor) says that a deep-loaded ship is slower to turn and slower to stop turning, and that a ship
  in ballast stands higher out of the water and is pushed about more by wind.
- **Sea state.** Waves and swell knock the head off. Accept small, regular movements that cancel out, and avoid
  fighting every wave with the wheel.

## A BC example

A small tour boat is steering 270 along the shore of a bay on a calm day. The helmsman sees the head fall off to 274
(to starboard) and puts on 5 degrees of port rudder. The head starts back toward 270. A beginner keeps the rudder on
until the compass reads 270, and the ship swings on to 266. An experienced helmsman takes the rudder off, or puts on a
touch of starboard, when the head reads about 272, and the ship settles on 270. Watch for the first small drift, use
a small angle, and take the rudder off a little before the head is back on the course.

## Exam traps

- **More rudder is not better.** Large movements make the ship wander and create drag.
- **The compass card does not turn with the ship.** The ship turns under the card.
- **A lag is normal.** Do not add rudder when the head has not yet answered; wait for the effect.
- **Do not steer only by compass** when a mark ahead can show drift earlier.
- **An unresponsive wheel is reported at once**, not worked around in silence.
