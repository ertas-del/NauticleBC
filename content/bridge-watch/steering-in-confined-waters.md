# Steering in channels and rivers: squat, bank effect, interaction

**Study aid only.** This lesson explains why a vessel behaves strangely in confined water. Real passage planning for
under-keel clearance, speed and passing arrangements must use current charts, tide and current tables and the
master's orders. The examples here use invented numbers.

## What you need to know

In open, deep water a ship steers cleanly. Squeeze it into shallow or narrow water and three things start to happen:

- ==Squat==: the ship sits lower in the water than it should.
- ==Bank effect== (==bank cushion== and ==bank suction==): the banks push and pull on the ship, making it sheer.
- ==Interaction==: another vessel close by pushes and pulls on you, and you on it.

A helmsman who has never heard of these sees only the symptoms: the wheel feels heavy or light, the head wants to
swing the wrong way, the ship vibrates. Knowing the cause tells you the fix, which is nearly always **slow down early**
and **keep your distance**.

## Squat

As a hull moves, water has to flow past it. In shallow water the gap under and beside the hull is small, so the water
speeds up through it. Faster water means lower pressure (the same Bernoulli effect that lifts an aircraft wing). The
hull is pulled down: it **sinks lower** and may change trim (bow-down or stern-down, depending on the hull shape). This
is **squat**. It is not the ship settling on the bottom; it is a dynamic effect that only exists while the ship is
moving, and it disappears when the ship stops.

What controls it:

- **Speed.** Squat is ==roughly proportional to the square of the speed==. Halve the speed and squat falls to about a
  quarter. Speed is the one factor you can change quickly.
- **Shallow water.** The effect becomes serious when the depth is less than about ==four times the draught==.
- **Close to a bank**, which narrows the flow area further.
- **Fuller hulls** (like a loaded bulk carrier) squat differently from finer ones, but all hulls squat.

What it does: extra draught (risk of touching the bottom even when the chart shows enough water), a change of trim,
vibration, ==poor helm response== and sheering. Navigators allow for squat in the **under-keel clearance** they plan.

### Worked example (invented numbers)

At 12 knots a vessel squats 0.60 m. What are the squats at 8 knots and at 6 knots?

Squat varies with speed squared, so multiply by (new speed / old speed) squared.

- At 8 knots: 0.60 x (8 / 12) x (8 / 12) = 0.60 x 0.444 = **0.27 m**.
- At 6 knots: 0.60 x (6 / 12) x (6 / 12) = 0.60 x 0.25 = **0.15 m**. This is the "half the speed, quarter of the
  squat" case.

If the vessel's draught is 2.1 m and the water over a shoal at low tide is 2.5 m, at 12 knots she needs 2.1 + 0.60 =
2.7 m: **not enough**. At 6 knots she needs 2.1 + 0.15 = 2.25 m: enough, with 0.25 m to spare. This is a teaching
sum; the real figure for any ship comes from the master's information, not from a rule of thumb.

## Bank effect

Run a ship close to a steep bank and the water squeezed between the hull and the bank behaves differently from the
water on the open side. Two things happen, and both have a name:

- ==Bank cushion==: water piles up between the **bow** and the bank and pushes the **bow away** from the bank.
- ==Bank suction==: the water speeding past the **stern** lowers the pressure and **draws the stern toward** the bank.

The combined effect is that the ship's head turns toward the middle of the channel while the stern is pulled in toward
the bank. It is worst when the ship is close to the bank, in shallow water and at speed. A ship that is steered too
close can suddenly sheer across the channel toward the opposite bank. The helmsman's answer is early, small rudder movements,
reduced speed, and keeping a proper distance from the bank. How much rudder a particular ship needs, and in which
direction, comes from your instructor and the ship's standing orders, not from this lesson.

## Interaction between vessels

Every moving ship carries a pressure pattern: high pressure at the bow, low pressure along the sides and aft. When two
ships are close, the patterns overlap and each ship pushes the other.

- **Passing close.** Each ship's pressure pattern acts on the other, which can swing the bows and sterns suddenly.
- **Overtaking.** This is the most dangerous case, because the two ships are side by side for a long time. The smaller
  vessel may ==take a sheer into the path of the larger==, or the bows of both may turn away from each other so the
  sterns swing together.
- **Aggravating factors.** ==Higher speed==, shallow water and small distance make the effect stronger. A deep-draught
  ship answers corrections more slowly.

Precautions: reduce speed in good time, keep the greatest practicable distance, agree overtaking by VHF or sound
signals before starting, keep power available for the rudder (the ==rudder is less effective with the engine
stopped==), and be especially careful when a small vessel meets a large one.

The Collision Regulations decide **who does what** (see the Rule 9 lesson, which also gives the Canadian addition for
a channel with a current). This lesson explains **why the ship behaves badly** when you do it.

## Current and tide in rivers and narrow passages

In a river, a tidal passage or the entrance to a BC inlet, the water itself is moving. A ship heading 090 in a 3-knot
current from the south is not travelling 090 over the ground; she is carried north as well. Rules of thumb:

- **Steer by the marks** (leading lines, ranges, a fixed object ahead) and not only by compass, so the set shows up at
  once (see the steering technique lesson).
- A current changes your speed **over the ground** but not your speed **through the water**. Squat and rudder effect
  depend on the water flowing past the hull, so think in speed through the water.
- Allow for the stream when passing a bend or another vessel.
- The TP 10936 outline names steering in rivers with tide, current and other vessels, but the sources read for this
  lesson give no further detail, so ask your instructor for the local rules of thumb.

## A BC example

A tug with a barge in tow comes up a narrow BC river channel where a small fishing boat is working near the bank. The
mate sees the bank cushion pushing the tug's head out, so he reduces speed from 8 to 5
knots (invented figures). The squat falls to about 40 percent of its earlier figure (5/8 squared = 0.39), the helm becomes lighter and the
tug passes at a safe distance. He did not wait for the head to sheer. He acted when the first small push appeared.

## Exam traps

- **Squat sinks the vessel lower; it is not a grounding.** It exists only while moving.
- **Halving speed cuts squat to a quarter**, not to a half.
- **Bank cushion acts on the bow; bank suction acts on the stern.** Learn which end is pushed and which is pulled.
- **Overtaking is the high-risk case for interaction.**
- **The remedy is nearly always: reduce speed early, increase distance.**
- **A stopped engine makes the rudder less effective**, so do not stop it while passing close unless there is a reason.
