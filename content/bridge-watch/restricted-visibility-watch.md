# Keeping watch in restricted visibility

**Study aid only.** The collision rules are taught in the COLREGs lessons on Rules 6, 19 and 35. This lesson shows how a
watch puts them together on the bridge.

## What you need to know

Fog, heavy rain, snow and smoke are all restricted visibility. Fog is common around Vancouver
harbour and the Fraser River approaches in late autumn and winter. When it arrives, the bridge watch changes in several ways at once.

The STCW watchkeeping principles say the officer of the watch's **first responsibility** is to comply with the Collision
Regulations on ==fog signals==, ==safe speed== and ==engine readiness==. The officer must also:

1. ==inform the master==;
2. ==post a proper look-out==;
3. ==exhibit navigation lights==;
4. ==operate and use the radar==.

==Restricted visibility encountered or expected== is also one of the situations in which the master must be notified
immediately (see the calling-the-master lesson). **Expected** matters: call before the fog arrives.

## Safe speed and engines ready

Rule 19 says every vessel proceeds at a ==safe speed adapted to the restricted visibility==, and a power-driven vessel
has her ==engines ready for immediate manoeuvre==. Rule 6 lists the factors for safe speed.

The consequences on the bridge: the officer slows down, tells the engine room to stand by, and the watch gets ready to
go astern or stop.

## Fog signals

Rule 35 requires sound signals in or near an area of restricted visibility, by day and by night. The ones every rating
should know:

| Vessel | Signal | Interval |
|---|---|---|
| ==Power-driven, making way== | ==One prolonged blast== | at intervals of ==not more than 2 minutes== |
| ==Power-driven, underway but stopped (no way)== | ==Two prolonged blasts==, about 2 seconds apart | at intervals of ==not more than 2 minutes== |
| ==At anchor== | ==Bell rung rapidly for about 5 seconds== | at intervals of ==not more than 1 minute== |

The lesson on Rule 35 has the signals for other vessels (not under command, sailing, fishing, towing and so on).

**Worked example: how many signals in an hour?** A vessel makes way in fog and sounds one prolonged blast every 2
minutes, the longest allowed interval.

1. Signals per hour = 60 ÷ 2 = **30** (at least).
2. A vessel at anchor ringing every 1 minute, the longest allowed, rings 60 ÷ 1 = **60** times per hour (at least).

So the anchored bell sounds at least twice as often as the underway whistle.

## The look-out in fog

Fog is when look-out matters most, and when sight helps least:

- Hearing becomes your main sense. Keep **quiet**, switch off noise you control, and listen. Ask the officer if the
  wheelhouse door or a window may be opened so you can hear.
- ==Report every sound signal at once==, with its **relative bearing** and what it was: "One prolonged blast, two points
  on the starboard bow."
- Guidance from the UK (MGN 137) says additional look-outs should be posted in restricted visibility. It is supporting
  guidance, not Canadian law, but the Rule 5 and STCW principles point the same way.
- Report **any** change in visibility to the officer: it may be clearing, or thickening.
- Listen for engines, voices and any fog signal, including from buoys and shore stations.

**Radar** helps, but only as a part of the lookout. It may miss small vessels, ice and floating objects, especially in clutter
from rain or sea, and it does not hear signals. In fog a look-out and a radar together do better than either alone.

## When you hear a fog signal ahead

Rule 19 says that on hearing a fog signal apparently ==forward of the beam==, or if a close-quarters situation ahead cannot be
avoided, a vessel must ==reduce speed to the minimum at which she can keep her course==, take all way off if necessary, and
navigate with ==extreme caution== until danger of collision is over. The exception: where it has been determined that no risk
of collision exists.

You will see how the officer does it. For you, the job is to keep reporting the signal's bearing, so the officer can tell
whether it is steady, drawing aft or growing louder.

## On the bridge: a worked example

Dense fog in Haro Strait. The master has been called; the vessel is on slow ahead. You are on look-out on the bridge wing.

1. You hear one prolonged blast fine on the port bow. You report: "One prolonged blast, fine on the port bow."
2. The officer acknowledges and reduces speed to the minimum that keeps steerage.
3. A minute later, a second blast from slightly closer: "One prolonged blast, port bow, louder." Your repeated reports
   tell the officer the other vessel is probably approaching.
4. The officer takes all way off and keeps listening. A radar target appears on the same bearing. Both together give a
   clear picture.

## Exam traps

- The officer's **first** responsibility: fog signals, safe speed, engine readiness. The four extras: inform the master,
  post a look-out, show lights, use radar.
- **Prolonged blast intervals**: not more than **2 minutes** underway, **1 minute** at anchor for the bell.
- **Underway but stopped = two prolonged**, not one.
- Radar does not replace a look-out, and a clear screen does not mean clear water.
- Fog signals are for **in or near** an area of restricted visibility, day or night.
