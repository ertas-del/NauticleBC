# Stability awareness for deck crew

**Study aid only. Not a stability calculation tool.** Real loading decisions come from the vessel's own stability documents and the master.

## What you need to know

Stability is a vessel's ability to ==return to upright== after something pushes it over: a wave, the wind, a shifting load, a turn at speed. A stable vessel leans, then comes back. An unstable one keeps going. You do not need the sums to help keep your vessel on the right side of that line. You need to understand the picture, and then form a few habits.

## The picture in plain words

Two forces decide everything. The vessel's whole weight pulls down, and we can treat it as acting at one point, the ==centre of gravity, G==. The water pushes up with a force equal to that weight, and we treat it as acting at the ==centre of buoyancy, B==, which is the centre of the underwater shape. When the vessel sits upright, the two forces are in line and cancel.

Now heel the vessel over. The underwater shape changes, so B slides toward the side that went down, while G stays where it was (unless something shifts). The upward push is now off to the low side of the downward pull, and that pair of forces makes a turning effect that rolls the vessel back upright. That turning effect is the ==righting moment==. The lower G is, and the wider and fuller the hull, the stronger the effect.

If G is too high, the turning effect is weak or even reversed. A vessel with a high G is ==tender==: it rolls slowly and leans a long way for a small push. A vessel with a low G and a lot of stability is ==stiff==: it snaps back quickly with a short, sharp roll. Stiff is safer, though less comfortable.

## What changes G, and what you can do about it

Anything you put high up raises G. Anything you put low down lowers it. Adding weight on deck, on a flying bridge or in the rigging makes the vessel more tender. Ice does the same, and so does water that stays on deck.

| Hazard | What it does | Your habit |
|---|---|---|
| ==Top weight== (deck cargo, gear stacked high, a heavy load on the bridge) | Raises G | Stow heavy things low and near the centreline; ask before adding anything high. |
| ==Free surface== (part-full tank, water or fish sloshing in a hold, water on deck) | Liquid moves to the low side as the vessel leans, which acts as if G had risen | Keep tanks full or empty; clear deck water; tell the officer if a tank is slack. |
| ==Open doors, hatches and ports== at sea | Water floods in and can no longer drain; the vessel can be lost from a modest heel | Keep them shut and dogged; follow the officer's orders about closures. |
| ==Blocked freeing ports and scuppers== | Deck water cannot drain, so it piles up and sloshes | Keep them clear; never wedge or lash covers shut. |
| ==Ice== on masts, rails and the superstructure | Heavy, high, adds windage and sinks the vessel lower | Report it at once; clear it from high up first. |
| ==Loose loads and gear== | A load that slides to one side makes a lasting lean | Lash everything; check lashings after weather. |
| ==Passengers or crew crowding to one side== | Moves the weight sideways | Do not crowd one rail; follow the master on where people may stand. |
| ==Lifting over the side== | A suspended weight acts as if it hung from the boom head, high above deck | Keep lifts low and close in; never lift without the master's say. |

## Warning signs

A tender, unstable vessel gives warnings, and the deck crew are often first to notice them. The most important is a ==slow, lazy roll==. The vessel goes over, hangs, then comes back slowly, and does not feel as lively as it did yesterday. A second sign is a ==lasting lean== (a list) that does not go away, which may be a shifted load, a slack tank or water trapped somewhere. A third is ==water staying on deck== instead of draining. Any of these means: tell the officer straight away. Do not wait to see if it improves.

## Duties you should know

Under the Canada Shipping Act, 2001, every crew member must carry out their duties in a way that does not endanger the vessel and must ==report to the master any safety hazards== they become aware of (s. 113). A slack tank, a blocked freeing port or a loose load is exactly that. The master of a Canadian vessel who meets ==severe ice accretion== (air temperature below freezing with gale-force winds causing heavy ice on the superstructure) must tell vessels nearby and the prescribed shore authorities (s. 112). The master must also make sure the applicable ==load lines are not submerged== (s. 110(2)), which is about not loading the vessel deeper than allowed.

## A short BC picture

A small workboat leaves a Fraser River marina with a deck cargo stacked on top of the cabin roof, heads out into the Strait of Georgia in a short steep chop, and finds the roll has become slow and heavy. A deckhand who notices the change and speaks up gives the master the chance to slow down, change heading and re-stow the load low before things get serious. A deckhand who says nothing leaves the master without the information they need.

## Exam traps

A "stiff" vessel is not a worse vessel. It is the tender one that worries you. High G reduces stability, so the answer to "where should the heavy item go" is low and central. Water on deck is not just weight; it also moves, and that makes it worse than the same weight bolted down. And closing a door is not enough if the freeing ports are blocked: closing keeps water out, freeing ports let it out.
