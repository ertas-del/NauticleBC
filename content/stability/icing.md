# Icing

**Study aid only. Invented numbers.** Real icing limits belong in a vessel's stability documents and the master's judgement.

## What you need to know

Ice is weight. It is also weight in the worst possible place: on masts, rails, rigging, the cabin top and the upper part of the hull, well above G. In freezing conditions, spray freezes where it lands and can build up on a vessel hour after hour. The effect on stability is like loading a heavy, top-heavy cargo on deck that you cannot throw overboard in the dark.

## The four harms

1. **It raises G.** Weight added high pulls the whole vessel's centre of gravity up, cutting GM.
2. **It adds weight**, so the vessel sinks. The freeboard falls and so does the angle at which the deck edge goes under.
3. **It adds windage.** Ice thickens every rail, stay and mast, so the wind pushes harder and the heeling force goes up.
4. **It can block things.** Freeing ports, scuppers, hatches, vents and deck gear can freeze solid, so water cannot drain and doors cannot be closed.

All four act at once, and they get worse together: a more heeled vessel, in a stronger wind, taking more spray, building more ice.

## Worked example: the Tern ices up

The Tern (invented): 40 t, KG 1.95 m, KM 2.40 m, GM 0.45 m, freeboard 0.35 m, beam 3.5 m, TPC 0.4305 t/cm.

Overnight in freezing spray the Tern collects **6 t of ice**, with its centre on average 3.20 m above the keel.

1. New displacement = 40 + 6 = **46 t**.
2. Moments: 78.0 + 6 x 3.20 = 78.0 + 19.2 = 97.2 t.m.
3. New KG = 97.2 / 46 = **2.113 m**. (Shift formula check: 6 x (3.20 - 1.95) / 46 = 0.163; 1.95 + 0.163 = 2.113.)
4. Suppose the hydrostatic table gives KM 2.47 m at 46 t. New GM = 2.47 - 2.113 = **0.357 m**, a loss of about a fifth.
5. Sinkage = 6 / 0.4305 = **13.9 cm**, so the freeboard falls from 0.35 m to about **0.21 m**.
6. The deck edge now goes under at atan(0.21 / 1.75) = **6.9 degrees**, down from 11.3 degrees.

Look at the result: GM down a fifth, freeboard down 40 percent. Neither looks dramatic by itself, but the freeboard is what's lost fastest. The ice has also thickened every rail and stay, so the wind now pushes harder on a vessel that is heeling more easily. A GM of 0.357 m at 6.9 degrees to the deck edge is a vessel in trouble.

### Taking ice off: from the top down

The crew clears 2 t of ice from a height of 3.50 m. New displacement = 44 t.

1. Moments: 97.2 - 2 x 3.50 = 90.2 t.m.
2. New KG = 90.2 / 44 = **2.050 m**. (Shift formula: 2 x (3.50 - 2.113) / 44 = 0.063, so G falls by 0.063 m, from 2.113 to 2.050.)
3. With KM 2.45 m at 44 t, GM = **0.40 m**.

Removing 2 t of the **highest** ice has brought back more than 0.04 m of GM. Had the 2 t been taken from low on the hull, at 1.0 m, G would have gone **up**, not down, because removing a weight below G raises G. So ==remove ice from the highest parts first==: mast, rigging, rails, then the cabin top, then work downward.

## The duty to report

The master of a Canadian vessel who meets ==subfreezing air temperatures with gale-force winds causing severe ice accretion on the superstructure== must give notice to vessels in the vicinity and to the prescribed shore authorities (Canada Shipping Act, 2001, s. 112). The same section covers other dangers such as dangerous ice, a tropical storm, a derelict, and force 10 wind with no storm warning received. In practice that means a radio call; check your vessel's procedures for who the prescribed shore authorities are.

## The fishing-vessel comparison

The Master, Limited certificate is not a fishing certificate, but the Fishing Vessel Safety Regulations show how the law treats icing. Section 3.05 says a fishing vessel must not operate in a ==freezing spray warning area== unless its stability assessment shows that it can do so safely, and it must carry a means to remove accumulated ice. If you run a commercial boat in BC waters in winter, the lesson is the same: check the forecast for freezing spray, treat it as a stability hazard, and carry the tools for removing ice (mallets, scrapers, safe access and warm gear).

## What to do

- ==Avoid it==: read the marine forecast for freezing spray. Change heading, slow down, run in the lee of the land, or stay in port.
- If you cannot avoid it: reduce the spray you take (slow down, change heading, find shelter), and begin removal **early** while the ice is thin.
- ==Remove from the top down.== Use mallets and scrapers, and never put crew at risk aloft in a seaway.
- Keep freeing ports, scuppers and hatch openings clear.
- Tell the master the moment you see ice build. Tell the master if the roll slows or the lean changes.

## Exam traps

Ice does not just add weight; it adds windage and removes freeboard, and it raises G. The right order is **high first**. The reporting duty in s. 112 is for severe ice accretion with gale-force winds in subfreezing air, and it is the master's duty. The freezing-spray rule in s. 3.05 is a fishing vessel regulation. And removing a weight from below G makes stability worse, not better.
