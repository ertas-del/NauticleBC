# Rule 14: Head-on situation

## What it is

When ==two power-driven vessels== are meeting on ==reciprocal or nearly reciprocal courses== so as to involve risk
of collision, ==each shall alter her course to starboard== so that each passes on the ==port side== of the other.
Port to port.

Unlike the crossing and overtaking rules, Rule 14 gives **both** vessels the same duty. There is no stand-on
vessel. Each turns to starboard, and the two turns add together.

{{figure:head-on-situation}}

## How to recognise it

You are in a head-on situation when you see the other vessel ==ahead or nearly ahead== and:

- **by night**, you could see her ==masthead lights in a line or nearly in a line== and/or ==both sidelights==;
- **by day**, you see the matching aspect: her bow-on, with masts lined up.

A vessel under 50 metres may show only one masthead light, so you will see one white light above red and green.
A vessel 50 metres or more shows two masthead lights, the after one higher; head-on they appear one above the
other.

## Doubt

If you are in ==any doubt== whether a head-on situation exists, ==assume that it does== and act accordingly. The
common case is a vessel fine on your bow, where you see first a red, then both, then a green as she yaws. Do not
try to decide if it is a crossing with you as stand-on. Treat it as head-on and alter to starboard, early and
clearly.

## Who Rule 14 does not cover

- Two **sailing vessels** meeting head-on: use Rule 12 (tack and windward).
- A **power-driven vessel meeting a sailing vessel** (or a fishing vessel, or a vessel not under command): use Rule 18.
  The power-driven vessel keeps out of the way.

## Canadian twist: narrow channels with a current

In the ==Canadian waters of a narrow channel or fairway== with a ==current or tidal stream==, the Canadian Rule 9
overrides Rule 14(a) for two power-driven vessels meeting from opposite directions. The vessel going ==with the
stream is the stand-on vessel==, proposes where to pass and signals it. The vessel stemming the stream keeps out of
the way and holds back if needed. See the Rule 9 lesson for the signals.

## A BC bridge example

Night, open water south of Nanaimo. Fine on your bow you see a white light over a red and a green, with the white
light nearly in line with a second, higher white light behind it. Both vessels are power-driven, and the range is
closing.

- Masthead lights in line and both sidelights: head-on. Each of you alters to starboard.
- Make the turn early and big enough to show her your red sidelight clearly, then give the sound signal for an
  alteration to starboard (Rule 34).
- Do not turn to port to "get out of her way faster". If she turns to starboard as the rule says and you turn to
  port, you turn into each other.

## Common mistakes

- Waiting for the other vessel to act first. Both have the duty.
- Turning to port because the other vessel looks slightly to port. If it is head-on or in doubt, turn to starboard.
- Applying Rule 14 to a sailboat meeting a power-driven vessel.
- Small alterations that keep both sidelights in view on the other bridge. Show her your port side clearly.
- Forgetting the Canadian narrow-channel rule when meeting another power-driven vessel in a tidal channel.
