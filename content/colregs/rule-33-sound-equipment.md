# Rule 33: Equipment for sound signals

Rule 33 tells you what sound signalling gear a vessel must carry. The answer depends on length. Annex III then gives
the technical specification for that gear.

## What the rule requires

| Length | Must carry |
|---|---|
| ==12 m or more== | a ==whistle== |
| ==20 m or more== | a whistle ==and a bell== |
| ==100 m or more== | a whistle, a bell ==and a gong== |
| ==under 12 m== | not obliged to carry the above, but must have ==some other means of making an efficient sound signal== |

Some details:

- The gong's tone and sound must be such that it ==cannot be confused with the bell==. Large vessels use the bell
  forward and the gong aft at anchor in fog (Rule 35), so the two must sound different.
- The bell or gong, or both, ==may be replaced by other equipment== with the same sound characteristics, for example
  an electronic system. But ==manual sounding of the signals must always be possible==, so the gear still works if
  the power or the automatic system fails.
- The whistle, bell and gong must meet Annex III.

## Canadian addition: small pushing and pulling vessels

The Canadian text adds a paragraph. In the
==Canadian waters of a roadstead, harbour, river, lake or inland waterway==, a vessel that is:

1. under 12 metres in length,
2. ==built or converted for the purpose of pushing or pulling== any floating object, and
3. not solely employed in yarding or warping operations,

must carry the sound signal gear required for a vessel of 12 metres or more. In other words, a small working tug or
boom boat cannot rely on the "under 12 m, some other means" exemption. Rule 35 has a matching Canadian paragraph for
the fog signals these vessels must sound.

## Annex III basics

You do not need to memorise the whole annex, but these points are fair game:

- **Pitch follows size.** The bigger the vessel, the ==lower the whistle's fundamental frequency==. The bands are
  70-200 Hz for vessels of 200 m or more, 130-350 Hz for 75 m to under 200 m, and ==250-700 Hz for under 75 m==. A
  deep, low note in fog suggests a large ship.
- **Audibility range** (given for information) by length:

  | Length | Audibility range |
  |---|---|
  | 200 m or more | 2 nautical miles |
  | 75 m to under 200 m | 1.5 nautical miles |
  | 20 m to under 75 m | 1 nautical mile |
  | under 20 m | ==0.5 nautical mile== |

  These ranges are typical, not guaranteed. Fog, wind, background noise and your own engine can cut them sharply.
  The Canadian amendment of 2014 states that the audibility-range column is not part of the Regulations, so treat the
  figures as guidance.
- **Bell size.** For a vessel of 20 m or more the bell's mouth diameter must be ==at least 300 mm==, and the striker
  must weigh at least 3 per cent of the bell's mass.

## A bridge-watch example

A 10 metre aluminium boat on the Fraser River is fitted and used to push log booms between booming grounds. Under the
international text alone, a 10 metre boat could get by with a portable air horn. Under the Canadian paragraph, if it
was built or converted to push or pull floating objects and is not solely yarding or warping, it must carry the sound
gear of a 12 metre vessel. Check what is actually fitted before you take the wheel.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking the bell starts at 12 m. Under the current rule it starts at ==20 m==.
- Assuming "under 12 m" means no sound gear at all. It still needs some other efficient means of sound signalling.
- Forgetting the Canadian small pushing/pulling vessel paragraph.
- Treating the Annex III audibility ranges as a promise. In real fog you may hear a small craft much closer than
  half a mile, or not at all.
