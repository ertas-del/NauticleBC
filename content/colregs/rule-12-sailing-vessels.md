# Rule 12: Sailing vessels

## What it is

Rule 12 only covers one pairing: ==two sailing vessels== approaching one another so as to involve risk of
collision. It does not cover a sailing vessel meeting a power-driven vessel or a fishing vessel. Those are dealt
with by Rule 18 (and by Rule 13 if one is overtaking).

## The three tests

1. **Different tacks.** When each has the wind on a different side, the vessel with the ==wind on the port side==
   keeps out of the way of the other. (Port tack gives way to starboard tack.)
2. **Same tack.** When both have the wind on the same side, the vessel ==to windward== keeps out of the way of the
   vessel to leeward.
3. **Can't tell.** If a vessel with the wind on the port side sees a vessel to windward and ==cannot determine with
   certainty== which side that vessel has the wind on, she keeps out of the way.

## Which side is the wind on?

Rule 12 settles this with a definition rather than with the wind vane. The windward side is the side
==opposite to that on which the mainsail is carried==. For a square-rigged vessel, use the largest fore-and-aft
sail instead.

So a boat with her main boom out to starboard is treated as having the wind on her port side: she is on port
tack. This matters running downwind, when the true wind can be nearly dead astern. Look at the main, not the
flag.

## Plain explanation

Test 3 exists because test 1 needs you to know the other boat's tack. A boat to windward can be hard to read
when her sails are seen edge-on or are hidden behind her own. The port-tack boat is the one that might have to
give way anyway, so the rule puts the doubt on her: if unsure, keep clear.

Rule 12 sits under Rule 13. A sailing vessel coming up from more than 22.5 degrees abaft another vessel's beam
is ==overtaking== and keeps out of the way, even if she is on starboard tack. And a sailboat with her engine
running and propelling her is a power-driven vessel by definition, even with sails up. Rule 12 does not apply
to her.

## A BC bridge example

Two cruising sailboats are beating up Haro Strait in a northwesterly. Boat A has her main to port (wind on her
starboard side, so starboard tack). Boat B has her main to starboard (port tack). They are converging with a
steady bearing.

- Different tacks: B, on port tack, keeps out of the way. She can tack, bear away to pass astern of A, or slow.
- A is the stand-on vessel and Rule 17 applies to her: hold course and speed, but be ready to act if B does not.

Later the same day both boats are on starboard tack, one a few lengths to windward of the other, and converging.
The boat to windward keeps out of the way of the boat to leeward.

## Common mistakes

- Deciding tack from the wind vane rather than the side the mainsail is on.
- Applying Rule 12 to a sailboat that is motorsailing. Under power, she is a power-driven vessel.
- Thinking starboard tack beats the overtaking rule. Rule 13 comes first.
- Using Rule 12 between a sailboat and a fishing vessel or a ferry. That is Rule 18's job.
- Forgetting the "can't tell" test when the windward boat's sails are hard to read.

## Check yourself

1. A boat has her main boom out to starboard. Which side is the wind on, for Rule 12?
2. Both boats are on port tack. Which one keeps out of the way?
3. You are on port tack and cannot tell the tack of a boat to windward. What do you do?
4. A starboard-tack sailboat is coming up from your quarter and gaining. Who keeps clear?
5. Why does Rule 12 not apply to a sailboat motoring with her sails up?
