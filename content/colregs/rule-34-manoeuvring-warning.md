# Rule 34: Manoeuvring and warning signals

Rule 34 is the signal rule for vessels that ==can see each other==. Fog signals are a separate rule (Rule 35). Mixing
the two up is one of the most common exam and oral errors.

## Manoeuvring signals

When vessels are ==in sight of one another==, a ==power-driven vessel underway== that manoeuvres as the Rules allow
or require shows it on her whistle:

| Signal | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ==one short== | "I am ==altering my course to starboard==" |
| ==two short== | "I am ==altering my course to port==" |
| ==three short== | "I am ==operating astern propulsion==" |

Points to note:

- These are **action** signals. You sound them as you make the manoeuvre, not as a proposal.
- They are for a power-driven vessel. A sailing vessel under sail does not use them.
- They are only used when vessels are in sight. In fog, when you cannot see the other vessel, you do not sound one
  short for an alteration to starboard.

**Light signals.** Any vessel may add light signals, repeated as needed while the manoeuvre is carried out: one flash
for starboard, two for port, three for astern. Each flash lasts ==about one second==, with about one second between
flashes and ==at least 10 seconds== between successive signals. The light, if fitted, is an
==all-round white light visible at least 5 miles==.

## Overtaking in a narrow channel or fairway

Where overtaking in a narrow channel needs the vessel ahead to help (Rule 9), the vessels exchange signals while in
sight of one another:

| Signal | Meaning |
|---|---|
| ==two prolonged, one short== | "I intend to overtake you ==on your starboard side==" |
| ==two prolonged, two short== | "I intend to overtake you ==on your port side==" |
| ==one prolonged, one short, one prolonged, one short== | the vessel ahead ==agrees== |

If the vessel ahead does not agree or is unsure, she sounds the doubt signal. Agreeing does not make the overtaking
vessel any less responsible for keeping clear.

## Doubt or danger

When vessels in sight of one another are approaching and either one does not understand what the other is doing,
or doubts that the other is doing enough to avoid collision, the vessel in doubt ==immediately== sounds
==at least five short and rapid blasts==. It may add at least five short and rapid flashes of light.

Five short is a warning, not a manoeuvre. After sounding it you still have to act under the steering rules.

## Bends and obstructions

A vessel nearing a bend, or an area of channel or fairway where other vessels may be hidden by an obstruction,
sounds ==one prolonged blast==. Any approaching vessel around the bend or behind the obstruction that hears it
==answers with one prolonged blast==. This signal is given whether or not the visibility is good, because the
whole point is that you cannot see round the corner.

## More than one whistle

If a vessel has whistles fitted ==more than 100 metres apart==, she uses ==only one whistle== for manoeuvring and
warning signals, so listeners do not hear a confusing double signal.

## Canadian paragraphs

The Canadian text adds paragraphs. Two apply in the
==Canadian waters of a roadstead, harbour, river, lake or inland waterway==, which includes BC harbours and rivers:

- **Leaving a dock or berth.** A ==power-driven vessel leaving a dock or berth== sounds ==one prolonged blast==.
  There is a narrow exception for a ferry on a frequent scheduled run, in good visibility, when the master has
  decided the signal is not needed for a safe departure. Check the exact conditions in the current text.
- **Bridge-to-bridge radiotelephone.** A vessel ==may use bridge-to-bridge radiotelephone== instead of the
  prescribed whistle signals to reach agreement in a meeting, crossing or overtaking situation. If agreement is not
  reached, ==whistle signals are exchanged in good time and prevail==.

Other Canadian paragraphs give different manoeuvring signals (and light signals) for the ==Great Lakes Basin==. Those
do **not** apply on the BC coast. If you read an American or Great Lakes guide that says one short means "I intend to
leave you on my port side", that is a different system.

## A bridge-watch example

You are on a 30 metre vessel inbound in a BC harbour channel, in good visibility. A tug ahead is slow and you want
to pass her. On VHF the tug's master agrees you can pass on her port side. Under the Canadian radiotelephone
paragraph that agreement can stand instead of the whistle exchange. If the radio call had gone unanswered, you would
sound two prolonged, two short and wait for one prolonged, one short, one prolonged, one short before overtaking.
As you come to the blind corner by the next wharf, you sound one prolonged and listen for an answer.

## Common mistakes

- Sounding one short or two short in fog. Manoeuvring signals are for vessels in sight of one another.
- Treating one short as "I intend to" rather than "I am altering". That is the US inland and Great Lakes style.
- Sounding only three or four blasts for doubt. The rule says at least five.
- Confusing the agreement signal with the overtaking signal.
- Forgetting that the bend signal is answered with one prolonged blast.
- Leaving a berth in a Canadian harbour without the one prolonged blast.
