# Rule 8: Action to avoid collision

Rule 8 does not tell you who must give way. Other rules do that. Rule 8 tells you **how to act** once you know you
must act. It applies in any condition of visibility.

## What the rule asks of you

In plain terms:

- **Follow the other rules.** Any avoiding action has to fit the rest of Part B. You do not get to invent a
  manoeuvre that breaks another rule.
- **Be positive, early and seamanlike.** If the situation allows, act decisively and in ample time. Waiting
  until the last minute leaves you with only bad options.
- **Be readily apparent.** A change of course, speed or both should be large enough that the other vessel can
  see it, by eye or on radar. Avoid a string of small alterations.
- **Prefer a course change when you have sea room.** If there is enough room, altering course alone can be the
  most effective way to avoid a close-quarters situation, provided it is made in good time, is substantial, and
  does not set up a new close-quarters situation with someone else.
- **Aim to pass at a safe distance, then check.** Keep watching until the other vessel is finally past and clear.
  Your action is only proven when it has worked.
- **Use your speed.** If you need more time, you may slow down or take all way off by stopping or reversing.
- **If you must not impede another vessel, act early.** A vessel that the rules say must not impede the passage
  of another has to take early action to leave that vessel enough sea room.

## Why it works this way

Collision avoidance is a conversation between two bridges with no radio. The only thing the other vessel can read
is what your vessel does. If your alteration is too small to notice, it tells them nothing. If they alter at the
same moment as you, in the same sense, the two small changes can cancel out. A bold, early, obvious change is
easy to read and easy to confirm.

The rule gives no number of degrees. "Large enough" depends on vessel size, speed and sea room, so judge it
from the other vessel's point of view: could someone watching your compass-bearing change or your aspect on
radar tell that you have acted?

## Vessels that must not impede

Several rules tell one vessel not to impede another (for example in narrow channels, and in traffic
separation schemes). The rule says such a vessel does not escape that duty just because it gets close. If
the two vessels are approaching so that risk of collision exists, the vessel that must not impede still has to
act early, with full regard to what the other rules ask. The vessel whose passage is not to be impeded also
stays bound by the Rules. Neither side may treat this as a licence to stop looking or acting.

## A bridge-watch example

You are the give-way vessel on a quiet evening in open water. The other vessel is on your starboard bow with a
steady bearing. Your options:

1. Turn 5 degrees to starboard, then 5 more, then 5 more as the range closes. A radar operator on the other
   vessel may see nothing for several minutes, and you have spent your sea room.
2. Make one early alteration large enough to open the bearing clearly, check that nothing else is now close,
   hold the new course until the other vessel is clear, then resume your track.

Option 2 is what the rule describes. If there is a shoal close on the side you want to turn towards, a
reduction in speed (or stopping) may be the better tool, and Rule 8 expressly allows it.

## Common mistakes

- Making several small alterations that the other vessel cannot detect.
- Turning at the last moment, when only a collision-avoiding emergency manoeuvre is left.
- Turning without checking whether the new course creates a close-quarters situation with a third vessel.
- Resuming course too early, before the other vessel is finally past and clear.
- Forgetting that slowing down or stopping is a legitimate part of the action.
- Thinking "I have the right of way" means you can stop watching the other vessel.
