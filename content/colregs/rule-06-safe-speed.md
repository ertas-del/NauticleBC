# Rule 6: Safe speed

## What the rule asks of you

Every vessel must proceed at a ==safe speed== ==at all times==. A safe speed lets you do two things:

- take ==proper and effective action== to avoid collision, and
- ==stop within a distance suitable== for the conditions at the time.

The Rules give ==no speed figure==. Instead they list things to take into account.

**Everyone considers:**

- the state of **visibility**,
- **traffic density**, including concentrations of fishing vessels or other vessels,
- **manoeuvrability**, particularly stopping distance and turning ability in the conditions,
- at night, ==background light== such as shore lights or back-scatter from your own lights,
- the state of **wind, sea and current**, and how close **navigational hazards** are,
- your **draught** in relation to the depth of water available.

**Vessels with working radar also consider:**

- the characteristics, efficiency and **limits of the radar**,
- any constraints from the **range scale** in use,
- the effect of **sea state, weather and other interference** on detection,
- the chance that ==small vessels, ice and other floating objects== are not picked up at a useful range,
- the **number, location and movement** of vessels the radar detects,
- the more exact **assessment of visibility** that radar ranging of other vessels may allow.

## The Canadian addition

The Canadian text adds two paragraphs for Canadian roadsteads, harbours, rivers, lakes and inland waterways. When you pass a ==dredge, a tow, a grounded vessel or a wreck==, you must go with caution and at a speed that will ==not adversely affect== them, and you must follow any relevant notices to mariners or navigational warnings. If you cannot tell with certainty whether your passing will have an adverse effect, you must proceed with caution at the ==minimum speed at which you can keep on your course==.

## Plain-language explanation

Safe speed is a judgement that you keep making, not a limit you look up. The same boat can be safe at 15 knots in a clear, empty strait at noon and unsafe at 6 knots in thick fog among fishing boats. Slower speed gives more time to see, more time to decide, and a shorter distance to stop. The key question is whether you could still avoid a collision or stop in time if something appeared.

Radar helps you judge, but it does not replace judgement. Small boats, ice and floating debris can be missed, and sea clutter or rain can hide targets. A radar set on a long range scale may not show close targets clearly. Treat what the radar shows as part of the picture and let its limits slow you down.

The Canadian paragraphs are mainly about **wash and wake**. A fast boat passing a dredge, a tow, or a vessel aground can throw a wake that causes damage or danger. If you are not sure, creep past at the lowest speed that still lets you steer.

## On the bridge

At dusk you are heading down a coastal channel with scattered log debris, a few anchored vessels and drizzle reducing visibility. Your radar shows several echoes, but you know small objects and logs can fade in the rain clutter. A safe speed here is a speed at which you can stop or turn clear if a log or an unlit boat shows up late. That speed is lower than you would use on a bright, clear afternoon. Later you approach a dredge working inside a harbour. You are not sure how your wake will affect her, so you slow to the minimum speed at which you can still hold your course while you pass, and check for any notices that apply.

## Common mistakes

- Treating the posted limit or "normal cruising speed" as the safe speed.
- Reasoning that because radar shows nothing ahead, it is safe to run fast in fog.
- Forgetting that stopping distance grows with speed, load and current.
- Overlooking the vessel's draught when running in shallow or narrow water.
- Passing a dredge, tow or wreck at normal speed without thinking about wake.
