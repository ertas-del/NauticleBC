# Rule 15: Crossing situation

## What it is

When ==two power-driven vessels== are ==crossing== so as to involve risk of collision, the vessel which has the
other ==on her own starboard side== ==keeps out of the way==. She shall also, if the circumstances of the case
admit, ==avoid crossing ahead== of the other vessel.

The other vessel, the one that sees the give-way vessel on her **port** side, is the stand-on vessel and follows
Rule 17.

## Plain explanation

**Why starboard?** Each vessel's natural avoiding turn is to starboard. If the vessel on your starboard bow is
yours to avoid, a starboard turn takes you behind her. If both vessels did that in every situation, the turns would
agree rather than conflict.

**Why avoid crossing ahead?** Crossing ahead puts your vessel in the stand-on vessel's path, at the point where any
misjudgement of speed is most dangerous. The usual give-way actions are:

- a ==substantial alteration to starboard== to pass astern of the other vessel;
- ==slowing down or stopping== to let her pass ahead;
- a combination of both, made early (Rule 16).

"If the circumstances of the case admit" covers the cases where passing astern is truly not possible, for example
because of a shoal or a third vessel. Then you choose the safest action, but crossing ahead is the exception, not the
plan.

**At night.** If you see the other vessel's ==red sidelight== on your starboard side, she has you on her port side:
you are the give-way vessel. If you see her green sidelight on your port side, she has you on her starboard side:
you are normally the stand-on vessel. Old memory aid: "if to starboard red appear, 'tis your duty to keep clear."

## Canadian modification: crossing a river

The Canadian Regulations add **Rule 15(b)**. Notwithstanding paragraph (a), in ==Canadian waters==, a vessel
==crossing a river== keeps out of the way of a ==power-driven vessel ascending or descending the river==. This
does not apply on the ==St. Lawrence River seaward of Ile Rouge==.

So on a Canadian river the "starboard side" test is replaced: traffic going up or down the river has priority over a
vessel crossing it, whichever side it comes from. On the BC coast this is most likely to matter on a navigable river
such as the lower Fraser. Note the wording: the vessel crossing is "a vessel", and the protected vessel is a
"power-driven vessel".

## A BC bridge example

You are in a power-driven water taxi heading north across the approaches to a busy harbour. A tug with no tow is
heading west, on your starboard bow at 2 miles, with a steady bearing. You can see her red sidelight at night.

- She is on your starboard side: you are the give-way vessel.
- Act early. Alter boldly to starboard so that you pass well astern of her, or slow down so she crosses ahead.
- Do not speed up to cross her bow. That is what the rule tells you to avoid.

Now move the same encounter to the lower Fraser River. You are crossing from one bank to the other, and a
power-driven vessel is coming down the river on your port side. Under Rule 15(b) you keep out of her way, even
though she is on your port side.

## Common mistakes

- Applying Rule 15 to a sailing vessel or a fishing vessel. Rule 15 is power-driven to power-driven only.
- Crossing ahead because "there is just enough room".
- Small alterations that leave the bearing nearly steady.
- Turning to port to "go around" a vessel on your starboard bow. You end up crossing ahead.
- Forgetting the Canadian river rule and claiming stand-on status against a vessel coming down a river.
