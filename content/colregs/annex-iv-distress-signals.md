# Annex IV: Distress signals

Rule 37 sends you here. Annex IV lists the signals that mean ==distress and need of assistance==. Signals may be used
on their own or together. The Canadian text of Annex IV was rewritten in 2014 to make clear which signals may be
used in Canadian waters and to add modern radio alerts.

## The list, grouped

**Sound**

- a gun or other explosive signal fired at ==intervals of about a minute==
- a ==continuous sounding== with any fog-signalling apparatus (not a pattern, just continuous)

**Pyrotechnics and smoke**

- rockets or shells throwing ==red stars==, fired one at a time at short intervals
- a ==rocket parachute flare or a hand flare showing a red light==
- a smoke signal giving off ==orange smoke==

**Morse and voice**

- ==SOS== (dot-dot-dot, dash-dash-dash, dot-dot-dot) by any signalling method: light, sound or radio
- the spoken word ==MAYDAY== by radiotelephone

**Flags, shapes and the body**

- the International Code signal of distress, ==N over C== (the flags N and C)
- a ==square flag with a ball==, or anything like a ball, above or below it
- ==flames on the vessel==, such as a burning tar or oil barrel
- ==slowly and repeatedly raising and lowering arms== outstretched to each side

**Radio and electronic**

- a ==DSC distress alert== on ==VHF channel 70==, or on the MF/HF DSC distress frequencies
- a ship-to-shore distress alert through ==Inmarsat or another mobile satellite service==
- signals from an ==EPIRB== (emergency position-indicating radio beacon)
- approved signals from radiocommunication systems, including ==survival craft radar transponders==

## Helping searchers find you

Annex IV also draws attention to the International Code of Signals and the search-and-rescue manual, and to two aids
for being seen ==from the air==:

- a piece of ==orange canvas with a black square and circle==, or another suitable symbol
- a ==dye marker== in the water

## Canadian addition

A secondary source reports that the Canadian Annex IV adds a signal that may be used in Canadian waters:
a ==high-intensity white light flashing at regular intervals of 50 to 70 times a minute==, for example a strobe.
This has not yet been checked against the current regulation text, so treat it as unconfirmed. It is one more reason
not to use a strobe just to attract attention (Rule 36 says to avoid that anyway).

## Only for distress

Annex IV ==prohibits using these signals except to indicate distress== and need of assistance, and prohibits any
signal that might be confused with them.

## Look-alikes to watch for

| You see or hear | Distress? | Why |
|---|---|---|
| continuous fog horn, no pattern | yes | continuous sounding of fog apparatus |
| one prolonged every 2 minutes in fog | no | power-driven vessel making way (Rule 35) |
| white flare | no | not in the Annex IV list |
| red parachute flare | yes | red pyrotechnic |
| orange smoke | yes | listed smoke colour |
| five short and rapid blasts | no | doubt signal (Rule 34) |
| N over C flags | yes | International Code distress signal |
| ball over a square flag | yes | flag and ball signal |

## A bridge-watch example

On an evening crossing of the Strait of Georgia you see a red star rise, burst and fall, then a minute later another.
Two red stars at short intervals is a distress signal. Note the bearing and time at once, keep the spot in sight,
plot your position, and tell the master. The master will report it and decide on going to assist, which is outside
the scope of this lesson.

## Common mistakes

- Thinking a white flare is a distress signal. Red stars, red flares and orange smoke are.
- Confusing the continuous fog signal (distress) with a normal fog signal pattern.
- Forgetting the radio and electronic signals: DSC, satellite alerts, EPIRBs and radar transponders.
- Mixing up N over C (distress) with other code flags.
- Firing expired flares for practice in public view.
