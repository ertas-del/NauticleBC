# Rule 20: Lights and shapes, when they apply

Part C of the Rules (Rules 20 to 31) tells every vessel how to **say what she is and what she is doing**, with lights at night and shapes by day. Rule 20 sets the ground rules for all of it.

## What the rule asks of you

- **All weathers.** The lights and shapes Rules apply ==in all weathers==. Fog, rain or a flat calm do not switch them off.
- **Lights: sunset to sunrise.** Lights must be shown ==from sunset to sunrise==. During that time you may not show any other light that ==could be mistaken for a prescribed light==, that ==impairs their visibility or distinctive character==, or that ==interferes with keeping a proper lookout==.
- **Lights in daytime too.** Lights that are carried must also be shown from sunrise to sunset ==in restricted visibility==, and may be shown at any other time it is considered necessary.
- **Shapes: by day.** The Rules about shapes apply ==by day==.
- **Annex I.** Lights and shapes must meet the positioning and technical details in ==Annex I== (heights, spacing, sizes, colours, intensity).

## Plain-language explanation

Think of lights and shapes as a language. At night the other bridge cannot see your hull, only your lights, so the lights must be right and must not be drowned out. Bright deck floodlights, a glowing wheelhouse or a decorative string of coloured lights can hide or imitate your navigation lights. That is why Rule 20 bans other lights that confuse, dazzle or spoil the lookout.

In fog in daytime you switch your navigation lights on: an approaching vessel may see them before she makes out your hull. Day shapes (black balls, cones, diamonds and cylinders) are the daytime version of the special lights. A shape only means something if it is the right one and is shown where it can best be seen.

## Canadian additions to know

The Canadian Collision Regulations add some lighting rules outside the international text:

- **Blue flashing light (Rule 45).** A ==government vessel== or a ==police vessel== may show a ==blue flashing light== as an identification signal when giving help to someone in grave and imminent danger, or when doing law enforcement in Canadian waters. A **Canadian Coast Guard Auxiliary** vessel may show it when taking part in search and rescue at the Coast Guard's request. Showing it ==does not relieve the vessel from the steering and sailing rules==.
- **Dangerous goods (Rule 39).** In Canadian waters of a roadstead, harbour, river, lake or inland waterway, a vessel taking in, discharging or carrying dangerous goods shows an ==all-round red light or flag "B"== when not underway, and ==flag "B" but not the red light== when underway.
- **Backup lights (Rule 46).** Most Canadian vessels that must show lights need an ==alternate system of navigation lights==: a backup set of masthead lights, sidelights, sternlight and anchor lights. It does not apply to vessels under 15 m, cable ferries or pleasure craft.
- **Proof of compliance (sections 5 and 6).** Lights and shapes on vessels other than pleasure craft need a proof of compliance, either a document on board or a label on the fitting, showing they meet the standards.

## A BC bridge example

You are on watch in a 20 m crew boat leaving Nanaimo at dawn in thick fog. The sun is up, but visibility is restricted, so your navigation lights stay on. Later the fog lifts and you pass a fuel barge alongside a dock showing an all-round red light: in Canadian harbour waters that signals dangerous goods. Back alongside in the evening, the deck crew switch on bright floodlights to work cargo. Before you get underway again, check that the floodlights do not drown out, or look like, your navigation lights.

## Common mistakes

- Switching navigation lights off at sunrise on a foggy morning.
- Thinking shapes are optional "because everyone has radar".
- Working floodlights that hide the sidelights or dazzle the lookout.
- Treating a blue flashing light as a "right of way" light. It is an identification signal only.
- Confusing the Canadian dangerous goods signal (one all-round red, not underway) with a vessel not under command (two all-round reds in a vertical line).
