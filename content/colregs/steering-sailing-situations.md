# Putting Rules 11 to 19 together: encounter drills

This lesson does not add new rules. It runs the rules you have learned through the kind of situations an examiner
will describe at the oral. Work each drill before reading the answer.

## The decision order

Use the same order every time:

1. **Can I see her?** If yes: Section II (==Rules 11 to 18==). If not, and you are in or near restricted
   visibility: ==Rule 19==, and there is no stand-on vessel.
2. **Is anyone overtaking?** Rule 13 comes before Rules 12, 15 and 18.
3. **What kind of vessels?** Two power-driven: Rule 14 or 15. Two sailing: Rule 12. Different kinds: Rule 18.
4. **Any Canadian twist?** Narrow channel with a current (Rule 9), crossing a river (Rule 15(b)).
5. **Act like the rule says.** Give-way: Rule 16 and Rule 8. Stand-on: Rule 17.

## Drill 1: Overtaking or crossing? The 22.5 degree line

Work out the **relative bearing**: the other vessel's true bearing from you, minus your true heading. Add 360 if
the answer is negative.

- A relative bearing between ==112.5 and 247.5 degrees== is more than ==22.5 degrees abaft your beam==, on one
  side or the other. A vessel there that is gaining on you is ==overtaking==. She sees only your
  sternlight.
- Forward of that line she sees one of your sidelights. If she is a power-driven vessel converging with you, it is a
  crossing or head-on situation.

**Case A.** You steer 300 degrees true. A power-driven vessel bears 060 degrees true from you and is gaining.
Relative bearing = 060 - 300 = -240, + 360 = **120 degrees**. That is 30 degrees abaft your starboard beam,
beyond the 22.5 degree line. She is **overtaking**, and she keeps clear. You keep course and speed (Rule 17
applies to you as the vessel being overtaken).

**Case B.** You steer 090 degrees true. A faster power-driven vessel bears 190 degrees true and is converging.
Relative bearing = 190 - 090 = **100 degrees**, only 10 degrees abaft your starboard beam. She can see your green
sidelight, so she is **not** overtaking. It is a ==crossing== situation, and she is on your starboard side, so
==you are the give-way vessel==. Many people get this one wrong because she is "behind" the beam.

**Case C.** At night you are coming up on a vessel and her light flicks between green and white. You are on the
line. In doubt, ==assume you are overtaking== (Rule 13(c)) and keep clear.

## Drill 2: Head-on at night

You are a power-driven vessel. Dead ahead, closing, you see a white light above a red and a green, with a second
white light higher and behind it, the two whites nearly in line.

- Masthead lights in line plus both sidelights: ==head-on==.
- Both vessels ==alter to starboard== and pass ==port to port==. Neither stands on.
- Make your turn early and obvious, so that she loses your green and sees only your red. Sound one short blast
  (Rule 34) as you alter.
- If you see only red and white fine on your starboard bow with a steady bearing, it is not head-on: it is a
  crossing and you are give-way. If the picture keeps changing, treat it as head-on.

## Drill 3: The give-way and stand-on sequence

Two power-driven vessels in clear weather, crossing with a steady bearing. Vessel G has the other on her starboard
side (give-way). Vessel S has G on her port side (stand-on). The distances below are only an example; the right
moments depend on speed, size and sea room.

| Range | Vessel G (give-way) | Vessel S (stand-on) |
|---|---|---|
| 6 miles | Sees risk developing. Plans her action. | Keeps course and speed. Watches G's bearing and aspect. |
| 4 miles | Takes ==early and substantial== action: a bold turn to starboard to pass astern, or a large speed reduction. | Keeps course and speed. Checks the bearing is now opening. |
| 2 miles (if G has done nothing) | (Should have acted already.) | In doubt: sounds ==at least five short and rapid blasts==. ==May== act alone: a turn to starboard or slowing. ==Not a turn to port== for a vessel on her port side. |
| Very close | Still bound to keep out of the way. | ==Must== take the action that best aids to avoid collision. |
| After | Holds new course until ==finally past and clear==, then resumes. | Resumes passage once G is past and clear. |

## Drill 4: Radar-only contacts in fog

Thick fog, nothing in sight, you are a power-driven vessel at a safe speed with engines ready. Each contact is
closing and you have decided to alter course. Rule 19(d) says to avoid, so far as possible, a turn ==to port for a
vessel forward of the beam== (unless you are overtaking her) and a turn ==towards a vessel abeam or abaft the
beam==.

| Contact relative bearing | Where | Avoid | Sensible alteration |
|---|---|---|---|
| 005 | Ahead, slightly starboard | Port | Starboard (bold) |
| 030 | Starboard bow | Port | Starboard |
| 300 | Port bow | Port | Starboard |
| 090 | Starboard beam | Starboard (towards) | Port, or adjust speed |
| 135 | Starboard quarter | Starboard (towards) | Port, or adjust speed |
| 225 | Port quarter | Port (towards) | Starboard |
| 355, a slower vessel you are overtaking | Ahead | (No port-turn restriction) | Either side, with ample room |

Two notes. First, in every case **slowing down** is also allowed, and with a contact forward of the beam that you
cannot avoid getting close to, Rule 19(e) **requires** you to slow to bare steerage way. Second, if two contacts call
for different turns (one at 030 and one at 135, say), speed reduction may be the only action that suits both.

## A BC bridge example: all four at once

You are on watch on a 22 metre crew boat crossing the Strait of Georgia at dusk. A bank of fog lies to the south.

1. A tug is overtaking you from 140 degrees relative: she keeps clear (Drill 1).
2. A ferry is head-on, masthead lights in line: you both alter to starboard (Drill 2).
3. A fishing vessel with gear out crosses from your port side: you are power-driven, so Rule 18 makes you keep out
   of her way even though she is on your port side.
4. You enter the fog. A radar contact appears at 030 relative, closing: Rule 19 now applies, and a port turn is out
   (Drill 4). But the tug from step 1 is now a radar contact at 140 relative, on your starboard quarter, so a
   starboard turn would be towards her. This is the two-sided case: a substantial speed reduction may be the best
   action, with careful plotting of both contacts.

## Common mistakes

- Choosing an action before deciding which rule applies.
- Calling a vessel 10 degrees abaft your beam "overtaking". The line is 22.5 degrees abaft the beam.
- Using Section II roles for radar contacts.
- Holding on as stand-on vessel all the way into collision.
- Turning to port for a radar contact on the bow because it "opens the range faster".

## Check yourself

1. You steer 010 degrees true. A vessel bears 120 degrees true and is closing. Overtaking or crossing?
2. What lights tell you that you are head-on with a power-driven vessel at night?
3. In what order does the stand-on vessel's duty change as the range closes?
4. A radar contact in fog is at 135 relative and closing. Which way do you avoid turning?
5. Why is speed reduction often the best answer when contacts are on both sides?
