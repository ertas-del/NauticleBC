# Rule 7: Risk of collision

## What the rule asks of you

Rule 7 is about **deciding whether there is a risk of collision**. It asks you to:

- use ==all available means== suited to the conditions to decide whether risk exists. Rule 5 builds the lookout; this rule is about what you do with the picture;
- if there is ==any doubt==, treat the risk as ==existing==;
- use **radar properly** if it is fitted and working. That includes ==long-range scanning== to get early warning, and ==radar plotting or an equivalent systematic observation== of the objects you detect;
- **not make assumptions** from ==scanty information==, especially scanty radar information;
- watch the ==compass bearing== of an approaching vessel. If it ==does not appreciably change==, risk is to be deemed to exist.

The rule also notes that risk **may sometimes exist even when the bearing does change appreciably**, in particular when you approach a ==very large vessel==, a ==tow==, or ==any vessel at close range==.

## Plain-language explanation

Collision happens when two vessels reach the same point at the same time. If the other vessel is on a **steady bearing** from you while the **distance is closing**, she is staying in the same direction from you as she gets nearer, which means you are heading for the same spot. That is the classic warning. Bearing is useful because you can check it with a compass bearing from the bridge wing, a pelorus or a repeater, or from radar.

Why can risk exist even when the bearing is changing? A very big ship or a tow stretches over a long distance, and the part you pass close to may be far from the point you are tracking. At close range, a small change in bearing can still leave very little room between you. In those cases a bearing swing is not a reason to relax.

The "any doubt" sentence is a built-in safety bias. If you cannot tell, you act as if there is a risk and use Rule 8 (action to avoid collision) and the other Rules that follow. "Scanty information" means a single radar sweep, a quick glance, or a target that fades in and out. Keep observing before concluding that someone will pass clear, and do not rely on a hunch.

Radar is a tool for early warning and for **systematic observation**. A single look does not tell you much. Repeated observations over time show the other vessel's bearing, range and movement.

## On the bridge

At night you notice the red and white lights of a vessel about two points on your starboard bow. You take a bearing, wait a few minutes, and take another. The bearing is almost the same, and the range on radar is getting smaller. Risk of collision exists. You do not wait to be sure; you move to the action Rules. Another time you are closing a long tow at close range and the bearing to the tug is slowly changing. You remember that a tow can be very long, so you do not relax because of the drift in bearing. You keep watching, allow plenty of room, and treat the risk as real until you are clearly past.

## Common mistakes

- Waiting until you are "sure" there is a collision course before acting.
- Using one radar sweep, or a target that appears and vanishes, as the basis for a decision.
- Thinking a changing bearing always means safe passing, even close to a tow or large vessel.
- Leaving radar on a short range scale only and missing early warning.
- Checking bearing by eye only, without a compass bearing or systematic observation.
