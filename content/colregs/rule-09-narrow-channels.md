# Rule 9: Narrow channels

Rule 9 covers narrow channels and fairways. The Regulations do not define "narrow", so it is a judgement. The
tighter the water is for the vessel in question, the more Rule 9 matters. The rule applies in any condition of
visibility.

## What the rule asks of you

**Keep to the starboard side.** A vessel going along a narrow channel or fairway keeps as near to the ==outer limit==
on ==her starboard side== as is ==safe and practicable==. Those last words matter. A stiff crosswind, a bend, a
set from the current or a buoy in the way may all push you off the edge. The rule does not ask you to hug the
edge at any cost.

**Do not impede a vessel that can only use the channel.** Some vessels can safely navigate only inside the
channel, for example because of draught. Two kinds of vessel must not get in their way:

- a vessel ==under 20 metres== in length,
- a ==sailing vessel==.

**Fishing vessels.** A vessel engaged in fishing has a wider duty. She
==must not impede the passage of any other vessel== navigating within the channel or fairway, not only vessels
that are limited to it.

**Do not cross if it impedes.** A vessel ==must not cross== a narrow channel if the crossing would impede a vessel
that can safely navigate only within the channel. If that vessel is in doubt about what the crossing vessel is
doing, it may use the ==doubt/danger signal== from Rule 34.

**Overtaking.** Where overtaking can only happen if the vessel ahead has to help, the overtaking vessel signals
what it intends, using the Rule 34 overtaking signals. The vessel ahead, if it agrees, answers with the agreement
signal and makes room. If it is in doubt, it may sound the doubt/danger signal. Agreement does not make the
overtaking vessel any less an overtaking vessel. The ==Rule 13 duty to keep clear remains==. (Signal details belong
to the Rule 34 lesson.)

**Bends and blind areas.** A vessel nearing a bend, or any part of the channel where other vessels may be hidden
by an obstruction, navigates with ==particular alertness and caution== and sounds the Rule 34 ==bend signal==.

**Anchoring.** If the circumstances allow, ==avoid anchoring in a narrow channel==.

## Canadian additions

Rule 9 in the Canadian Regulations has extra provisions beyond the international text. Two matter on the BC coast.

1. **Barges and inconspicuous objects.** In the Canadian waters of a narrow channel or fairway, a ==barge==, or an
   ==inconspicuous, partly submerged vessel or object==, must not be navigated, moored or anchored so as to impede the
   safe passage of other vessels or objects using those waters.
2. **Current or tidal stream.** In the Canadian waters of a narrow channel or fairway, if there is a current or
   tidal stream and two power-driven vessels are meeting from opposite directions so that risk of collision
   exists, the usual head-on arrangement is changed:
   - The vessel going ==with the current or tidal stream is the stand-on== vessel. It
     ==proposes the place of passage== and signals which side it will pass, using the appropriate Rule 34 signal.
   - The vessel going ==against== the current or tidal stream ==keeps out of the way== and holds as necessary so that
     the passing can be made safely.
   - The vessel going against the stream replies promptly with the same signal if it agrees, or with the
     doubt/danger signal if it does not.

   A commonly given reason is that the vessel running with the stream is harder to slow down over the ground, while
   the vessel stemming it can hold more easily. This is a teaching explanation, not wording from the rule.

Other Canadian paragraphs of Rule 9 apply only to the waters of the Great Lakes Basin, so they are not relevant
on the BC coast.

## A bridge-watch example

You are on watch in a 14 metre power vessel approaching a dredged channel into a commercial harbour. Ahead, you
can see a deep-draught vessel entering the channel. You were planning to cross the channel on a short leg to a
marina on the other side.

- You are under 20 metres, so you must not impede that vessel if it can safely navigate only inside the channel.
- Crossing now would force the larger vessel to alter or slow. Wait, let her pass, then cross when you will not
  impede anyone.
- If the channel has a blind bend ahead, expect traffic you cannot see, and make the bend signal.

## Common mistakes

- Using "keep to starboard" as a fixed rule and ignoring "safe and practicable".
- A small vessel assuming it has right of way because it is "in the channel first".
- Crossing a channel at the last moment because the gap "looks big enough".
- Treating the overtaking agreement signal as if the overtaken vessel must now do all the work.
- Leaving a barge or a tow anchored or moored where it narrows a channel.
- Forgetting the Canadian tidal-stream provision. In Canadian waters it can reverse the usual head-on practice
  for two power-driven vessels.
