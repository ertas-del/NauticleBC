# Rule 5: Keeping a proper lookout

## What the rule asks of you

Rule 5 is one sentence, and every later Rule depends on it. Every vessel must keep a ==proper lookout==
==at all times==. The lookout is kept:

- by ==sight==,
- by ==hearing==,
- and by ==all available means appropriate== to the circumstances and conditions at the time.

The point of it all is to make a ==full appraisal of the situation and of the risk of collision==.

Rule 5 sits in Section I of the Steering and Sailing Rules, so it applies ==in any condition of visibility==: clear
day, dark night, fog or rain (see the Rule 4 lesson).

## Plain-language explanation

**"At all times"** means exactly that. There is no quiet-water exemption, no daylight exemption and no exemption for
small vessels. Clear daylight in open water is when people tend to stop looking, and it is still a time when the rule
applies in full.

**"Sight and hearing"** are both named. Hearing matters most when you cannot see far: in fog you may hear a fog
signal, an engine or a voice before anything shows on radar or by eye. A closed wheelhouse with loud engines, music or
a noisy heater can quietly remove hearing from your lookout.

**"All available means"** is everything aboard that adds to the picture and suits the conditions:

- radar, used on suitable range scales and watched systematically,
- AIS and electronic charts,
- VHF radio, for traffic calls and broadcasts,
- sound signals from other vessels,
- other crew who can look or listen from a different position.

These are aids to the lookout, not a replacement for it. The Rule 6 lesson explains that radar may not pick up
==small vessels, ice and other floating objects== at a useful range, and that sea state and rain can hide targets. AIS only
shows vessels that are transmitting. A blank screen is not proof of clear water.

**"Full appraisal"** is the reason for the rule. The lookout is the input to Rule 7 (is there a risk of collision?) and
Rule 8 (what do I do about it?). If you have not detected a vessel, you cannot judge the risk or plan the avoiding
action.

## On the bridge

You are on night watch crossing the Strait of Georgia in a 15 metre workboat. Visibility is good, AIS shows two ferries
well clear, and the radar is on a long range scale. It would be easy to sit back and watch the screens.

A proper lookout here means:

- regularly looking all round with your eyes, including astern and on the quarters,
- keeping a radar scale that would show a small boat close in, and switching ranges from time to time,
- keeping the VHF on and the wheelhouse quiet enough to hear,
- remembering that kayakers, small fishing boats and drifting logs may show no AIS and only a weak radar echo, or none.

Partway through the watch you see a dim white light fine on the starboard bow that is not on AIS and only shows on radar
when you turn the range down. Your eyes found it first; the instruments then helped you track it. That is the
lookout working as the rule intends.

## Common mistakes

- Treating a plotter or radar screen as the lookout.
- Leaving the radar on a long range scale only, so close small targets are lost in the picture.
- Assuming that every vessel nearby shows up on AIS.
- Shutting the wheelhouse up so tightly, or running it so noisily, that nobody could hear a sound signal.
- Letting the person at the helm be the only lookout while also navigating, plotting and talking on the radio.
- Relaxing the watch because "nobody is around" or "it is broad daylight".
