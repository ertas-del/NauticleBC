# How the oral works and what it covers

*Study aid, not a navigation aid. This lesson is based on what training providers report; Transport Canada's own oral syllabus has not been checked. The sample prompts are original and are not Transport Canada questions.*

## What you need to know

The oral is the last step. By the time you sit it you have the sea service, the certificates and the written exams. The examiner is now asking: **would I be comfortable with this person in charge of a vessel?**

Reported facts about how it works:

- ==In person== with a Transport Canada examiner, ==by appointment==.
- ==Taken last==, after the other requirements are met.
- It ==may be held on board== the vessel, with you ==manoeuvring and running drills==.
- The examiner ==may add restrictions== to your certificate based on your experience and your answers (for example a narrower area).
- There is no published pass percentage. It rests on ==the examiner's judgement==.

The same kind of exam, a ==practical and oral on general seamanship== for the vessel, is also one of the ways to renew the certificate (with a refresher MED course).

## What the oral covers

Providers say the oral can cover **anything** from the related courses and anything else the examiner thinks the master of that vessel must know. The topics that come up most in provider descriptions are:

| Area | Examples |
|---|---|
| **Emergencies** | ==Fire, flooding, man overboard, abandonment, collision, grounding== |
| **Your vessel** | Systems (electrical, plumbing, propulsion), equipment, emergency gear |
| **Shiphandling** | Berthing, anchoring, handling in wind and current |
| **Communications** | On board, ship to ship, distress calls |
| **Pollution prevention** | What you must not discharge, what you must report |
| **Passenger and cargo safety** | Briefings, lifejackets, loading, securing |
| **Rules and law** | Canada Shipping Act duties, collision regulations, navigation regulations |
| **Other topics** | Stability awareness, weather and passes, radar and electronic aids, tides and currents |

The rest of the app builds the knowledge these answers draw on. This lesson is about **how to answer out loud**.

## How to build a spoken answer

For emergencies and decisions, a simple order helps you stay calm and complete. This is a study technique, not a rule from a regulation:

1. **Safety of people first.** Say who you will protect and how (lifejackets, headcount, muster).
2. **Stop the problem getting worse.** Reduce speed, shut fuel, close doors, turn to keep the casualty in sight, whatever fits.
3. **Call for help.** Say what you would send, on which channel, and what information you would give.
4. **Keep control.** Position, crew roles, who is doing what.
5. **Afterwards.** Log it, report it, check the vessel before carrying on.

Then add **your own vessel**: the real name of the extinguisher, the actual place the lifejackets are kept. Examiners notice when answers are about the boat in front of them and not a textbook ship.

Habits that help:

- Answer the question that was asked, then stop. If more is wanted the examiner will ask.
- Say "I would check the regulation or the manufacturer's manual" when you do not know a figure; do not invent one.
- Practise out loud. Written knowledge and spoken answers are different skills.

## Sample oral prompts

These prompts are original practice material. Rehearse each aloud for about two minutes, then check what you said against the matching lessons. Each prompt lists what a good answer touches. The points are prompts for your memory, not model answers or regulatory numbers.

**1. Fire in the engine space.** You are the master of a small passenger vessel, 3 nm from the dock, and the engine room alarm sounds. What do you do?
*A good answer touches:* the people on board and where they go; stopping the fire from feeding (fuel, ventilation, power); which extinguisher or fixed system and why; keeping the vessel manoeuvrable or anchored; the call for help and what it says; what you check afterwards.

**2. Man overboard.** A passenger goes over the side from the foredeck. The sea is choppy and the person is wearing a lifejacket. Describe what you do from the moment you hear the shout.
*A good answer touches:* alert and keep a lookout on the person; mark the position; manoeuvre back; the recovery method for your vessel; hypothermia and first aid afterwards; calling for help if needed.

**3. Grounding on a falling tide.** You touch bottom in a narrow passage two hours after high water. What are your first actions and your first questions?
*A good answer touches:* checking people and damage; soundings around the vessel; the state of the tide and what it will do; whether to try to back off or stay; stability and flooding checks; calling for help.

**4. Flooding.** An unexplained bilge alarm sounds in the aft compartment of your workboat. What do you do?
*A good answer touches:* finding the source; pumps and their limits; closing what can be closed; effect on trim and stability; preparing to evacuate or beach; what you tell the crew and passengers.

**5. Collision.** Another vessel has struck your vessel on the starboard side near the bow. Nobody is hurt. What next?
*A good answer touches:* people, damage, and watertight integrity; exchanging details with the other vessel; reporting; whether to continue; the log.

**6. A passenger briefing.** You are about to leave the dock with 10 passengers. What do you tell them and why?
*A good answer touches:* where lifejackets are and how they are worn; where to stand and sit; what to do in an emergency; the "no" list (smoking, moving about, leaning).

**7. Poor visibility.** Fog closes in as you leave a harbour. What changes on your bridge?
*A good answer touches:* a safe speed; the sound signals you must make; extra lookouts; radar and VHF use; whether to carry on, anchor or turn back; the collision rules that apply when vessels are not in sight of each other.

**8. Your vessel's systems.** Describe how the electrical system on your vessel is arranged and what you do if the main supply is lost.
*A good answer touches:* the real layout of your batteries and switches; navigation lights and VHF; what is on emergency power; how you check these before sailing.

**9. A decision under pressure.** The forecast has worsened and the sea in the crossing is steepening. You have passengers who want to go. What is your decision process?
*A good answer touches:* the forecast and warnings you would check; the vessel's limits and the certificate's limits; the safest alternative; the master's authority and duty; what you tell the passengers.

**10. Pollution.** You see a sheen spreading from your vessel while fuelling. What do you do and who do you tell?
*A good answer touches:* stopping the transfer; containing the spill with what is on board; reporting; recording what happened.

## A BC example

An examiner on a Nanaimo dock asks a candidate with a 10 GT workboat: "You are in Dodd Narrows near slack water and the engine stops. What do you do?" A strong answer starts with the crew and the vessel's drift, goes to anchoring or raising help on VHF, mentions the tide and current at the narrows, and says what the candidate would check on the engine afterwards. It uses the real vessel: where the anchor is and how long it takes to deploy. A weak answer lists engine faults and never mentions the current.

## Exam traps

- Reciting a textbook list when the examiner asked about *your* vessel.
- Inventing a figure you do not know. "I would check" is better than a wrong number.
- Going straight to the fault and forgetting the people.
- Skipping the call for help, or not saying what you would say in it.
- Forgetting that the oral may be on board: knowing where the gear is matters as much as knowing what it is.

## Check yourself

2. Name four emergencies the oral is reported to cover.
3. Why might the examiner add restrictions to your certificate?
4. Which part of a spoken answer should come first in an emergency?
5. Practise sample prompt 3 aloud, then look up the tide and grounding material in the app.
