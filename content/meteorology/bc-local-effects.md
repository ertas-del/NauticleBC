# Local winds, fog and seas on the BC coast

**Study aid only.** Local effects can beat the regional forecast. Treat this as a guide to what to ask, not what you will find.

## What you need to know

The forecast gives a regional figure. The BC coast is mountains and narrow water, and three local things can make the real conditions different from the forecast: **wind funnelled through inlets**, **fog**, and **sea state made worse by swell or by tide**.

## Inflow and outflow winds

An ==inflow== is air coming from the water into an inlet, fjord or valley. An ==outflow== is air coming out of the mountains down the inlet toward the water.

| Type | When | Strength |
|---|---|---|
| Summer inflow | Daytime heating draws cool marine air in (sea breeze and upslope wind) | Usually not strong |
| Summer outflow | At night cold air drains down valleys and combines with land breeze | ==Can be strong and hazardous== |
| Winter outflow | Cold interior air makes high pressure east of the Coast Range and low pressure over the coast | ==Very strong== |

Examples:

- ==Howe Sound==: the Squamish wind blows as an outflow overnight and early morning, then switches to an afternoon inflow around noon. It is strongest at the head of the sound.
- ==Qualicum wind==: a southwesterly inflow up Port Alberni Inlet crosses the island through a gap and comes out as a strong outflow into the Strait of Georgia, with ==16 to 20 knots== against ==2 to 8 knots== elsewhere in the strait. Tugs towing log booms have had booms torn apart.
- Winter outflows affect Howe Sound, the Strait of Georgia, the lower Fraser Valley, the Gulf Islands and the central coast, where gale warnings with winds to 45 knots occur.

Outflows ==blow hardest at the inlet mouth== and can leave nearby water calm, so a vessel that rounds a headland out of a calm bay can meet sudden strong wind. Northwesterly or northerly regional wind strengthens outflows; light southerly wind strengthens afternoon inflows. Environment Canada wind warnings say whether the wind is ==outflow or inflow==.

## Sea breeze

A ==sea breeze== is a shallow, cool daytime wind from sea to land. It needs sun-heated land about ==5 C or more warmer== than the water, usually under ==high pressure== with weak regional wind. It builds as the land warms, is strongest from ==midday to mid-afternoon==, and often dies in the late evening. Typical speed at the coast is about 22 km/h (roughly 12 knots). The wind direction turns clockwise through the day.

## Fog

Fog forms when air cools past its dew point.

| Type | How |
|---|---|
| ==Advection (sea) fog== | Warm humid air moves over cooler water. The main sea fog and a real hazard. Any time, any season; upwelling of cold water makes more |
| Radiation fog | Over land at night, calm; can drain from valleys onto the water; usually clears in the morning |
| Frontal fog | Where warm air rises over colder air, rain evaporates into the cold air and saturates it |
| Sea smoke (steam fog) | Cold air over warm water; wispy, short-lived, not a navigation hazard |

Sea fog is nearly constant on summer mornings in some coastal regions, so August is nicknamed "Fogust". Fog clears when the day warms the air or when ==stronger wind mixes it with drier air==. It can appear suddenly. Sailing Directions PAC 200 warns of frequent heavy swell and dense fog in Fitz Hugh Sound. In fog, Rule 19 applies (see the colregs module) and the forecast's fixed line means visibility under 1 mile.

## Swell and wind waves

==Swell== is a train of waves made by ==distant storms== that travels long distances. A swell with a ==longer period== has more energy and can break bigger: a 6 ft swell at 15 seconds can break as a 10 ft wave, whereas at 7 seconds it breaks near 6 ft. Local wind on top of swell makes a ==confused sea==. Direction is where the swell comes from. A vessel running parallel to the swell rolls heavily. PAC 200 notes strong gales are frequent in Queen Charlotte Sound and Hecate Strait, and heavy seas can break over Nahwitti Bar in a west gale.

## Wind against current

When wind and current run the same way, the sea is calmer. When they oppose, the moving water ==shortens and steepens== the wind waves into ==choppy, confused seas that feel worse than the wind alone suggests==. Trouble spots: headlands, bars, harbour entrances, narrow channels. A ==turning tide== can build waves with no change in wind, so they seem to arrive from nowhere. Fast currents in narrows form standing waves, eddies and whirlpools. Some passes that are impassable at the peak of the flow can be navigable at ==slack water==. The Sechelt (Skookumchuk) Rapids can run to ==17.6 knots==. Current tables give the slack times; see the tides module.

## A BC example (invented numbers)

A tug master plans to enter Howe Sound at 0500 in August. The regional forecast is "wind light." He knows that summer outflow in Howe Sound runs overnight and in the early morning, strongest at the head. He expects stronger wind toward Squamish and a switch to inflow near noon. He delays the exposed leg or plans to be at the head after the outflow weakens, and checks the current tables for slack in the pass he must cross later.

## Common mistakes

- Trusting a "light" regional forecast in an inlet with a summer or winter outflow.
- Thinking calm water at the mouth means no wind in the inlet.
- Calling every fog "radiation fog". At sea the key one is advection fog.
- Forgetting the swell period; two swells of the same height behave very differently.
- Entering a pass at peak flow with the wind against the current.
