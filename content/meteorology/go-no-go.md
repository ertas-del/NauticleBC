# Go or no-go decisions

**Study aid only. Every number in the scenarios is invented.** Real limits come from your vessel's documents and your own judgement, and the master decides.

## What you need to know

No regulation lists a wind speed at which you must stay in. What the law says is narrower: the vessel must be fit for its area of operation, and you must not operate it **beyond its design limitations**. Everything else is judgement, and the oral exam tests whether you can weigh the facts in an organised way.

## The law in short

- **Small Vessel Regulations (SVR) s. 404** (passenger-carrying vessels of not more than 15 GT carrying not more than 12 passengers): the vessel ==shall be designed and equipped to operate safely in its area of operation==, and ==no person shall operate it under circumstances that exceed its design limitations, if any==.
- **SVR s. 501** says the same for ==workboats==.
- **SVR s. 717(4)**: the builder or importer must give the end user a document setting out the vessel's ==design limitations==, including capacity, power and ==environmental limitations==.
- **Canada Shipping Act, 2001 s. 112**: the master must warn nearby vessels and the authorities ashore on meeting ==force 10 or more with no storm warning==, or subfreezing air with gale-force wind and severe icing. See the wind lesson.

So a go/no-go starts with a question about the boat: what limits does the document give, and is the forecast inside them? If no limit is written, the master still has to decide whether the vessel can operate safely in that sea.

## A checklist

Work down the list in order. Stop when an item says no.

1. ==Warnings==: what category is in effect for every area on the route and when? A gale for part of the day is a different problem from a gale all day.
2. ==Forecast winds== along the route, by time. Look for the shift ("becoming") and the strongest period.
3. ==Fetch and sea==: long open fetch plus several hours of wind builds seas. Check the wave forecast and swell.
4. ==Tide and current==: is the wind against the current in a pass? When is slack? Can you be there at slack with the wind you expect?
5. ==Local effects==: outflows from inlets, afternoon inflows, fog (visibility under 1 mile).
6. ==Vessel limits==: design limitations, stability, fuel, crew and passengers. Icing risk if cold.
7. ==Shelter==: where can you go if it worsens, and how long to get there?
8. ==Time and light==: do you finish in daylight, and when is the next forecast?
9. ==Communications==: can you hear the continuous marine broadcast and call the Coast Guard?
10. ==Change trigger==: what will you see (wind, pressure, sea) that makes you turn back, and where?

## Scenario 1: the exposed crossing

A 9 m passenger boat (invented design limit: seas to 1.5 m) is to cross the Strait of Georgia with six passengers. At 0430 the forecast says northwest 10 to 15 knots rising to 25 to 30 in the afternoon, seas 1 to 1.5 m rising to 2 m. No warning yet.

- Morning: force 3 to 4, within the limit.
- Afternoon: 25 to 30 is force 6 and seas 2 m exceed the 1.5 m limit.
- The risk is in the second half of the day, and the boat would be on the water when the sea builds.

Decision: ==go early and finish before noon==, or ==do not go==. A late departure fails the limit. Re-check at 1030 and have a place to shelter. Operating above the design limit would not just be risky; it is exactly what SVR s. 404 forbids.

## Scenario 2: the pass

A tug with a log tow must pass a narrows with a peak current of 8 knots (invented). Wind is forecast northwest 20 knots all day. The current table gives slack water at 1130 and 1730 (invented).

- When wind opposes the current the seas steepen, worse than the wind alone suggests, and a turning tide can build waves with no change in wind.
- The pass is only comfortable near slack.

Decision: arrive for the ==1130 slack== with the current table open, and be ready to hold off if you are late. If the wind comes up stronger than forecast, delay to the next slack or anchor.

## Scenario 3: the fog and the outflow

In early August the forecast is "wind light" for the area, "fog implies visibility less than 1 mile". The route leaves a calm bay and passes the mouth of an inlet. In a summer night and early morning, outflow from the inlet can be strong at the mouth.

Decision: go with radar, sound signals and a slow speed, **or** wait for the fog to clear. Plan for a wind at the inlet mouth that the regional forecast will not show.

## When to re-check

The forecast is reissued at ==04:00, 10:30, 16:00 and 21:30==. Check the latest before departure and again before any exposed leg. If the sea or wind is worse than forecast, believe the sea. Forecasts also carry less skill the further ahead they look, so do not plan a risky leg on days 3 to 5.

## Common mistakes

- Treating "no warning" as "safe". The vessel's own limits still apply.
- Going because the morning looks fine when the afternoon forecast is worse.
- Ignoring wind against current in a pass.
- Not having a fallback harbour.
- Looking at one source only.
- Treating a design limit as a target.
